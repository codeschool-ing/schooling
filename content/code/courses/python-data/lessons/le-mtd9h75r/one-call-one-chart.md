**seaborn takes a DataFrame and the names of its columns, and draws a statistical chart in one call.**
It is built on matplotlib: every chart it draws is matplotlib underneath, on an `Axes` you can keep
adjusting with everything lesson 18 taught. What it adds is the statistics, the grouping and the
colours that matplotlib leaves to you.

The notebook reads the trips with their lengths:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

fmt = "%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S"
trips = pd.read_csv("trips.csv")
trips["started_at"] = pd.to_datetime(trips["started_at"], format=fmt)
trips["ended_at"] = pd.to_datetime(trips["ended_at"], format=fmt)
trips["minutes"] = (trips["ended_at"] - trips["started_at"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
```

The distribution of trip lengths, one histogram per plan, on one axes:

```python
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.5), layout="constrained")
sns.histplot(data=trips, x="minutes", hue="plan", binwidth=2, ax=ax)
ax.set_xlim(0, 80)
ax.set_title("Day passes ride longer, and their lengths spread wider")
type(ax).__name__, len(ax.patches)
```

`data=` is the table and `x=` names a column of it. **`hue=` names another column whose values split
the data by colour**, with a legend made for you; the same chart in matplotlib alone needs a loop over
the plans, a call per plan and a legend built by hand. `binwidth=2` makes each bar two minutes wide,
and `ax=ax` tells seaborn to draw on the axes you made, so `layout`, `set_xlim` and `set_title` work as
they did in lesson 18. The bars it drew are rectangles on that axes, `ax.patches`, two sets of bars
for two plans.

## The table seaborn wants

`hue="plan"` works because `plan` is a column and every trip is a row: the **long** shape of lesson
15. A wide table, one column per plan, would not have a column to name. Most of the time, getting a
chart out of seaborn is getting the table into the long shape first, with `melt` if it arrived wide.

The trips are the right shape already, and the numbers under the chart say what it shows:

```python
trips.groupby("plan")["minutes"].median().round(1)
```


The annual subscribers' trips cluster around 12 minutes; the day passes' around 20, with a longer
tail to the right. The `xlim` cut the tail at 80 minutes for legibility, and the next section looks
at what lies beyond it.

**The first chart a library draws answers "what is in this column"; a chart worth showing anybody
answers one question, and says which.** The difference is a handful of calls: what is in front, what
is behind, what each axis means, and a title that states the point.

Daily trips are noisy, and lesson 16's seven-day mean shows the trend through the noise. Both on one
axes, the noise light and behind, the trend dark and in front:

```python
week = daily.rolling(7).mean()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.5))
ax.plot(daily.index, daily.to_numpy(), color="0.7", linewidth=0.8, label="each day")
ax.plot(week.index, week.to_numpy(), linewidth=2, label="7-day mean")
ax.set_title("Trips per day in 2025: no trend, a weekly rhythm")
ax.set_ylabel("trips")
ax.legend(loc="lower left");
```

Each call does one job:

| call | does |
|---|---|
| `color="0.7"` | a grey; a string holding a number from 0 to 1 is a shade from black to white |
| `linewidth=0.8` and `2` | the thin line is context, the thick line is the point |
| `label=` and `ax.legend()` | the legend reads each line's `label`; without them it has nothing to say |
| `ax.set_title(...)` | the title states the finding, not only the subject |
| `ax.set_ylabel("trips")` | the unit of the axis; dates on the other axis explain themselves |

The `;` at the end of the last line is a notebook habit. `ax.legend()` returns the legend object, and
without the semicolon the notebook would print its description, `<matplotlib.legend.Legend at
0x…>`, above the chart. The semicolon tells IPython to show nothing for the last line.

## The title is a sentence

"Trips per day, 2025" names the data. "No trend, a weekly rhythm" says what the chart shows, and a
reader can check it against the line: the seven-day mean stays between about 70 and 106 all year,
with no slope, and the grey line underneath swings every week between the weekday highs and the
weekend lows of lesson 15. If the title and the line disagree, the reader believes the line, and
should.

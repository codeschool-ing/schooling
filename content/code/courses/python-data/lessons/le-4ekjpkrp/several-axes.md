**`plt.subplots(rows, columns)` makes a grid of axes in one figure, and `sharey=True` gives them one
vertical scale, so bars in different charts can be compared by eye.** Two charts side by side on
different scales invite exactly the comparison they cannot support.

Trips by hour of the day, on weekdays and at weekends. There are 261 weekdays and 104 weekend days in
2025, so the counts are divided by the number of days of each kind, giving trips per day in each hour:

```python
trips["hour"] = trips["started_at"].dt.hour
trips["weekend"] = trips["started_at"].dt.dayofweek >= 5
days = pd.Series(daily.index.dayofweek >= 5).value_counts()
per_hour = trips.groupby(["weekend", "hour"]).size().unstack("weekend") / days
per_hour.round(1).loc[[7, 12, 17]]
```

```
weekend  False  True 
hour                 
7         11.7    3.2
12         5.0    4.6
17        10.8    3.6
```

`unstack` put the two kinds of day in the columns, `False` and `True`, and dividing by `days` divided
each column by its own count, because the division aligns on labels, as lesson 9 showed. At 7 in the
morning a weekday has more than three times the trips of a weekend day; at noon they are level.

Two axes in one row, sharing their vertical axis:

```python
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.5), sharey=True, layout="constrained")
for ax, weekend, title in zip(axes, [False, True], ["Monday to Friday", "Saturday and Sunday"]):
    ax.bar(per_hour.index, per_hour[weekend])
    ax.set_title(title)
    ax.set_xlabel("hour")
axes[0].set_ylabel("trips per day")
fig.suptitle("Weekdays have two rush hours; weekends have none");
```

`axes` is a NumPy array of two `Axes`, so a loop sets up both the same way and only what differs is
written twice. `fig.suptitle` titles the whole figure, above the two titles of the axes.

**`sharey=True` is the line that makes the comparison honest.** Without it each axes scales to its own
data: the weekend chart, whose highest bar is under 5, would stretch to fill the same height as the
weekday chart, whose highest is near 12, and the two would look alike. With it, the weekend bars are
visibly shorter, which is the fact the chart exists to show. Charts drawn this way, the same chart
repeated for each group on one scale, are often called **small multiples**, and they are usually
clearer than one crowded chart with every group in it.

A rota works if the people on it are still willing to be on it a year from now. That is the test, and it is failed quietly: nobody announces that they are leaving because of the pager, they just stop volunteering, start swapping weeks away, and eventually take a job that has none.

## What to count

Three numbers say most of what matters, and the program in section 03 already prints them:

- **pages per shift.** Google's SRE book puts the ceiling at **two incidents per twelve-hour shift**: enough time to handle each one properly, write it up and still sleep. The Billing team's 82 pages in 13 weeks is about six a week, far under that ceiling.
- **pages at night**, between 22:00 and 07:00. A night page costs the next day as well, and 25 in a quarter is about two a week.
- **pages per person, counting the ones passed on.** This is the number that the rota hides and the one that went wrong: Rafa took 54 and Caio 6.

**The Billing team's problem is not the amount of paging; it is where the paging lands.** The team-wide totals look healthy, and averaging them would have reported a healthy rota while one person was absorbing half of it. A load is measured per person, or it is not measured.

## What the Billing team changed

Bia took the quarter's numbers to the team in October, and they agreed four changes:

- **Bia joins the rota.** Six people make it one week in six, about 17% each, and one holiday no longer pushes anybody to one week in four.
- **Card-provider pages go to the person on call, with Rafa on a call beside them** rather than taking over, and every page Rafa is asked to help with becomes a line in the runbook he writes that week. The aim is a number that falls: passed-on pages per month, reported beside the rest.
- **Month-end has its own secondary.** The last two days of each month are covered by a second person chosen in turn, so month-end is shared and nobody's luck in the calendar decides who gets it.
- **A page between 22:00 and 07:00 means a late start the next day**, without asking, as section 05 recommends.

None of these touched the alerts. The handover notes say that many of the quarter's pages needed nobody; finding which ones, and removing them, is cheaper than any of the four changes above, and it is lesson 18.

## Looking at it every month

On-call load drifts. A new service adds alerts, a provider gets worse, the month-end moves through the rota. **A team that counts its pages per person once a month**, in ten minutes with a program like `oncall.py` reading the paging tool's export, sees the drift while it is a number rather than a resignation letter. It belongs in the same review as the error budget of lesson 16 and the action items of lesson 15, because all three are the same question: is the way this team runs its service something the team can keep doing?

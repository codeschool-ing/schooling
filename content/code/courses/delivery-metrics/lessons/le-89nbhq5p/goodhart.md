title: When a measure becomes a target

In 1975 the economist Charles Goodhart, writing about monetary policy, observed that a statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later put it in the form everybody quotes: **"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."** The social scientist Donald Campbell had reached the same conclusion about social indicators: the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the processes it was meant to monitor.

Both are usually quoted as warnings about dishonesty. They are not. **They describe what honest people do with a number they are rewarded for.** A measure is useful because it moves with something you care about. Make the measure the goal, and people look for the cheapest way to move it, and the cheapest way is rarely the thing you cared about. Nobody has to lie. Each step is defensible on its own.

## Why delivery metrics are especially exposed

Every delivery metric in this course rests on choices: what counts as a deployment, which commit starts lead time, what counts as a failure, when an item is "started". Lesson 5 listed them and said to write them down before looking. Those choices are where gaming lives. **A metric whose definition can move will move towards the reading that looks better** as soon as anybody's standing depends on it, and the people moving it will believe, correctly, that each change was reasonable.

## The opposite mistake

The answer is not to stop measuring. A team without numbers argues from opinion, which was this course's starting point, and the loudest opinion wins. The answer is the one lesson 6 began: **keep the numbers as instruments of the team that produces them, not as targets set by somebody else**. A team that uses its own cycle time to find its own bottleneck has no reason to game it; gaming would only blind itself. A team that is ranked by its cycle time has every reason. Lesson 20 turns that distinction into rules.

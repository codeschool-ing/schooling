Lesson 10 produced sentences like **"85% likely by the end of October, about even odds by the 27th"**. Those sentences are unfamiliar to most people who ask for dates, and the first job of a tech lead with a forecast is to make them land without being heard as either a promise or a dodge.

## Two different things called a date

| | a forecast | a commitment |
|---|---|---|
| what it says | what is likely, given what we know | what we will do, whatever happens |
| its shape | a range with probabilities | a single date |
| who owns the risk | stated openly, shared | the team, silently |
| what changes it | new information, every week | a renegotiation |

Most arguments about dates are a forecast heard as a commitment. The team says "probably the end of October"; the listener writes "31 October" in a plan; the team misses it by a week, inside the range it gave, and is blamed for breaking a promise it never made. **The fix is in the wording, every time**: say the probability, say which date it attaches to, and say when the forecast will next be updated.

## A vocabulary that works

These sentences turn lesson 10's output into something a stakeholder can plan around:

- **"85% likely by 30 October."** The planning date. Stakeholders who must not be let down plan on this one.
- **"About even odds by 27 October."** The median. Useful to say, because it shows the 85% date is not padded: there is a real chance of finishing earlier.
- **"Almost certainly by the first week of November."** The 95th percentile. The date to give anyone booking something expensive that depends on it.
- **"We will update this every Monday."** The sentence that turns a forecast from a promise into a process.

## Why a range is not an excuse

The usual objection is that a range is a way of avoiding accountability. It is the opposite. A single date hides the uncertainty inside the team, where nobody else can see it or help with it, and when the date slips the uncertainty arrives all at once. **A range puts the uncertainty where the people who carry its consequences can see it**, early enough to decide what to do: move a launch, cut scope, add a buffer, accept the risk. That is accountability in a form other people can use.

A commitment still has its place. When a contract or a regulator needs one date, the team chooses the date from the forecast, usually the 85th or 95th percentile, and says which. What it must not do is pick a date from hope and present it as a forecast.

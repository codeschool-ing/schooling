Here is a conversation Bia, the Billing team's tech lead, has in early October with Marta, the director of the sales team, about the new invoicing screen. It is written for the course, and its moves are the ones that matter.

> **Marta:** The big accounting fair is on 6 November. I need to tell the shops the new invoicing screen is ready. Can we say it will be?

> **Bia:** Let me tell you what the history says, and then we decide together. There are thirty items left. If nothing grows, we are about 85% likely to be done by the end of October. But this kind of work always grows, and allowing for that, our 85% date is mid-November.

> **Marta:** So the answer is no.

> **Bia:** The answer is that 6 November is roughly a coin toss for the whole screen. We have three better options than tossing it.

## The three levers

Every date conversation comes down to three things, and **only two of them can be fixed at once**: the scope, the date and the confidence.

- **Fix the date and the confidence; let the scope move.** "By 6 November, with 85% confidence, we will have *this much*." Lesson 10's `howmany.py` answers exactly that, for the days left. Marta announces the screen with the features it will certainly have.
- **Fix the scope and the confidence; let the date move.** "All thirty items, 85% likely by mid-November." Marta moves the announcement, or announces a date after the fair.
- **Fix the scope and the date; accept the confidence.** "All thirty items by 6 November is about even odds." Marta announces it anyway, knowing the risk, with a fallback plan agreed now rather than improvised on 5 November.

> **Marta:** If I announce at the fair, which items can you promise?

> **Bia:** Give me the ten that matter most to the shops, in order. I'll run the forecast for the days we have and tell you how far down the list we get at 85%. You choose what goes on the poster.

## What Bia did not do

**She did not pad.** A secret buffer, quoting mid-November as "the date" while expecting the end of October, would have been discovered the first time the team finished early, and every later forecast would have been discounted.

**She did not give in.** Saying "yes, 6 November" to end the conversation would have moved the risk from the person who can manage it, Marta, to the team, who cannot, and it would have been paid for in evenings.

**She did not hide behind the numbers.** The forecast was the start of the conversation, not the end. The decision about risk belonged to Marta, and the forecast's job was to make it a decision rather than a surprise.

## When the answer is still no

Sometimes no lever produces an outcome anybody likes: the date is fixed by the outside world, the scope is fixed by a regulation, and the confidence is low. Then the useful conversation is about **what changes the system**: fewer things in progress at once, lesson 4, removing a dependency, or stopping other work. Adding people late is rarely one of them, for reasons Fred Brooks set out in *The Mythical Man-Month* in 1975 and lesson 12 makes concrete.

A forecast is a snapshot. Every week the history gains seven days, the items left change, and new work is discovered. **Re-running the forecast every week is cheap**, two commands and a minute, and it turns a date from a one-off promise into a running measurement.

## The burn-up with a cone

The most useful picture of a piece of work in progress is a **burn-up chart**: one line for the items finished so far, rising; one line for the total scope, which also rises as work is discovered; and, from today, a fan of forecasts showing where the finished line is likely to meet the scope line.

Two features of the chart do the communicating. **The scope line is drawn honestly**, rising when work is added, so that nobody can mistake growth for slowness. And **the cone narrows** as the work nears its end, because fewer items are left and the remaining uncertainty is smaller.

## What to watch week by week

- **The 85% date moving later, week after week.** One week is noise; three in a row is a system that has changed. Find out what: the scope growing faster than assumed, throughput falling, a blocked item, an incident-heavy fortnight.
- **The scope line rising faster than the finished line.** The work is growing faster than it is being done. The date is receding, and no amount of effort on the existing items will catch it. This is a scope conversation, not a speed one.
- **The cone not narrowing.** If the spread stays wide as items are finished, the items are probably not comparable, lesson 9, or the history window includes another policy, lesson 10.

## Saying so early

When the forecast moves, **say so in the week it moved**, with the new range and the reason. "Our 85% date moved from 30 October to 9 November this week, because the review found two integration cases we had not planned; we have added four items" is a sentence a stakeholder can act on in October. The same news in the first week of November is a missed deadline.

Teams that re-forecast weekly and report changes promptly find that the conversations about dates get easier over time, not harder, because stakeholders learn that the forecast is honest in both directions: it moves earlier as readily as later. **Trust in a forecast is built by the weeks it was updated, not by the day it was right.**

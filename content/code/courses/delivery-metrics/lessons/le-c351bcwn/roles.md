In an incident without roles, everybody does the same thing: they all look at the graphs, they all guess at causes, and nobody tells the support team what to say to the shops calling in. The remedy is borrowed from emergency services. The **Incident Command System** was developed in the 1970s, after large wildfires in California showed that agencies arriving at the same fire could not coordinate, and its central idea carries over intact: **one person coordinates, and does not do the hands-on work**.

## The four roles

**Incident commander (IC).** Owns the incident until it is closed. Decides the severity, assigns the other roles, chooses between options ("do we roll back or wait?"), and keeps asking the three questions that keep an incident moving: what do we know, what are we doing, and when do we check again. **The IC does not debug.** The moment the IC opens a terminal, nobody is coordinating.

**Technical lead.** The person, or small group, investigating and fixing. Usually the on-call engineer and whoever knows the affected system best. They report to the IC what they see and what they propose, and they do the work.

**Communications lead.** Tells everybody outside the response what is happening: the support team, leadership, the status page, the users. They write updates on a fixed rhythm so the people fixing are not interrupted by people asking.

**Scribe.** Writes down what happened, when, as it happens: every observation, every decision, every action, with its time. Lesson 14 is about this role, because it is the one teams most often leave out and most often regret.

## Small incidents, few people

Four roles does not mean four people. In a small SEV3 one person may hold all of them. In a SEV1 at the Billing team's size, five or six people is typical. The roles are **responsibilities that must be held**, and naming who holds each one is the first thing an IC does. "Rafa, you're technical lead. Duda, scribe. Caio, comms. I'm IC." takes ten seconds.

## Who should be IC

Not necessarily the most senior person, and usually not the person who knows the system best, because that person is needed as technical lead. The best IC is somebody who can keep calm, keep track and make a decision with incomplete information. **It is a skill, practised in drills and small incidents**, and a team that rotates the IC role through its members has more people who can do it at two in the morning. The tech lead, Bia in the Billing team, often takes it at first and should not be the only one who can.

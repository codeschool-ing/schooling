The page did most of the work the day before. The half hour is for the questions, and the same few come up in nearly every review. Each has an answer that an earlier lesson already worked out.

## "Can you go faster?"

The director asked it within five minutes. The tempting answers are "yes" and "we'll try", and both are promises with nothing behind them. Bia answered with lesson 1: **the team finishes about one item a day, and that has not changed all quarter.** Cycle time fell because there is less work in progress, not because anybody types faster. Going faster, in the sense of finishing more, means either fewer interruptions, smaller items, or more people, and lesson 12 is the warning about the third. What the team can promise is that whatever it starts, it finishes soon.

## "Can we have a target?"

The head of product suggested making 4 days the target for next quarter. It sounds harmless and it is the most dangerous suggestion in the room, for the reason lesson 7 spent a whole lesson on: a cycle-time target can be met by starting items later, splitting them artificially, or not counting the waiting. Bia's answer was that the number is a symptom, and that the team would report it every quarter, with the rules that produced it, so that a change in either would be visible. **Report the measure; target the practice.** Lesson 20 comes back to this, because it is the line between using a team's numbers and abusing them.

## "How do they compare with the Payments team?"

Lesson 6 answers it: the teams do different work, count items differently and release through different pipelines, and a comparison would reward whichever team splits work smaller. Bia offered the useful version instead: the Payments team could try the 3 August rules and compare itself with its own July.

## "Why should we believe the forecast?"

Because it can be checked. A forecast of "85% likely" is wrong about one time in seven by design, and right the rest; a team that lists its past 85% dates beside what happened shows whether its forecasts behave that way. **A forecast's credibility is its track record**, and the quarterly review is the one place where that record is shown to the people who rely on it.

## Saying the bad news first

The incident was on the page under its own heading, and Bia raised it before anybody asked. That is not only honest; it is practical. A team that brings its own bad news gets to explain it, with the timeline and the action items. A team whose bad news is brought up by somebody else spends the rest of the meeting defending itself, and every good number after that is heard as spin.

## After the meeting

Two things are written down within a day: **the decisions**, so that the next review can start from them, and **the questions nobody could answer**, with who will answer them and by when. The director's decision was that two other teams would try the 3 August rules in November and report their own before and after at the next review. That is a decision the numbers supported, and it is the best outcome a quarterly review can have: not applause, but a choice made on evidence.

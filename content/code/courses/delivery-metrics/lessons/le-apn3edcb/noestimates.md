In 2012 Woody Zuill, a developer and coach, started using the hashtag **#NoEstimates** for a conversation about whether teams needed to estimate every item at all. Vasco Duarte later wrote a book with the same name. The label was provocative on purpose and it is often misread, in both directions.

## What it is not

It is **not a refusal to say when things will be ready.** Nobody serious in the conversation argued that a business should plan without forecasts. Teams practising it give dates; they produce them differently.

It is **not "estimates are always wrong, so never make one."** Estimates are wrong in a predictable way, and the conversation is about whether a cheaper method is as good for the purpose at hand.

## What it is

A set of habits that this lesson has already measured:

- **Split work into small items** of roughly similar size, using the right-sizing question of the previous section.
- **Count items** instead of summing points, because the count forecasts as well and costs nothing.
- **Forecast from throughput history**, which lesson 10 does with a simulation.
- **Spend the time saved on the work**, and on the conversation about what to do next, which is where estimation meetings were supposed to help and usually did not.

At its heart is a question to ask of any practice, estimation included: **what decision does this estimate inform, and would a cheaper method inform it as well?** For "will this item fit in our usual cycle time?", a yes or no does. For "when will these forty items be done?", a forecast from history does, and lesson 10 shows it does better than adding up forty guesses.

## Where it goes wrong

A team that drops estimation without right-sizing has neither: no estimates, and items whose sizes vary by a factor of fifty, so that a count means nothing. The Billing team's history shows the risk mildly, in its eight-point items. **Counting without splitting is just not estimating**, and it produces bad forecasts that discredit the idea. The habit that makes #NoEstimates work is the splitting, and it is the hard part.

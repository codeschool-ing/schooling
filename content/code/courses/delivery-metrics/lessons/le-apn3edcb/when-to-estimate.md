title: When an estimate is worth its cost
version: 1
---

None of this means estimating is always waste. It means an estimate is a tool with a price, and some decisions are worth the price. Four are.

## Deciding whether to do something at all

Before a piece of work is started, someone has to decide whether it is worth doing, and that depends on what it will cost. A rough answer is enough: **is this days, weeks or months?** An order of magnitude, given by the people who will do the work, informs that decision well, and it is cheap because it does not pretend to be precise.

## Work the team has never done

Counting relies on history. A migration, a new payment provider, a regulatory change: work unlike anything in the team's past has no history to count. Here an estimate is the only forecast available, and the best way to make it is to look for a **reference class**, similar work done elsewhere, in the team's own past or in another team's, and ask how long that took. Daniel Kahneman called this taking the *outside view*: instead of reasoning forward from the plan, which reliably produces optimism, start from what happened to projects like it.

## Commitments made to someone outside

A contract with a fixed price, a launch tied to a date somebody else chose, a dependency another team is waiting on. When a promise has to be made before the work starts, it has to rest on something, and an estimate given as **a range with a probability**, "between six and ten weeks, most likely eight", is honest in a way a single number is not. Lesson 11 is about that conversation.

## Finding a misunderstanding

The most underrated use of an estimate is not the number at all. When two people on a team give the same item a one and an eight, one of them has understood something the other has not. **The disagreement is the useful output**, and a team can get it with a two-minute conversation without ever writing a number down.

## What to stop doing

What rarely earns its cost is the habit most teams have: estimating every item, every sprint, in a meeting with everyone present, and then summing the numbers into a forecast. The Billing team's files measured why. The estimates predicted individual durations weakly, and summed into weeks they were no steadier than a count. That meeting is time that could go into splitting the work, which improves both the forecast and the flow.

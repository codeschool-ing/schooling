A team that is asked "how big is this?" can answer in three currencies, and most teams use one of them out of habit rather than choice. Each answers a slightly different question and costs a different amount to produce.

| currency | the answer looks like | what it is good at | what it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **time** | "three days" | a date, if the guess is right | a commitment people hear even when you call it a guess |
| **story points** | "a five" | comparing items with each other | a planning meeting, and a number people convert to time anyway |
| **a count** | "one item" | forecasting from history, lessons 10 and 11 | splitting work until items are of similar size |

**Time** is the currency everybody outside the team understands, which is its strength and its trap: "three days" is heard as a promise, and lesson 2 showed that most of an item's elapsed time is waiting, which no estimate of effort includes.

**Story points**, which `process-management` covered in its own lesson, were invented to avoid that trap: a relative measure of size, deliberately not in hours, so that nobody converts it into a deadline. In practice it is converted anyway, usually by dividing by a velocity, and the meetings that produce the points are among the most expensive a team holds.

**A count** is what this course has used since lesson 1. It needs no meeting: every item counts as one. It works on one condition, that items are of broadly similar size, and the section after next is about meeting that condition deliberately.

## The question underneath

All three are answers to the same underlying question, **"when will it be ready?"**, which this lesson does not answer and lesson 10 does. What this lesson asks is narrower and more uncomfortable: **does the estimate add anything to what the team's history already says?** The Billing team estimated every item in points, and its files record both the estimate and what happened, so the question can be measured instead of debated.

A Monte Carlo forecast sounds sophisticated and is one of the simplest programs in this course. The idea fits in three sentences.

1. **Write down the team's throughput for every day of a recent period**: 0 items on a Saturday, 2 on a Tuesday, 1 on a Wednesday.
2. **Make up one possible future** by drawing days from that list at random, one draw for each day ahead, and adding up the items.
3. **Do it ten thousand times**, and look at how the totals are spread.

## Why it works

Each simulated day is a day that really happened. It carries every interruption, every review that waited and every item that turned out bigger than it looked, because those are what made that day's throughput what it was. Drawing them at random and in a different order asks: **if the next month is made of days like the last six weeks, in some order, how much gets done?** Ten thousand answers to that question show which totals are common and which are rare.

The method makes three assumptions, and the rest of the lesson keeps them in view:

- **the recent past resembles the near future**: same team, same rules, same kind of work;
- **the days are independent**: a good Tuesday does not make Wednesday better or worse;
- **items are comparable**: a forecast in items needs items of broadly similar size, which is lesson 9's right-sizing.

## Why ten thousand

Fewer runs give a lumpier histogram, and a percentile read from it moves a little each time the program runs. Ten thousand is enough that the percentiles barely change between runs, and it takes a second on any computer. The programs in this lesson fix the random seed so that your runs are the same as the lesson's; remove the seed and the answers will wobble by an item or a day, which is a useful reminder that a forecast is a distribution and not a number.

## Which days to learn from

The programs learn from **17 August to 30 September**: forty-five days, all under the new rules, starting two weeks after the change so that the old system's leftovers, which lesson 1 saw draining through August, have mostly gone. The choice of window is the most important decision in the whole forecast, and this lesson's sixth section changes it to show how much.

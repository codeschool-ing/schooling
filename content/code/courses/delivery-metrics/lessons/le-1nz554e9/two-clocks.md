title: Two clocks, and where each one starts
version: 1

Ask three people on a team how long an item takes and you can get three honest answers that disagree by weeks. One means from the moment a developer picked it up; one means from the day the request was written down; one means until it was merged, another until a customer could use it. **None of them is wrong. They are reading different clocks**, and an argument about a number is usually an argument about which clock nobody said out loud.

This course uses two, and names them every time.

| clock | starts | stops | whose view |
|---|---|---|---|
| **cycle time** | when somebody starts the item | when its change merges | the team's: how long the work took once begun |
| **lead time** | when the request enters the backlog | when the change is running in production | the requester's: how long they waited for it |

**Cycle time is the one a team controls most directly.** Everything inside it is the team's own doing: how many items they keep open, how fast reviews happen, how often work is blocked. Lessons 1, 3 and 4 work on it.

**Lead time is the one the rest of the company feels.** A shop owner who reported a wrong invoice does not care when a developer started; they care when the fix arrived. Lead time contains cycle time and adds two stretches the team often forgets it owns: **the wait in the backlog before anybody starts, and the wait between the merge and the deployment**.

## A third clock with the same name

Lesson 5 meets the DORA metrics, and one of them is called *lead time for changes*. It is a different clock again: from a commit to that commit running in production. It measures the pipeline and the review, and it ignores the backlog completely. The two share a name and answer different questions, which is exactly the situation in which a report says "lead time went down" and two people in the room picture different things. **When you quote a lead time, say where it starts.**

## Where the clock starts is a decision

The start of lead time looks obvious and is not. On the Billing team every request that enters the backlog is eventually done, oldest first, because the simulation works that way. On a real team the backlog holds ideas that will never be built, and measuring from the day each was written down has two problems. The clock runs on things nobody has agreed to do; and the only items that ever report a lead time are the ones that were eventually done, which quietly flatters the number.

The Kanban community's answer is the **commitment point**: the moment the team agrees that an item will be done, often a column called *Ready* or *Selected*. Lead time is measured from there. The Billing team's `created` column is that moment by construction, because nothing in their backlog is ever dropped. For your own team, write down which column starts each clock, put it at the top of the board, and do not move it without saying so; a change of definition looks exactly like an improvement on a chart.

## Counting days

Two smaller choices change every number by a little, and each should be written down rather than argued about later.

- **Elapsed or inclusive.** This course counts the days between two dates: started on 1 September and merged on the 3rd is two days. Some tools count both ends and call it three, which is Daniel Vacanti's convention in *Actionable Agile Metrics for Predictability*. Neither is wrong, and mixing them is.
- **Calendar or working days.** This course counts calendar days, because a requester's wait does not pause on Saturday, and because Little's law in lesson 1 needs work in progress and cycle time on the same calendar.

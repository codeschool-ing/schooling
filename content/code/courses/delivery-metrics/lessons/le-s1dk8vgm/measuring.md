title: Measuring which alerts are worth it
version: 1
---

A team cannot argue its way to a quieter pager; everybody has an alert they are sure is important. It can measure. The measure is simple: **of the times this alert paged somebody, how many needed that person to do something?** Call it the alert's **actionable rate**.

The data is already there for a team that writes handover notes the way lesson 17 describes. The heading **pages this week** lists every page with what was done, and "nothing; it cleared on its own" is an answer that counts.

## A quarter of pages

**Save the program below as `pages.py`.** It holds the Billing team's alerts for the quarter from 8 July to 6 October, as counted from thirteen handover notes, and gives each one a verdict.

```schooling-example

      "note": "**The quarter's pages, one line per alert**, counted from the handover notes of lesson 17: how often it paged, how many of those needed somebody to act, and how many came at night. On a real team this is an export from the paging tool, with the \"needed a person\" column filled in from the notes."
    },
    {
      "code": "pages = sum(a[1] for a in ALERTS)\nneeded = sum(a[2] for a in ALERTS)\nprint(f\"{pages} pages, {needed} needed a person ({needed / pages:.0%})\")\nfor name, paged, useful, night in ALERTS:\n    share = useful / paged\n    verdict = \"keep\" if share >= 0.5 else \"remove or make it a ticket\" if useful == 0 else \"fix\"\n    print(f\"  {name:33} {paged:3} pages  {share:4.0%} needed  {night} at night  {verdict}\")\nkept = [a for a in ALERTS if a[2] / a[1] >= 0.5]\nprint(f\"paging only on the kept alerts: {sum(a[1] for a in kept)} pages, \"\n      f\"{sum(a[3] for a in kept)} at night\")\n",
      "note": "**A verdict per alert.** Half or more of its pages needed a person: keep it. None did: remove it or make it a ticket. Somewhere between: fix it. The last line counts what the pager would have done with only the alerts worth keeping."
    }
  ]
}
```

## Reading it

- **One page in five needed a person.** The other four taught whoever was on call that pages can wait, which is the lesson Rafa had learnt by 30 September.
- **The noisiest alert never needed anybody.** "Database disk over 80%" paged 19 times, seven of them at night, and every time the disk had been filling slowly for days. It is a ticket's job: the disk needs attention this week, not tonight.
- **The alert Rafa ignored was right three times in sixteen.** "Card provider p99 over 2 s" is not useless; it is a cause dressed as a symptom, firing whenever the provider is slow, whether or not the shops notice. Three of its pages mattered, and the other thirteen made sure nobody believed the fourteenth.
- **The three alerts worth keeping are symptoms.** Charges failing, the statement job failing, refunds failing: things a shop would notice, each one needing somebody to act.

The last line is the case for acting: **paging only on the alerts worth keeping would have meant 13 pages in the quarter instead of 82, and 3 at night instead of 25.**

## What the number cannot tell you

The actionable rate measures what happened, not what might. An alert that paged twice and needed nobody both times may still be the one that catches the disaster next year; a disk that has never filled before still can. That is why the program's verdict for a never-useful alert is "remove **or make it a ticket**": the signal is kept, and moved to where it costs nobody's sleep. The judgement about which is which belongs to the team, and the number only makes sure the judgement is made with the evidence on the table.

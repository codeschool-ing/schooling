The chapter on monitoring in Google's SRE book gives a short list of questions to ask of any rule that pages a person. Put in a team's words:

- **Is it urgent?** If it can wait until morning, it should.
- **Can the person do something about it?** A page that ends in "nothing to do but watch" is a notification, not a page.
- **Are users being hurt, or about to be?** A server under strain while every charge is answered in a second is not a problem anybody outside the team has.
- **Will I ever be able to ignore it?** If the honest answer is yes, sometimes, the rule is wrong, because it will teach people to ignore it always.

An alert that pages has to pass all four. Anything else still has a place, just not in somebody's pocket at three in the morning.

## Three places for a signal

| where it goes | for | example from the Billing team |
|---|---|---|
| **a page**, which wakes somebody now | urgent, actionable, user-visible | card charge errors over 1%; refunds failing |
| **a ticket**, read in working hours | needs a person, not tonight | a TLS certificate expiring in 30 days; disk filling over a week |
| **a log line or a dashboard** | worth seeing when somebody looks | API CPU at 85%; one health check failing once |

Most teams have too many pages because they never built the other two. Without a ticket queue that somebody reads, "page it" becomes the only way to make sure something is seen, and every alert drifts upwards into the pager.

## Symptoms, not causes

The four questions favour one kind of alert over another. **A symptom** is something the user experiences: charges failing, charges slow, a statement missing on the 1st. **A cause** is something inside the system that might lead to a symptom: CPU high, a queue long, a disk filling.

Causes make bad pages for two reasons. They fire when nothing is wrong, because the system often copes, and CPU at 85% with every charge answered in a second is a system doing its job. And they stay silent when something is wrong for a reason nobody thought of, because nobody wrote an alert for it. A symptom fires whatever the cause, and only when the user is affected.

The burn-rate alert of lesson 16 is the strongest symptom alert a team has: it asks how fast the shops are being let down, and does not care why. **Page on symptoms; keep causes on the dashboard**, where they help whoever is investigating a symptom find out why.

## Every page comes with a runbook

One rule turns the four questions into a habit: **an alert that pages has a runbook**, a short page linked from the alert that says what it means, how to check whether it is real, and what to try first. Writing one is the test. An alert whose runbook would say "look around and see" fails the question about action, and an alert nobody wants to write a runbook for is one nobody believes in. The runbook is also where Rafa's knowledge, from lesson 17, ends up when he is not awake.

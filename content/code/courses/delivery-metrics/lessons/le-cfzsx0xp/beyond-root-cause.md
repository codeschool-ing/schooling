The phrase "root cause" suggests that an incident has one, and that finding it is the point. For simple failures that is nearly true. For most incidents in software it is a story told afterwards, choosing one thread from a knot, and the choice of thread decides what gets fixed.

## The double charge as a knot

Asked for the root cause of 30 September, most people would say **the retry in `BIL-218`**. That is one thread. Here are the others, every one of which, changed, would have prevented the incident or made it much smaller:

- **The retry had no idempotency key**, so the card provider could not tell a retry from a new charge. Retrying payments safely needs one, and nothing in the team's practice required it.
- **The tests had no slow provider.** In testing the provider always answered quickly, so the retry never fired.
- **The review did not ask about retries.** Duda reviewed the change carefully for what it said it did; nothing prompted a question about what happens when a charge succeeds slowly.
- **It shipped on the busiest afternoon of the month**, when month-end charges made the provider slow enough to trigger the retry.
- **Nothing alerted.** Seventeen minutes passed before a customer noticed; an alert existed as an action item from August and had not been built.
- **The release reached every shop at once.** A change released to a small share of shops first would have harmed a few dozen; that too was an open action item, from July.

These are **contributing factors**, and a good postmortem lists them all. It then asks, for each, what would make it less likely or less harmful next time.

## The trouble with five whys

"Ask why five times" is a popular technique and a useful prompt, with a known weakness: it follows **one chain**. Why were shops double-charged? The retry charged again. Why? It could not tell a slow success from a failure. Why? No idempotency key. Why? Nobody required one. Why? The team had no guidance on payment retries. That chain is real and leads to a good action item. It also walks straight past the missing alert, the absent canary release and the month-end timing, each of which is its own chain.

Use five whys **on each contributing factor**, not on the incident as a whole. The incident has several chains; each factor usually has one.

## "Human error" is where to start

When a postmortem finds that a person did something that, in hindsight, caused the failure, that is the **beginning** of the analysis, not the end. The next question is always what made the action reasonable at the time, and what would have caught it. Duda's review was reasonable; the checklist did not ask about retries. Rafa's retry was a sensible fix for a real problem, slow card payments; the guidance on doing it safely did not exist. Each of those is fixable, and neither is fixed by asking two people to try harder.

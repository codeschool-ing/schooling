title: Planning a quarter without filling it

Put the lesson's three ideas together, the curve, capacity, and slack, and planning a quarter changes shape. The old way starts from headcount, multiplies by weeks and fills the result with items. The new way starts from the history and leaves room on purpose.

## Five steps

1. **Start from throughput.** The Billing team finishes about one item a day, seven a week, measured after every meeting, review and incident. A quarter of thirteen weeks is roughly ninety items at that rate.
2. **Forecast, do not multiply.** Run lesson 10's forecast for the quarter's days. The 85% answer, not the average, is the number of items the team can be confident of.
3. **Plan to a fraction of it.** Commit 70 to 80% of the 85% number to planned work. The rest is for the unplanned work the history says will come, and for improvement.
4. **Order the rest.** Everything that does not fit goes below a line, in order. It is not rejected; it is what the team does next if the quarter goes well.
5. **Re-forecast every week**, lesson 11, and move the line as the quarter unfolds.

The result is a plan that the team expects to meet, with a visible list of what comes next. When something urgent arrives mid-quarter, a team planned this way can take it on; a team planned to 100% can only take it on by dropping something, usually without anybody deciding which.

## The plan that is allocated at 100%

The commonest planning artefact in large organisations is a spreadsheet of people and percentages: Caio 50% on invoicing, 30% on refunds, 20% on the platform migration; Duda 100% on invoicing; and so on, every row summing to exactly 100%. **It is a picture of the curve's right-hand end.** Every person is fully allocated, so every new request waits; every person is split across work, so lesson 4's switching cost returns; and every project's progress depends on people whose time is promised elsewhere.

It also hides the most important fact about the plan: nothing in it can absorb a surprise. The first incident, the first sick week or the first urgent request is taken from somebody's 100%, and some project slips without anybody having decided that it should.

## Adding people

When a plan is late, the reflex is to add people. Fred Brooks described in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975) why that usually makes a late project later: new people need time to become productive, and they take time from the people who are productive, to explain, review and coordinate. In the terms of this lesson, **a new person reduces the team's capacity before increasing it**, and on a team already near the right of the curve the reduction lands as longer queues.

Adding people is right for a team that will exist for years and needs more capacity for years. It is wrong as a fix for a date next month. For that, lesson 11's levers, scope, date and confidence, are the honest ones.

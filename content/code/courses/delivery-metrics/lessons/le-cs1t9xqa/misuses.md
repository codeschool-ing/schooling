---
title: What they must not be used for
version: 1
---

Each misuse below is tempting, common, and done by people who mean well. Each one also has a lesson in this course that shows what it does to the numbers, and that is the part worth remembering when somebody proposes it.

## Ranking or reviewing individuals

**"Can I have cycle time per developer for the performance reviews?"** It is the request every team that measures eventually receives. Lesson 8 is the answer: work is shared, reviews are done by colleagues, the hardest problems are passed to whoever can solve them, and a person's number mostly says what kind of work landed on them. On the Billing team, a per-person table of pages would have put Rafa first and Caio last, and lesson 17 showed why: everybody called Rafa about the card code, and the month-end never fell in Caio's weeks. Neither number says anything about how good either of them is at the job. Use it to judge people, and they stop helping each other, which is the one thing the team's speed depended on.

## Targets on a measure

**"Next quarter, get cycle time under three days."** Lesson 7 showed how: start items later, split them artificially, close them before review is really done, leave the waiting off the board. The number falls; the shops wait as long as before, and the team has lost the instrument that would have told them so. **Report the measure; target the practice**, the line from lesson 19. "Keep work in progress at one item per person" is a practice a team can follow; "three days" is a number it can only manage.

## Comparing teams

**"Why does Payments deploy twice as often as you?"** Lesson 6: different work, different pipelines, different ways of counting an item. The comparison rewards whichever team splits its work smallest, and it teaches every team to make its numbers look like the leader's rather than to fix its own system. The legitimate version is a team against its own past.

## Punishing incidents

**"Who was responsible for the double charge?"** Lesson 15 answered it at length. A team that is punished for incidents learns to report fewer of them, declare them later, and write postmortems that protect people instead of explaining the system. The incident count falls, the error budget looks healthier, and the next 30 September arrives with less warning. **A blameless postmortem is not kindness; it is how a team keeps getting the facts.**

## Turning a forecast into a commitment

**"You said end of October, so end of October it is."** Lesson 10: the median of a forecast is a coin toss. Turning a probability into a promise moves the risk from the person who could manage it onto the team, who cannot, and it is paid for in evenings and in the next forecast, which the team will quietly pad. A forecast that is punished when it is wrong stops being a forecast.

## Reading a dashboard without the team

**A director browsing the team's charts and drawing conclusions** is the quiet misuse, and the hardest to see. The chart that looks alarming, a cumulative flow band widening, an ageing item, was the team's instrument for the problem it was already fixing. Lesson 3's working charts were built to be read daily by the people doing the work. Read once by somebody else, out of context, they produce questions about the wrong things and pressure in the wrong places. **The team's numbers are presented by the team**, with the context that makes them mean something.

## The pattern

Every misuse above takes a number the team produced to see its own system and uses it to judge something it was never designed to judge: a person, a team against another, a promise. **And every one of them destroys the number it uses**, because the people being judged are the people who record the data. That is the argument to make when the request comes, and it is stronger than "it would be unfair": it would stop working.

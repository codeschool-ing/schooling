Nineteen lessons taught how to produce a team's numbers: cycle time and its spread, the four DORA metrics, forecasts with probabilities, error budgets, on-call load, the share of pages worth answering. Every one of them was computed from records the Billing team already kept, and every one changed something. This last lesson asks the question all of that was for: **what may those numbers be used for, and by whom?**

It is not an appendix. It is the point, because the numbers do not decide their own use, and the same number does opposite things depending on whose hands it is in.

## One number, two uses

Take the most ordinary number in the course: how many pages woke each person last quarter, from lesson 17. Rafa 54, Caio 6.

**In the team's hands**, at the monthly review, that number found a problem nobody had seen: one person carrying half the pager because everybody passed him the card code. It led to runbooks, a call instead of a hand-off, Bia joining the rota. Rafa's load is falling, and the knowledge he held is spreading.

**In other hands**, the same number becomes a judgement about people. Rafa is "the one who handles incidents", or "the one who causes them". Caio "is not pulling his weight on call". Neither reading is true, both are easy, and the moment either is said in a performance review, the next quarter's numbers change: people stop passing problems on even when they should, stop writing them down in the handover notes, and the pager log becomes a record of what people are willing to admit to. **The number that found the problem is gone, and the problem is still there.**

## Why this keeps happening

Lesson 7 named the mechanism: when a measure becomes a target, people change the measure instead of the thing it measured. This lesson adds who does it. Teams do not game their numbers out of dishonesty; they do it because somebody above them used the numbers in a way that made gaming the rational choice. **Every misuse of a team's metrics is a decision taken by somebody who was not on the team**, usually with good intentions, and usually in a hurry.

So the protection cannot come from the team's honesty alone. It has to be agreed in advance, written down, and held by the people who would otherwise do the misusing. The rest of this lesson is what goes into that agreement, and why.

## What the course has been doing all along

Looking back, every lesson that taught a number also taught a limit on it:

- lesson 2 taught a percentile, and that an average hides the item that took two months;
- lesson 6, that DORA describes a system, not a team's worth;
- lesson 7, how each chart is gamed, and what the gaming looks like;
- lesson 8, that no single number describes a developer, and none should;
- lesson 10, that a forecast is a probability, and its median is a coin toss;
- lesson 16, that a spent budget is not anybody's fault;
- lesson 18, that the actionable rate is easy to inflate on paper.

This lesson gathers those limits into one place, because a team that knows them one at a time loses them one at a time.

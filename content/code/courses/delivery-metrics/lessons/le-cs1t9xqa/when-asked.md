The requests arrive in ordinary words, usually from somebody with a real need. The answer that works is never a flat refusal: it names what the request would do to the numbers, and then offers what the person actually needs. Four of them, as they reached Bia in the weeks after the review.

## "Per developer, for the reviews"

> **The engineering director:** HR wants something objective for the performance reviews. Can you send cycle time per developer?
>
> **Bia:** I can't, and it wouldn't be objective. Our items are shared, Rafa takes the hardest card problems from everybody, and reviews are done by whoever is free. A per-person number would mostly say who got the hard work. And the day it counts in a review, people stop helping each other and the number stops meaning anything, for the team as well as for HR.
>
> **The engineering director:** So what do I give HR?
>
> **Bia:** What I see every day: who unblocks others, who wrote the runbooks this quarter, who kept the timeline and who talked to support on the 30th. I'll write a paragraph for each of the five. It's more work than a number, and it's true.

## "A target for next quarter"

> **The head of product:** Four days is great. Can we commit to three next quarter?
>
> **Bia:** The four days came from a practice: one item each, reviews first. If we aim at the number instead, the easiest way to hit three is to split items smaller and start them later, and you'd see a better chart with nothing faster behind it. What I can commit to is the practice, and to showing you the number every quarter so you can see whether it holds.

## "Why are they faster than you?"

> **Marta:** The Payments team says they deploy every day. Why don't you?
>
> **Bia:** Their deploys and ours aren't the same thing: they release configuration changes we'd bundle into one. The comparison would push both teams to count differently, not to work better. What I can show you is that we went from five releases in July to twenty in September, and what that did for the shops.

## "Who caused it?"

> **A director from another department:** I heard about the double charges. Whose change was it?
>
> **Bia:** It was a release with four changes, and the postmortem found six things that had to line up, three of them fixes we'd agreed and not done. If I give you a name, the next incident gets reported an hour later, by somebody who has learnt what happens to the person named. The postmortem is open to you, and it says what we're changing.

## What the four answers share

Each one names **what the misuse would destroy**, in the asker's own terms: HR would get a number that measures the wrong thing, the head of product a chart with nothing behind it. Each one **offers the legitimate version**: a written assessment, a practice, a team against its own past, a postmortem. And none of them is defensive. The numbers are not being hidden; they are being kept useful.

## The point of the course

A team's numbers are an instrument the team uses to see its own work: where the time goes, how good its delivery is, when things will be ready, what happens when they break. Used that way, they make a team calmer, its promises more honest and its weeks more sustainable, which is what nineteen lessons showed on one team's history. Used to judge people, they stop being true within a quarter, and the team is left without the instrument and with the judgement.

**Measuring well is half of the craft. Deciding what the measurement is for, and keeping it that way, is the other half**, and it is the half that falls to whoever answers for a team.

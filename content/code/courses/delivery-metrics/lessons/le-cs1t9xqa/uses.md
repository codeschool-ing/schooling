The legitimate uses have something in common: **the people the numbers describe can see them, understand them, and act on them.** A number used that way gets more honest over time, because everybody has a reason to keep it accurate.

## The team improving its own system

The first use, and the one the course spent most time on. The Billing team changed its rules on 3 August and used its own board's history to see what the change did; it pruned its alerts because the handover notes showed four pages in five needed nobody. In both cases the team read its own numbers, changed its own rules, and measured the result. **Nobody outside the team had to ask for any of it.**

## Answering "when?" honestly

Forecasts are the use that most directly protects a team. In lesson 11, Bia told Marta that all thirty items were 85% likely by mid-November, and the 6 November fair got the features that were certain. Without the history, the answer would have been a date somebody hoped for, and missing it would have been paid for in evenings. **A forecast lets the person who owns the risk manage it**, instead of moving it silently onto the team.

## Deciding where to invest

The quarterly review of lesson 19 ended with a decision: two more teams would try the 3 August rules. That is a use of the numbers by people outside the team, and a legitimate one, because it decides about **a way of working**, not about the people working. The other teams will measure their own before and after, against themselves.

## Saying no with evidence

The least obvious use is defensive. When the error budget was spent, the policy of lesson 16 stopped feature releases for a month, and nobody had to win an argument to make that happen. When the director asked "can you go faster?", Little's law answered for Bia. **The numbers give a team a way to refuse a demand without refusing the person making it**, and that is often what keeps the team's week sustainable.

## The test for a new use

A use not listed here can be tested with three questions:

1. **Does it decide about the system, or about a person?** The first is what the numbers can carry; the second, they cannot.
2. **Will the people it describes see it, and could they explain it?** A number used out of their sight is a number they will learn to manage.
3. **If they knew it would be used this way, would they still record it honestly?** If the answer is no, the use will destroy the data it depends on, and it will do so quietly.

A use that passes all three is safe to make. One that fails any of them is the subject of the next section.

Refusing to rank people is half an answer. The other half is doing something better with the legitimate questions a ranking was supposed to answer.

## "Is anybody struggling?"

**Ask, regularly, in private.** A one-to-one conversation, every week or two, finds a struggling person sooner than any metric, and finds out why, which no metric does. The `people-leadership` course, the one before this in the Technical Leadership track, is about how to run those conversations. What this course adds is the team's data as a prompt: an item that has aged on one person's name, a survey answer at the bottom of the scale, a run of nights on call. Each is a reason to ask, never a conclusion.

## "Does the team have the people it needs?"

**Look at the team's flow, not its members.** Lesson 2's question, where does the waiting happen, answers this better than any count of individual output. In July the Billing team's work waited in front of one reviewer. The answer was not "Bia is unproductive" or "hire a reviewer". It was a change in who reviews, which fixed the queue with the same people. Lesson 12 turns this into arithmetic about capacity.

## "Does this person deserve a promotion?"

**Gather evidence of contribution, including the invisible kind.** Reviews given and what they caught, incidents handled, colleagues taught, a design that made later work easier, the blocked item somebody finally unstuck. Much of it is in the systems, which record reviews and incidents, and the rest is in what colleagues say. The difference from a ranking is that this evidence is read **about one person, by somebody who knows the context**, rather than computed for everybody and sorted.

## Measure the team, talk to the people

The rule that summarises the lesson is short. **Team-level measures are for improving the system; individual conversations are for supporting people.** Mixing them breaks both: a team measure turned on individuals stops measuring the team, because people optimise their own number; a conversation built on a ranking stops being a conversation, because it starts with a verdict.

Lesson 20 returns to this as the first of the rules about what a team's numbers may be used for. It is the course's central argument with itself, and this lesson is where it first appears.

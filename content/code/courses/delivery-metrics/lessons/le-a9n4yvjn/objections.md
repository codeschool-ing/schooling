title: The objections, answered with the history
version: 1
---

Every team that tries a limit hears the same five objections. Each has a reasonable worry inside it, and each can be answered with something more useful than conviction.

## "People will be idle"

Sometimes they will, for an hour. The Billing team's history says what happened over months: **throughput rose from 6.0 to 7.0 items a week** while work in progress fell to a quarter, and in the experiment of this lesson's third section three policies finished 122 to 128 items. Idle-looking time was spent reviewing, which was the bottleneck. A team where people are always busy is usually a team where items are always waiting, because somebody's full calendar is somebody else's queue. Lesson 12 makes this precise.

## "Some work is urgent"

It is, and an expedite lane with a limit of one handles it without abandoning the limit for everything else. The failure to avoid is the one where everything becomes urgent because urgency is the only way past the limit. Count how often the expedite lane is used; if it is every day, the problem is upstream of the board.

## "Our items are too different in size"

A limit counts items, and a large item and a small one each take one place. That is a feature. A large item holding a place for weeks shows on the ageing chart and invites the right question: can it be split into pieces that finish? Lesson 9 shows that counting items forecasts about as well as weighing them, which is part of why counting is enough.

## "We have to start things for many stakeholders at once"

Starting is not delivering. A stakeholder whose item is started and then waits three weeks has gained the feeling of progress and lost nothing but time, while a stakeholder whose item starts a week later and finishes in four days is better served. **A limit turns "started" from a promise into a schedule.** What a team owes many stakeholders is an honest order and an honest forecast, lessons 10 and 11.

## "The limit is always full, so it isn't working"

A limit that is always full is doing its job, if the items behind it are young: it is holding the team to finishing. The Billing team's work in progress sat at six every day of September. The question is never whether the limit is reached; it is **what is holding its places**. One of those six places was `BIL-189`, blocked for forty days. The limit was working, and one of its places had been quietly given away.

## The objection nobody says out loud

The real resistance is often simpler: a limit makes waiting visible, and visible waiting makes somebody accountable for it. Before 3 August, Bia's review queue was spread across five people's open items, and nobody's fault. After it, a queue was a queue. That visibility is uncomfortable at first and it is the reason to do it. **Waiting that nobody can see is waiting nobody fixes.**

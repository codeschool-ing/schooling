The commonest objection to a work-in-progress limit is that it will slow the team down. Fewer items open, the argument goes, means fewer items being worked on, which means fewer items finished. The Billing team's history lets you check that argument instead of having it. **Save the program below as `compare.py`.** It puts any number of periods side by side.

Read it line by line, because each line answers a different worry.

**Work in progress fell from 21.6 to 6.0.** That was the change itself: from up to three items per developer, plus a review queue that kept growing, to one item each and reviews done first.

**Throughput did not fall. It rose, from 6.0 items a week to 7.0.** The team finished slightly more with far less open. That is the result the objection says is impossible, and it is the ordinary result for a team that had too much open: the work was not being slowed by a lack of open items, it was being slowed by waiting.

**Cycle time fell by three quarters.** The median went from 18 days to 4 and the 85th percentile from 29 to 8. Little's law said it would, and lesson 1 measured why: with throughput about the same, cutting work in progress to a quarter cuts the time each item spends inside to about a quarter.

## Why throughput can rise

Two effects in `billing.py` are worth naming, because they are what happens on real teams too.

- **Switching costs.** A developer with three items open loses part of each day getting back into whichever one they pick up. The program charges a fifth of the day for it, which is a modest guess; Gerald Weinberg's often-quoted figures in *Quality Software Management* are larger.
- **The bottleneck got help.** Before 3 August one person reviewed everything. After it, everybody reviewed, before starting anything new. The queue in front of review, which lessons 2 and 3 found, stopped being the place where work waited.

Neither effect needs anybody to work harder. Both come from **finishing before starting**, which is the whole policy in three words.

## One month is not a verdict

September is one month and thirty items. A single month's throughput moves around by itself, and the next section shows a version of this team whose September throughput is higher than the real one with no limit at all. **Compare policies over long periods, and compare cycle times more readily than throughput**, because cycle times move a lot when work in progress changes and throughput moves a little.

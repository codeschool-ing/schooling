## `sql-databases`, position 5: where the records live

A relational database keeps data in tables of exactly the shape lesson 1 asked of a sheet: one row
per record, one column per field, one value per cell, and a key that names each row. The difference
is that the database **enforces** that shape. It is where the signals of writing in section 05
lead, and the course takes up threads this one left loose:

| here | there |
|---|---|
| keys and the columns that point at them, lesson 1 | its lesson 1: primary and foreign keys, which the database checks on every row |
| validation that a paste can get round, lesson 8 | its lesson 3: data types and constraints, which nothing gets round |
| `SUMIFS` in lesson 5, pivot tables in lesson 10 | its lesson 6: `GROUP BY` and aggregation |
| many people on one file, section 03 of this lesson | its lesson 8: transactions, so that writes at the same time do not collide |

The question this course has asked most often, revenue by channel, looks like this in SQL:

```sql
SELECT Channel,
       COUNT(*)          AS sales,
       SUM(Bags * Price) AS revenue
FROM Sales
GROUP BY Channel
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
```

Run over Café Serra's 108 sales, in SQLite, it returns three rows:

| `Channel` | `sales` | `revenue` |
|---|---|---|
| `Wholesale` | 38 | 38,731 |
| `Online` | 47 | 11,143 |
| `Shop` | 23 | 1,620 |

They are the numbers a `SUMIFS` per channel or a pivot table with `Channel` in its rows gives. On
your workbook,

```localised
=SUMIFS(Sales[Revenue], Sales[Channel], "Wholesale")
```

answers **38,731**, the first row. One business, asked the same question by three tools, gives one
answer, which is the promise of lesson 1 kept one course further on.

## `analytics-bi`, position 8: where the answers are shared

A BI platform is where a dashboard goes when many people read it. The course covers Power BI in its
lesson 4, and **Power BI is built from the same parts as lessons 13 to 16**: Power Query, with the
same M language, to import and shape; a data model with relationships; and DAX, the same language,
for measures. Power BI Desktop can even import an Excel workbook's queries and data model. The
`Total Revenue`, `Revenue LY` and `Revenue YoY %` you wrote in lesson 16 are written the same way there.

What changes is everything lesson 17's section on sharing had to work around:

- **one copy, published**, that people open in a browser, instead of a file in each inbox;
- **refresh on a schedule**, instead of somebody pressing Refresh All on Monday morning;
- **access by person**, down to showing each reader only the rows they may see, instead of a PDF
  for anyone who should not have the data.

Its lessons 2 and 3 take the step after that: defining a metric once, for every team, so that the
same KPI cannot give three answers in three workbooks. Its lesson 6 returns to dashboard design,
the subject of lesson 17, with more tools and the same rules.

## Before either

`statistics`, at position 4, comes next in the track and is the one most worth taking before the
other two. A dashboard full of averages and year-on-year percentages is only as sound as the reader's
sense of what an average hides and when a change is just noise, and that course is about exactly
that.

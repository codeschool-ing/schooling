**A model that is easy to use has one table of events in the middle and one table per way of
describing them around it.** The table of events is the **fact table**. The tables around it are
**dimensions**. Drawn on paper, the dimensions sit around the facts like the points of a star, and
the arrangement is called a **star schema**.

The Café Serra model has one fact table and three dimensions:

| table | kind | rows | what one row is | what it holds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Sales` | fact | 108 | one sale | numbers to add up (`Bags`, `Price`, `Revenue`) and the codes of what it was about |
| `Products` | dimension | 6 | one product | ways to describe a product: `Origin`, `Roast`, `Grams` |
| `Customers` | dimension | 11 | one customer | ways to describe a customer: `Type`, `City`, `State` |
| `Calendar` | dimension | 730 | one day | ways to describe a day: `Year`, `Month`, `Quarter` |

The first three are the tables you have had since lesson 7. `Calendar` is new, and section 06 of
this lesson builds it.

## Telling a fact from a dimension

The test is the grain of lesson 1 section 06, asked of each table. A fact table's row is something
that **happened**, with a date, and there are many of them: a new sale is a new row, every day. A
dimension's row is something that **exists**, and there are few: a new product is rare, and its row
does not say when anything happened to it.

The columns follow from that. A fact table holds two kinds: **numbers** that a report adds up, and
**keys** that point at dimensions. `Sales` has `Bags`, `Price` and `Revenue`, and it has `Date`,
`Customer` and `Product`. A dimension holds its own key and **attributes**, the words a report
groups and filters by. In a pivot table, the rule of thumb is short: **rows and columns come from
dimensions, values come from the fact table.**

## Why `Channel` stays in `Sales`

`Channel` describes a sale and has only three values, so it could have a dimension of its own. It
does not need one. A dimension earns its place by carrying attributes: `Products` turns a code into
an origin, a roast and a weight. A `Channels` table would hold the word `Online` and nothing else
about it, and a pivot table can group by `Sales[Channel]` directly. The day Café Serra wants to
record something about a channel, such as the fee its payment provider charges, is the day the
table is worth making.

## Every dimension meets the facts directly

In a star, a dimension joins the fact table and nothing else. `Products` does not join `Customers`,
and the calendar joins neither. That is what keeps a model predictable: every filter a pivot table
applies starts at a dimension and travels one step, into `Sales`.

The alternative is a dimension that points at another dimension. If `Origin` were a code pointing at
an `Origins` table with the region's altitude and harvest months, the model would be a **snowflake**,
and a filter on altitude would have to travel two steps to reach a sale. Excel handles that, and
each extra step is one more relationship to get right. Three region names do not need it, so
`Products` keeps `Origin` as plain text.

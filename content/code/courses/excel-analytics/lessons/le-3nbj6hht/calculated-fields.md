title: A calculated field works on totals

**A calculated field is a formula the pivot table applies to the totals of other fields, never to a
row.** That one sentence decides whether its answer is right. A ratio of two totals, such as revenue
per bag, comes out right in every cell. A product of two totals, such as price times bags, comes out
wrong in every cell, and nothing on the screen marks it.

## A pivot table to work on

Click any cell of the table `Sales`, choose **Insert › PivotTable**, and put it on a **New
Worksheet**, which places it at A3, as in lesson 10. Rename the sheet `Prices`. Drag `Product` to
**Rows**, then `Revenue` and `Bags` to **Values**. Each of the six products now has a `Sum of
Revenue` and a `Sum of Bags`.

## Revenue per bag, inside the pivot

What did a bag of each product actually sell for? Not the list price: wholesale customers pay less
than list, and every sale before 2026 was made at the old prices. The honest answer is the revenue a
product brought in divided by the bags of it that were sold.

1. Click a cell of the pivot table.
2. Choose **PivotTable Analyze › Fields, Items, & Sets › Calculated Field**. In Excel 2016 and 2019
   the tab is called **Analyze**.
3. In **Name**, type `Price per bag`.
4. In **Formula**, replace the `0` with the formula below. Double-clicking a field in the list
   types its name for you.
5. Click **Add**, then **OK**.

```localised
=Revenue/Bags
```

A third column appears, headed `Sum of Price per bag`, and the heading is misleading: nothing was
summed. In each row the pivot took that row's sum of revenue and divided it by that row's sum of
bags, and in the grand total it divided the grand totals. Give it two decimals with **Value Field
Settings › Number Format**.

Now drag `Price` into **Values** as well and change it to **Average** in **Value Field Settings**,
as lesson 10 did with other fields. The two columns look like two names for the same thing:

| `Product` | `Sum of Revenue` | `Sum of Bags` | `Price per bag` | `Average of Price` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `CER1K` | 21,356 | 200 | 106.78 | 110.11 |
| `CER250` | 837 | 24 | 34.88 | 35.09 |
| `DEC250` | 3,622 | 88 | 41.16 | 42.36 |
| `MOG250` | 3,354 | 69 | 48.61 | 50.77 |
| `SUL1K` | 21,082 | 178 | 118.44 | 121.00 |
| `SUL250` | 1,243 | 32 | 38.84 | 38.92 |
| Grand Total | 51,494 | 591 | 87.13 | 75.07 |

They are not the same thing. `Average of Price` averages the prices of the sales, and every sale
counts once whatever its size. `Price per bag` counts every bag once. Sale S1002 sold 17 bags of
`SUL1K` at R$ 116: it is one price among 21 in the average, and 17 of the 178 bags in the price per
bag. Wholesale orders are large and cheap, so they pull the price per bag down, to R$ 118.44 against
an average price of R$ 121.00. In the grand total the gap is wider, R$ 87.13 against R$ 75.07,
because the average of prices also mixes 250 g bags and 1 kg bags one sale at a time.

Which one is right depends on the question. "What does a typical sale charge" is the average.
"What did a bag bring in", the question behind any revenue forecast, is the price per bag. The
formulas of lesson 5 give both, and agree with the pivot:

```localised
=SUMIFS(Sales[Revenue], Sales[Product], "SUL1K")/SUMIFS(Sales[Bags], Sales[Product], "SUL1K")
=AVERAGEIFS(Sales[Price], Sales[Product], "SUL1K")
```

The first answers 118.4382, which the pivot shows as 118.44, and the second 121.

## The same rule, giving nonsense

Suppose the table had no `Revenue` column and somebody built one in the pivot instead: a calculated
field called `Revenue 2`, with this formula.

```localised
=Price*Bags
```

Excel accepts it, and for `CER250` it shows **9,264**. The product had 11 sales; the sum of their
prices is 386 and the sum of their bags is 24, and 386 × 24 is 9,264. The real revenue of `CER250`
is 837, and these two formulas confirm both halves of the mistake:

```localised
=SUMIFS(Sales[Price], Sales[Product], "CER250")
=SUMIFS(Sales[Bags], Sales[Product], "CER250")
=SUMIFS(Sales[Revenue], Sales[Product], "CER250")
```

The grand total of `Revenue 2` is **4,791,828**, more than ninety times the real 51,494. Every price
was multiplied by every bag, when each price should have met only the bags of its own sale.

## Which formulas belong in a calculated field

Read the formula aloud with the word *total* in front of every field. "Total revenue divided by
total bags" is a real quantity, so the formula belongs in a calculated field. "Total price times
total bags" is not, because a total of prices measures nothing. A formula that fails the test has to
be worked out row by row first, and **a row-by-row formula belongs in a column of the table**, as
`Revenue` did in lesson 2; the pivot then sums the column.

The same goes for a function inside a calculated field. `=IF(Bags>10, …)` does not ask whether a
sale was over ten bags. It asks whether the total in that cell is, which in the grand total row is
591.

Delete `Revenue 2` before going on: **Fields, Items, & Sets › Calculated Field**, choose it in the
**Name** list, and click **Delete**. Keep `Price per bag`. A calculated field is stored in the pivot
cache, the copy of the rows that section 06 describes, so every pivot table sharing that cache offers
it too.

A pivot table built on the data model of lesson 15 has no calculated fields at all. The measures of
lesson 16 take over the job there, and they make you say whether a formula works row by row or on
the totals.

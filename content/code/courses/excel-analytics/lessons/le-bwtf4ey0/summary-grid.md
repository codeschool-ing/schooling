**The report of lesson 1, products down the side and months across the top, is one `SUMIFS`
copied into every cell.** Each cell asks the same question, *how many bags of this product in this
month*, and only the product and the month change. Put the products in a column and the months in
a row, let the formula read both with mixed references from lesson 2, and one formula fills the
grid.

## Building it

1. Add a sheet called `Report`.
2. In **A2** type `Product`, and in **A3** to **A8** the six codes in the order of the lesson 1
   figure: `SUL250`, `CER250`, `MOG250`, `DEC250`, `SUL1K`, `CER1K`.
3. In **B2**, **C2** and **D2** type the first day of each month: `2025-01-01`, `2025-02-01` and
   `2025-03-01`. Each is a real date, so the formula can compare with it.
4. In **B3** type the formula below, then fill it right to D3 and down to row 8.

```localised
=SUMIFS(Sales!$E$2:$E$109, Sales!$D$2:$D$109, $A3, Sales!$B$2:$B$109, ">="&B$2, Sales!$B$2:$B$109, "<"&EDATE(B$2, 1))
```

It is long, and every piece is something you have met. It adds `Bags` where the product equals the
code in column A and the date falls from the month's first day to just before the next month's.
`EDATE(B$2, 1)` is the date one month after B2, and lesson 6 takes the date functions further.

- `Sales!$E$2:$E$109` and the other ranges are **absolute**: every cell reads the same columns of
  the same 108 rows.
- `$A3` locks the **column** and leaves the row free. Filled right, it stays on column A, where the
  codes are; filled down, it becomes `$A4`, `$A5`, the next product.
- `B$2` locks the **row** and leaves the column free. Filled down, it stays on row 2, where the
  months are; filled right, it becomes `C$2`, the next month.

Get one dollar sign wrong and the grid still fills with numbers, which is the danger. With `A3`
instead of `$A3`, the cell in column C reads B3, a number of bags, as if it were a product code, and
finds nothing.

## Totals outside the grid, and the check

Type `Total` in **E2** and in **A9**. In **E3** put `=SUM(B3:D3)` and fill it down to E8; in **B9**
put `=SUM(B3:B8)` and fill it right to E9. The grand total in E9 answers **116**.

Now ask the same question with one formula that knows nothing about the grid:

```localised
=SUMIFS(Sales!E2:E109, Sales!B2:B109, ">="&DATE(2025,1,1), Sales!B2:B109, "<"&DATE(2025,4,1))
```

It also answers **116**. A grid whose total does not match the single formula has a cell asking the
wrong question, usually a reference that moved when it should have stayed. This grid matches, and
every cell agrees with the report drawn in lesson 1: 2, 5 and 0 bags of `SUL250`, 19, 5 and 0 of
`CER1K`, down to the 116.

The totals sit in their own row and column, after the products, never between them. That is the
lesson 1 rule about subtotals among the data, kept: the grid is a report, built from the records,
and nothing will ever be added up across it by mistake.

## A new quarter is three new dates

Type `2026-01-01`, `2026-02-01` and `2026-03-01` over the three month headers. Every cell
recalculates, and E9 answers **117**, the first quarter of 2026. Nothing was retyped and nothing
was filtered: the question lives in the header cells and the formula reads it.

That is the whole difference between this grid and the one in lesson 1, which somebody typed. Lesson
10 builds the same report as a pivot table in under a minute, and the two will agree, which is one
more check that both are right.

No later lesson needs the formulas you typed in columns J and K of `Sales` and `Customers`, or the
`Report` sheet. Keep the sheet if you want to compare it with lesson 10's pivot table, and delete the
cells in J and K either way, so that nothing sits beside the data when lesson 7 turns it into a
table.

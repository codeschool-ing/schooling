**The most common spreadsheet in any company is a report that somebody keeps typing data into.** It
starts as a summary for a meeting, with a title, months across the top and a total at the bottom.
Then next month's numbers are added to it, and the next, and a year later it is the only place the
data exists, in a shape that no formula, pivot table or query can read.

The figure shows the same bags twice: as that report, and as the records you pasted.

Each numbered mark is a habit that makes a sheet pleasant to read and hard to analyse:

1. **A title merged across the columns.** Merged cells cannot be sorted, filtered or selected as a
   column, and Excel refuses to sort a range that contains some. The title belongs in the sheet's
   name or in a cell above a range that starts lower down, never inside the range.
2. **Months as columns.** "January" is a value of the date, and here it has become a field name. To
   total a quarter you add three columns; to total a year, twelve; and the day a thirteenth month
   arrives, every formula has to be widened. In the records, the month is in the `Date` column, and
   a new month is new rows that every formula already covers.
3. **A group label in place of a column.** `250 g bags` sits in a row of its own, so which group
   `MOG250` belongs to is said by *position*, by being below that label. Sort the sheet and the
   products leave their labels behind. A field that says `Grams` on every row, as `Products` does,
   survives any sort.
4. **Subtotals among the data.** A sum of the whole `Jan` column counts every bag twice, once in
   its row and once in its subtotal, and the grand total three times. Every formula then needs to
   know which rows to skip, and the next person to add a row does not.
5. **Blank rows as decoration.** Excel finds the edge of a range by looking for the first empty row
   or column. A blank row cuts the range in two, and a sort, a filter or a pivot table built from
   a cell in the top half sees only the top half.

Three more habits do not appear in the drawing and do the same damage. **Several values in one
cell**, such as `SUL1K x3; CER250 x2`, which no function can add without first taking the text
apart. **Units typed beside the number**, as in `14 bags`, which turns the number into text, the
failure of section 08. And **colour used as data**, a row painted yellow because it is unpaid: no
formula can count a colour, and the next person may not know what yellow meant.

## The report is an output

None of this means reports are wrong. The report on the left answers a question at a glance, and
the people who read it should get one. The rule is about **which one you keep**. Keep the records,
and build the report from them: lesson 5 builds this exact grid with formulas, lesson 10 with a
pivot table in a minute, and both rebuild it when the data changes. Keep only the report, and
every new question means retyping it.

When the report is all you have, it can be turned back into records. Lesson 14 does that with
Power Query, in a step called **unpivot**, on a budget that arrives with its months across the top.

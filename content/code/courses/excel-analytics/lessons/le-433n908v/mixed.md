**A mixed reference fixes half an address: the column and not the row, or the row and not the
column.** It sounds like a curiosity until you need a grid, a table with one thing down the side,
another across the top and a calculation in every crossing. Then it is the difference between
typing one formula and typing eighteen.

## A grid of discounted prices

Café Serra's wholesale customers pay less than list, and the owner wants to see what each product
would cost at three discounts: 5%, 10% and 15%. Six products down, three discounts across.

Do it on the `Products` sheet, to the right of the data, leaving column H empty so the grid stays
apart from the table. Type `5%` in **I1**, `10%` in **J1** and `15%` in **K1**. Excel reads each
one as a number, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.15, shown as a percentage. Then, in **I2**:

```localised
=ROUND($F2*(1-I$1),0)
```

I2 shows **39**: a bag of `SUL250` at R$ 41 with 5% off is R$ 38.95, and `ROUND` with 0 decimals
makes it R$ 39, because Café Serra prices in whole reais. Drag the fill handle of I2 right to K2,
and then, with I2:K2 still selected, drag the fill handle down to row 7. Eighteen cells, one
formula:

## Reading the two dollars

Each half of the formula needs a different thing to stay put:

- **`$F2`** fixes the column and lets the row move. Copied down, it becomes `$F3`, `$F4`: each row
  reads its own product's list price. Copied across, it stays `$F`: the 10% column and the 15%
  column still read the list price, not the column beside it.
- **`I$1`** fixes the row and lets the column move. Copied across, it becomes `J$1` and `K$1`:
  each column reads its own discount. Copied down, it stays on row 1, where the discounts are.

Click K7 to see both at work at the far corner: `=ROUND($F7*(1-K$1),0)`, the decaf's list price
and the 15% discount, which gives **38**.

A way to choose the dollars without reasoning about them every time: ask, of each address, **which
way it must not move**. The list price must not leave column F; the discount must not leave row 1.

## Without the dollars

Type the same formula with no dollars in I2, `=ROUND(F2*(1-I1),0)`, and copy it one row down. I3
then holds `=ROUND(F3*(1-I2),0)`: the price of `SUL1K`, times one minus the number in I2, which is
the 39 just computed. The cell shows **-5016**. A negative price of five thousand reais is at least
obviously wrong; most broken grids are not so considerate, and give numbers that are merely
plausible.

The 10% column is worth one more look. It gives **106** for `CER1K`, which is what `C04` paid per
bag in sale S1074, in January 2026. Wholesale prices in `Sales` sit about 10% under list, rounded,
but not every one lands on this grid: `SUL1K` comes out at 119 here, and the wholesale sales of 2026
paid 118. A grid like this is a model of a policy, and the sales are what happened.

No later lesson needs the grid. Clear I1:K7 when you have finished with it.

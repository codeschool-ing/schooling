---
title: Four octets, thirty-two bits
version: 2
---

An IPv4 address is one number, 32 bits long. The form everybody writes, `10.20.10.21`, is a
convenience for people: cut the 32 bits into four bytes, which networking calls **octets**, and
write each one in decimal with a dot between them. A packet's header carries the 32 bits and
nothing else. There are no dots on the wire.

The picture most people start with is four separate fields, like a country, a city, a street and a
house. It is wrong, and it causes trouble the first time a mask cuts through the middle of an
octet, which lesson 12 does on purpose. **Where the network part ends and the host part begins is
decided by the mask, not by the dots.** In this office the mask is `/24`, which happens to fall on
a dot, and that coincidence is why the four-fields picture survives so long.

This lesson runs on lesson 1's office, built with `sudo bash ~/netlab/netlab.sh up office`. `ipcalc`
prints an address in binary beside the decimal. On pc1, whose address is `10.20.10.21/24`:

```
ana@pc1:~$ ipcalc 10.20.10.21/24
Address:   10.20.10.21          00001010.00010100.00001010. 00010101
Netmask:   255.255.255.0 = 24   11111111.11111111.11111111. 00000000
Wildcard:  0.0.0.255            00000000.00000000.00000000. 11111111
=>
Network:   10.20.10.0/24        00001010.00010100.00001010. 00000000
HostMin:   10.20.10.1           00001010.00010100.00001010. 00000001
HostMax:   10.20.10.254         00001010.00010100.00001010. 11111110
Broadcast: 10.20.10.255         00001010.00010100.00001010. 11111111
Hosts/Net: 254                   Class A, Private Internet

```

Read the `Address` line from the right-hand column: `00001010.00010100.00001010.00010101`, which
is 10, 20, 10 and 21. The space ipcalc inserts before the last octet is not decoration. It sits
where the 24 ones of `Netmask` end, so everything to its left is the network part and everything
to its right is the host part.

**Each octet is eight bits, so each one runs from 0 to 255**, and the bits are worth 128, 64, 32,
16, 8, 4, 2 and 1 from left to right. 21 is 16 + 4 + 1, so its bits are `00010101`:

Converting by hand works in both directions. From binary to decimal, add the place values of the
ones. From decimal to binary, take the largest place value that fits, subtract it, and carry on with
what is left. For 200: 128 fits, leaving 72; 64 fits, leaving 8; 32 and 16 do not; 8 fits, leaving
nothing. So 200 is `11001000`. Lesson 12 asks for this often enough that it is worth doing slowly
now, with a pencil, until the place values come without thinking.

Four more lines of the output are worth naming. `Network` is the address with every host bit set to
0, `10.20.10.0`. `Broadcast` is the address with every host bit set to 1, `10.20.10.255`. **Neither of
the two can be given to a machine**, which is why `HostMin` is `.1`, `HostMax` is `.254` and
`Hosts/Net` says 254 rather than 256. `Wildcard` is the mask turned inside out, with ones where the
mask has zeros; some router configurations ask for it instead of the mask, and lesson 12 meets it
again. The words at the end of the last line, `Class A, Private Internet`, are the subject of the
next two sections.

**Thirty-two bits make 4,294,967,296 addresses**, a little over four billion. That was a generous
number when IPv4 was published as RFC 791 in September 1981, and it is the reason lesson 9 exists.

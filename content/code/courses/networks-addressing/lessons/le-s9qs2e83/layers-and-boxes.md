title: Each box is named by what it reads

In a shop, most network equipment looks the same: a metal or plastic case, a row of ports, a few
lights. The name on the box is not much help either, because the thing a provider installs at home
is sold as a "router" and is really four devices in one case. **A device is named by the deepest
header it reads before it decides what to do with a frame**, and that is the question this lesson
asks of each one.

The networks course gave you the layers. Here they become a way of telling equipment apart:

| device | what it reads | what it decides | layer |
|---|---|---|---|
| hub | nothing: it repeats the signal | nothing; every port gets everything | 1 |
| repeater | nothing: it regenerates the signal | nothing; it only extends the cable | 1 |
| modem | the signal on the provider's line | how to turn it into frames, and back | 1, with the line's own 2 |
| access point | the radio frame and its MAC addresses | which station a frame is for | 1 and 2 |
| switch | the destination MAC address | which port the frame leaves by | 2 |
| router | the destination IP address | which neighbour the packet goes to next | 3 |
| firewall | addresses, ports and the state of the connection | let the packet through or drop it | 3 and 4, sometimes 7 |
| load balancer | the address and port, or the whole HTTP request | which server gets the connection | 4, or 7 |

The repeater and the network card are lesson 2's, with the bridge and the gateway. This lesson
takes the rest of the table, and the column that matters most is the second one. A switch that
read IP addresses would be a router, and a router that read ports would be a firewall; the case
around them changes nothing.

**One case can hold several devices, and one device can be a program.** The router in this lesson's
lab is also its firewall, because both are rules on the same Linux machine, and the home box of
the modem section is four devices at once. When somebody says "the router", the useful question is
which of its jobs they mean.

## The lab

Every command in this lesson ran on the office below. It is one Linux computer: each machine in the
drawing is a network namespace with its own interfaces, addresses and routes, each cable is a pair
of virtual network cards, and the switch is the kernel's bridge. Lesson 7 takes that apart. Its
addresses are private ranges and blocks reserved for documentation, so nothing in it reaches the
real internet.

**A prompt that says `ana@pc1` ran on pc1, as an ordinary user; `root@r1` ran on the router, at a
root prompt**, because configuring a device needs it. pc1 is outlined in its own colour because most
sessions start there. The load balancer has a colour of its own too, because it is the one device
in the drawing that answers in somebody else's name: the address 192.0.2.80 is its own, and the pages behind it are not.

Two devices in the table are missing from the drawing. The lab has no radio and no telephone line,
so the access point and the modem were not run; their section says what they do and shows no
output. The hub is missing for a different reason: one is hard to buy today, and its section
makes the switch behave like one instead.

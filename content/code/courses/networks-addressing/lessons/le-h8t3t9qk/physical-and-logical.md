Five shapes have names, and the rest of this lesson takes them one at a time.

The common mistake is to think there is one drawing. There are two, and they can disagree.

The **physical topology** is where the cables run: which socket on the wall goes to which port in
the cupboard. It answers the question an electrician asks, and the one you ask when a single
machine goes dark — *which cable is this?*

The **logical topology** is how the traffic actually travels: who receives a frame that somebody
sends, and which way a packet goes to reach the far side. **The logical drawing decides who sees
whose traffic and what a failure does to the path**, and it is not always the shape of the cables.

Two examples make the difference concrete.

- **A hub wired as a star behaves as a bus.** Every machine has its own cable to the hub, so the
  physical drawing is a star. But a hub repeats every bit it receives out of every other port, so
  every machine hears every frame, exactly as if they all hung off one shared cable. Lesson 1
  imitated a hub in the lab and watched pc3 receive frames that were not addressed to it. A switch
  on the same cables makes the logical drawing a star as well, because it sends a frame only to the
  port where its destination lives (lesson 18).
- **Token Ring was wired as a star and ran as a ring.** Each station had a cable to a central box,
  and inside that box the cables were joined so that the signal went from one station to the next
  and back to the first. The next section says why that arrangement was chosen.

The same split appears one layer up. Four routers can be cabled in a perfect ring and still send
most of their traffic along one side of it, because the routing protocol picked the shortest way. Cut a cable and the logical path changes, while the physical drawing has merely lost a line. The
ring and the mesh in this lesson show exactly that.

## Why a support person cares

The physical drawing is how you find the fault. The logical drawing is how you predict the damage.
When the finance office says "the network is slow since this morning", the physical map tells you
which closet and which switch; the logical map tells you whether the reception's PCs share that
switch's traffic, whether the backup link is now carrying everything, and who else is about to
call.

## The lab has no cables at all

Every machine in this course's lab is a **network namespace** on one Linux computer, and every
"cable" is a pair of virtual network interfaces joined in the kernel (lesson 7 builds it in
detail). So the lab has no physical topology to photograph. What it does have is the drawing in
`lab.sh`, and the kernel honours it: a namespace only reaches what its virtual cables reach. When
a section below "cuts a cable", it sets one end of a virtual cable down, and the other end sees the
signal disappear exactly as it would if somebody pulled the plug.

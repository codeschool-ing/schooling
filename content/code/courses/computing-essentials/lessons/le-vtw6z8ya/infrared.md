The odd one out. Infrared is not a radio at all — it is light, just below the colour red and
outside what the eye can see. Everything different about it follows from that one fact.

**Light does not go through walls and does not bend around corners.** So an infrared link needs
the two ends to see each other, and a remote control works by bouncing off the ceiling when you
are not aiming properly.

You can prove all of this in ten seconds: point a remote at a phone camera and press a button.
The camera's sensor sees infrared and the screen shows a flashing violet light that your eyes
cannot. **That is also the best way to test a remote** — if the light flashes, the remote is
fine and the problem is the television.

## How a remote actually works

The diode flashes on and off very fast, at about `38 kHz`, and the pattern of long and short
flashes is a number. The receiver ignores everything not flickering at that rate, which is how it
tells a remote apart from a lamp or the sun.

It is **one-way**. The remote never learns whether the television heard it, which is why pressing
the volume key twice sometimes moves it once and nothing anywhere reports an error.

## Why it lost, and where it stayed

Infrared moved data between laptops and phones for about ten years, and Bluetooth ended it in
one argument: **you had to line the two devices up and keep them still.** A radio in a pocket
beats a light beam on a desk every time.

Where it remains, it remains because it is almost free and good enough:

- **remote controls**, in essentially every television and air conditioner ever made;
- **presence sensors** — the kind that turns a corridor light on is reading body heat, which is
  infrared arriving rather than being sent;
- **touchless taps and dryers**, which send a beam and watch for a reflection;
- **night vision**, where a camera lights a scene with infrared that people cannot see.

## The one thing worth carrying away

**An infrared remote and an infrared sensor are both cheap because the link is stupid.** There is
no pairing, no address, no acknowledgement, no security at all — any remote can control any
television that speaks its codes, and that is why universal remotes exist and work.

It is a fair trade for a device that has to cost a dollar and run for four years on two cells,
and it is the clearest example in this lesson of the rule from the first section: **something at
the far corner of the range-speed-power triangle gives up everything else to get there.**

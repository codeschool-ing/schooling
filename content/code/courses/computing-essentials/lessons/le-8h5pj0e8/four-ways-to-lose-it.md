title: Four ways to lose it, and they are four different problems
version: 1

"Backing up" sounds like one thing and it is four. Data is lost in four distinct ways, and an
arrangement that handles three of them still loses everything on the day the fourth happens.

1. **You delete it.** Or overwrite it, or save over the good version with a bad one. This is by
   far the most common and it is the only one where the machine is working perfectly.
2. **The hardware fails.** A drive stops. An SSD's controller dies, which is the sudden kind —
   it works and then it is not there, with no warning noise.
3. **The machine goes.** Theft, fire, a flood, a drink. Everything in the room is gone at once,
   including the external drive on the desk beside it.
4. **Something changes the files while you still have them.** Ransomware encrypts them,
   a failing drive corrupts them quietly, a bad sync overwrites the good copy with an empty one.

## The sentence this lesson exists for

**Synchronising is not backing up.**

A sync service — Drive, OneDrive, iCloud, Dropbox — keeps two places identical. That is what it
is for and it is genuinely useful. It also means that **a deletion is copied**, and **a file
encrypted a second ago is copied**, and both within seconds, to every device you own.

It answers the second and third rows of that grid perfectly and the first and fourth barely.
Which is unfortunate, because the first and fourth are the ones that happen.

What partly rescues it is **version history**: most services keep previous versions and deleted
files for somewhere between thirty days and a year. That is a real safety net and it is a
different feature from the syncing, it has to be found and used deliberately, and it is not
always on for every plan.

## What a backup actually is

A backup is **a copy that does not change when the original does.** That one property is what
separates it from every kind of mirror, sync and duplicate, and it is what makes rows one and
four survivable.

Everything in the rest of this lesson is a way of making that copy cheap enough that you actually
keep making it.

## And the failure that is not on the list

**A backup nobody has ever restored from is a hypothesis.** Backup software that has been running
for two years to a drive whose filesystem is broken produces a green tick every night and nothing
at the end of it.

Restore one file. Not the whole machine — one file, from last month, to the desktop, and open it.
It takes four minutes and it converts the hypothesis into a fact. Do it when you set the backup
up and once a year after that.

---
title: What actually helps, and what is sold instead
version: 1
---

A slow home network is a large market, and most of what is sold into it addresses the wrong half
of the problem. This section is the short list, in order of how much it usually buys.

## Move the box, or run a cable

**Free, and it beats almost everything below.** The section on placement is the whole of the
advice; a router on a shelf in the middle of the flat instead of in the cupboard by the door
often ends the conversation.

**A cable ends it completely.** Ethernet to the television, the desktop, the games console —
anything that does not move — takes those devices off the radio entirely, which speeds up both
them and everything still on it. Flat cable runs under a carpet or along a skirting board; the
cable itself costs almost nothing.

This is the recommendation people least want to hear and the one that works.

## Mesh, which is the real answer for a large home

A **mesh** is two or three units that carry one network between them: same name, same password,
and a device moving through the house is handed from one to the next without dropping anything.

It is the right purchase when the house is genuinely too big or too solid for one box — several
floors, thick internal walls, a long flat. It is not the right purchase when the router is in a
cupboard by the door, because the cheapest unit of a mesh is a longer cable.

## A repeater, and the thing nobody mentions

A **repeater** (or *extender*) receives the Wi-Fi and transmits it again. It is cheap and it
works, with one cost that is almost never printed on the box: a simple repeater **hears and
speaks on the same radio**, so it can only do one at a time. Everything through it runs at about
half the speed it received.

Half of a good signal in a room that had none is a real improvement. Half of a weak signal is a
weak signal, which is why a repeater placed *where the Wi-Fi is bad* — the intuitive place — is a
repeater placed in the worst possible spot. It goes **halfway**, where the signal is still good.

## Powerline, which is either excellent or useless

A **powerline** adapter sends the network over the electrical wiring: one unit by the router, one
in the far room. When the two sockets are on the same circuit it is superb. When they are on
different circuits, or the wiring is old, or an extension lead is involved, it can be worse than
the Wi-Fi it replaced — and there is no way to know which without trying it.

Buy it from somewhere that takes returns.

## Things that do not help

- **A "booster" app on a phone.** There is no such thing. An app cannot change a radio's
  transmit power or the building's walls.
- **Antennas from the internet.** A bigger antenna on the router does not help the phone, and the
  connection is only as good as its weaker end.
- **Changing DNS to make the internet faster.** It changes how long a *name* takes to resolve —
  tens of milliseconds, once — and nothing about how fast anything downloads. It is worth doing
  for other reasons, and speed is not one of them.
- **A faster plan, while the Wi-Fi is the bottleneck.** This is the expensive version of not
  measuring: a hundred megabits arriving at a router that delivers twenty into the bedroom
  becomes three hundred arriving at a router that delivers twenty into the bedroom.

## The sentence to keep

**Measure where it is slow, then measure next to the router.** Everything above is a decision
that follows from which of the two numbers is the small one, and every purchase made without them
is a guess with a receipt.

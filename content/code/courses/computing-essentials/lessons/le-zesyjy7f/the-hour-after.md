title: The hour after, in the order that helps
version: 1
---

Something has already happened: a password was typed into the wrong page, a code was read out, a
transfer was made, somebody was given remote access. This section is the order, because in the
moment everybody does the same three things in the wrong sequence and loses the one that was
time-critical.

## The order

1. **Change the password — from a device you trust.** If somebody was given remote access, that is
   not this machine. A phone is fine.
2. **Sign out everywhere.** Every account has a list of active sessions and a *sign out of all
   devices*, and without it a changed password can leave the attacker signed in where they already
   were.
3. **Check what was changed, not only what was read.** This is the step people miss and it is the
   one that decides whether it is over: the recovery e-mail address, the recovery phone number,
   the forwarding rules on the mailbox, and any second factor that was added. **An attacker who
   leaves a forwarding rule behind keeps reading your mail after you have changed everything.**
4. **Tell the bank, if money or a card is involved**, by the number on the card. Instant transfers
   have a fraud process and it is worth starting within the hour; card payments can be disputed.
5. **Then the same password everywhere else it was used**, which is the moment everybody discovers
   exactly how many places that was.

## If the machine was given to somebody

Remote access means everything typed since was seen and anything could have been installed. The
honest answer for an important machine is to reinstall the system, and the practical middle is:
change passwords from a different device first, run a full scan, and treat that machine as
untrusted until it has been reinstalled.

**The bank's password changes from the phone, today, not from that machine.**

## Tell somebody

Two reasons, and neither is comfort. **The same message is going to everybody in the contact
list**, and saying so early stops the second victim. And where a business or a family shares
anything, the account that was taken is a route into the rest.

The shame is the mechanism, not an accident. Scams are built to make the victim feel stupid,
because a person who feels stupid does not tell anybody for three days, and three days is the
whole of what the attacker needs.

## And afterwards, the two questions worth asking

**What would have stopped it?** Usually one of the four things in this lesson, and usually the
second factor.

**What else uses that password?** The answer is the work, and it is the last time it will be this
much work, because the answer to *why was it used in so many places* is the section this lesson
opened with.

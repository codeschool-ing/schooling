Scams are not clever and they do not need to be. They work on attention, not on ignorance, and
the people who fall for them are not the people you expect — they are people who were busy,
worried, or expecting something similar at that exact moment.

Which is the useful news: **you do not have to recognise the content. You have to recognise the
shape.**

## The three parts

**Urgency.** Something must be done now: your account will be closed, the delivery returns today,
the payment fails at midnight. Urgency exists to remove the step where you check, and nothing
legitimate is ever damaged by being verified twenty minutes later.

**Authority.** It is the bank, the tax office, the courier, the boss, the platform. The name is
easy to put on a message and impossible to verify from inside the message itself.

**A channel you did not choose.** They contacted you. That is the part worth building a habit
around, because it inverts cleanly: *your bank calling you* and *you calling your bank* are
different situations, and only one of them can be faked.

Any two of those in one message is a reason to slow down. All three is the shape.

## What they ask for, which is always one of four

- **A code**, which is the second factor from two sections ago. **Nobody legitimate ever asks for
  it** — not the bank, not support, not the platform. It is the whole point of the code.
- **A password**, ditto.
- **A payment**, made now, by a method that cannot be reversed.
- **Remote access to the machine**, so they can *fix* something. This is the one that ends worst,
  because it skips every other defence.

## The one habit that covers all of it

**Hang up and call back on a number you found yourself.**

Not the number in the message, not the number the caller reads out, not the link. The number on
the back of the card, or from the app you already have installed.

This costs two minutes and it defeats the entire category, because the only thing a scam cannot
survive is the person leaving the conversation it controls.

## The tells, for when you want them

They are real and they are secondary, because a good imitation has none of them:

- an address that is nearly right — a letter swapped, a word added, a different ending;
- a greeting with no name, or your e-mail address used as one;
- a link whose visible text and actual destination differ, which a long press or a hover reveals;
- a request to move the conversation to another app;
- grammar or formatting slightly wrong — and note that this one has become unreliable, because
  writing a clean message in any language is no longer difficult.

## And the one that is not a message at all

**A page reached from a search result.** Somebody searches for their bank, or for a support
number, and clicks a paid result that leads to a copy. The address is the only thing that
distinguishes them, and the habit that defeats it is the same as above: reach a bank through its
app or through an address you typed.

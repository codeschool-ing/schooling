Here is a line off a shop page. Nothing in it is invented:

```
Intel Core i5-13400 · 16 GB DDR4-3200 · 512 GB NVMe SSD · Intel UHD 730
```

Four items, and by now you can place all of them.

| what it says | which part | what it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| `Intel Core i5-13400` | processor | mainstream tier, 13th generation — current enough |
| `16 GB DDR4-3200` | memory | the sensible default; `DDR4` is the generation, `3200` its speed |
| `512 GB NVMe SSD` | storage | solid state, on the fast connection. The important word is **SSD** |
| `Intel UHD 730` | graphics | built into the processor, not a separate card — next lesson's subject |

## What the spec does not tell you, and how to notice

**A missing word is information.** If a listing says `1 TB HDD` where another says `512 GB SSD`,
the first is offering four times the space and the four-month wait. Cheap machines are still
sold this way, and the capacity number is doing the persuading.

**A big number in the wrong place is the commonest trap.** `32 GB RAM · 1 TB HDD` on a budget
machine is a bad arrangement: twice the memory you need, on a machine whose every read takes ten
milliseconds. `16 GB RAM · 512 GB SSD` at the same price will feel like a different category of
computer.

**"Up to" means the one they did not send you.** `Up to 4.6 GHz` is the boost figure, reached
briefly and on one core.

## Three questions, in order

When somebody hands you a specification and asks whether it is any good, ask these, in this
order, and you will be right far more often than the price tag is:

1. **Is the storage an SSD?** If not, nothing else in the list can rescue it.
2. **Is there at least 16 GB of memory?** Below that you are buying the swapping section.
3. **Is the processor within about four generations?** The tier matters less than the age.

Everything else — the exact clock speed, the brand of the SSD, DDR4 against DDR5 — is a
refinement on a machine that already answers yes three times.

## And the honest caveat

This is a lesson about a desktop computer, and the same four parts are in a phone, a laptop and
a server. What changes is what you can replace afterwards: a desktop's memory and storage are
usually a screwdriver away, a laptop's sometimes are, and a phone's never are.

Which is the one place where **getting the specification right at purchase actually matters**,
rather than being something you can fix later. On the machines you cannot open, the three
questions above are the only chance you get.

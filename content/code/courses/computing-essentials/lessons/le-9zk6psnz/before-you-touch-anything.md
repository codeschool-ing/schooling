Assembling a computer is closer to assembling furniture than to surgery. The parts are robust,
every connector is keyed so it only goes in one way, and the whole job is an afternoon.

Two things do cause real damage, and neither is the one people worry about most.

## Force is the one that breaks things

**Nothing in a computer needs force.** A memory module clicks in with firm, even pressure on both
ends. A processor **drops into its socket under its own weight** — if you are pushing it, it is
the wrong way round and you are about to bend the pins. A cable that will not go in is the wrong
cable or the wrong way up.

If something resists, stop and look at it. That sentence prevents more damage than every other
piece of advice in this lesson put together.

## Static, honestly

Static electricity can damage a chip, and the risk is smaller than the internet suggests and
larger than zero. What is true:

- The damage is usually **latent** — the part works and fails months later, which is why nobody
  ever sees it happen and everybody has a story about never bothering.
- The risk is highest in a dry room on a carpet in winter, and low on a tiled floor in a humid
  summer.
- **Touching the bare metal of the case before you start, and again whenever you have walked
  across the room, is most of the protection available.** A wrist strap is better and costs
  little.

What is not true: that a spark is required. The discharge that kills a chip is below what you can
feel.

## The workspace

- **A large table**, not the floor and not a bed. Carpet generates static and swallows screws.
- **Good light**, because the front panel connectors are labelled in letters one millimetre high.
- **A magnetic tray or a bowl** for screws. A standard build has about twenty and they are not
  interchangeable.
- **The motherboard box** as a work surface for the board itself. It is the right size, it is
  non-conductive, and it is the surface for the first power-on.
- **A cross-head screwdriver**, magnetic if possible. That is the whole toolkit.

## Read two pages before you start

The motherboard manual has one diagram worth finding: **the board layout with every connector
numbered.** It tells you which of the four memory slots to use with two modules — almost never
the two nearest the processor — and where the front panel pins are.

The case manual has the other: which screws are which, and whether the power supply mounts fan up
or fan down.

Five minutes each, and they answer the two questions that otherwise cost an hour.

## The parts that are actually fragile

| | how it breaks |
|---|---|
| **processor pins** | bent by putting it in at an angle, or by dropping it. Sometimes straightenable |
| **the socket pins** | on modern boards the pins are in the socket, so a dropped screw is a dead board |
| **the M.2 screw** | tiny, easy to lose, and the drive will not sit without it |
| **front panel pins** | bent by pushing a connector on crooked |

Everything else on the list is sturdier than it looks.

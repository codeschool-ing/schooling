---
title: Knowing when to stop
version: 1
---

Diagnosis has an end that is not *fixed*, and recognising it is a skill of its own. Three
endings are honest and one is not.

## Stop when the next step costs more than the thing

A ten-year-old machine that needs a new board is a machine to replace. An hour of somebody's time
is worth more than most single components.

This is a real calculation and it wants doing out loud, because the sunk hours push hard the
other way: the longer you have been at it, the more it feels like giving up rather than deciding.

## Stop when the next step could lose something

**Anything that risks the data is where a home investigation stops** and a backup starts. A disk
that is making noises will survive being copied from once, and may not survive being tested for
an afternoon.

The order is: copy what matters off first, then experiment. That order is the only part of this
lesson that cannot be got back if it is taken in the other one.

## Stop when it is somebody else's half

The previous lesson drew this line for a network: the line light is red and nothing inside the
house will change it. Every subject has one. The point of stopping there is not politeness — it
is that continuing means changing things on your side that were never wrong, and those changes
stay.

## And the ending that is not honest

**It works now and I do not know why.** Sometimes that is where you are, and saying so is fine.
What is not fine is recording it as fixed, because the same fault will return and the notes will
say it was dealt with.

Write *unexplained* instead. That word costs nothing now and is worth an hour next time.

## What to tell somebody who can help

Whether it is a support line, a shop or a person in the family who knows more, what you send
decides how much of their time is spent re-doing yours:

- **the symptom**, in the one-sentence form from the first section;
- **when it started**, and what changed around then;
- **what you have already tested**, and what each test showed — especially the tests that passed;
- **the exact message**, photographed;
- **what you have not tried**, and why.

That last line is the one nobody writes and the one that saves the most, because otherwise the
first thing anybody does is ask you to try what you already did.

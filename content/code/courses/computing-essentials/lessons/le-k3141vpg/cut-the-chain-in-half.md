title: Cut the chain in half
version: 1

Anything that does not work is a chain of things that had to work. The instinct is to start at
one end and check each link. The method is to **cut the chain in half, decide which side the
fault is on, and do it again**.

## Why halving is not merely tidier

Eight links checked one at a time is up to eight tests. Halved, it is three. Sixteen links is
four. A thousand is ten.

That difference matters less in a home than the other consequence: **each test rules out a whole
half whether it passes or fails**, so there is no such thing as a wasted test. Checking links one
by one, a test that passes tells you almost nothing.

## Finding the middle of a chain you cannot see

The hard part is never the halving; it is knowing what the chain is. Three that cover most of it:

**A document will not print.** The chain runs: the application, the document, the print queue, the
driver, the connection, the printer. The middle is the queue — print from a different application,
or print a test page from the printer's own menu. One test, and half the chain is gone.

**A page will not load.** The application, the name lookup, the network, the line, the site. The
middle is *does another site load*, which is the previous lesson's first question and the same
move.

**A machine will not start.** Power at the socket, the lead, the supply, the board, the display.
The middle is *does anything at all light up or spin* — which separates two halves that have
nothing to do with each other.

## The middle is wherever a test is cheapest

The mathematical middle is not always the right place to cut. A test that takes two seconds and
lands a third of the way along beats one that takes an hour and lands exactly in the centre.

**So cut where you can test.** A cable you can unplug, a second machine already switched on, a
menu you can open — those are the natural joints, and a chain is best cut at a joint.

## What this replaces

It replaces the thing everybody does, which is to fix the most likely cause first. That is not
wrong, exactly — it is fast when you are right — but it has no structure, so being wrong three
times in a row leaves you exactly where you started with an hour gone.

Halving cannot leave you where you started. Every test moves the boundary, and the boundary is
the only thing you have to carry in your head.

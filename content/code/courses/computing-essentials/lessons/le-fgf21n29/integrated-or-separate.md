---
title: Integrated or separate, which is the only buying question that matters
version: 1
---

Most computers sold today have **no graphics card**. The graphics processor is built into the
same chip as the CPU — that is what `Intel UHD 730` or `Radeon Graphics` means on a
specification — and it borrows a slice of the machine's main memory instead of having its own.

For an enormous number of people that is not a compromise. It is simply the right answer.

## What integrated graphics does perfectly well

Everything that is not a game or a render:

- office documents, e-mail, spreadsheets of any size;
- several 4K monitors at once;
- video calls, and playing video of any resolution — modern chips decode it in dedicated
  hardware, so a 4K film costs the processor almost nothing;
- photo editing, and light video editing.

## What it cannot do

Games above modest settings, 3D modelling, serious video work, and anything that trains a model.
Not slowly — it is between five and thirty times behind, depending on the work.

## The question, and the honest order to ask it in

**Do you run something from the second list?** If you cannot name one, a separate card is money
spent on a machine that will be no faster at anything you do, and it will cost you twice more:

- the card costs money;
- the card needs a larger power supply, which costs money;
- the card makes heat, which needs cooling, which makes noise.

That chain is the reason this lesson puts these four together. **A graphics card is not one
decision.** It is the decision that sets the other three.

> **The one exception worth naming.** A machine with two or three large monitors and nothing
> demanding on them is still fine on integrated graphics; the number of screens is a connector
> question and almost never a performance one. People buy cards for this and rarely need to.

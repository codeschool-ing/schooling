**A retention curve is a cohort's row drawn as a line**: the share of the group still active at each
age, starting at 100 per cent and falling. Its value is in comparison. One curve says little; two
curves that part say that two groups of customers behave differently, and the age at which they part
says when.

**By channel, the curves part early and keep parting.** A year after signing up, 43.8 per cent of the
customers who came through a referral were still ordering, 30.5 per cent of those from search and
10.3 per cent of those from social media. In month 1 the three were within about seven points of each
other; by month 12 referral customers are four times as likely to be active as social ones. A team
that judged its channels by sign-ups, or by first-month retention, would see three similar channels.

**In time, the 2025 cohorts hold on better**, from 74.4 against 70.9 per cent at month 3 to 56.9
against 49.8 at month 6, while month 1 is the same. The gap opening after the first month points at
what changed: something that keeps customers in their second and third months, not something that
makes them place a second order. In Panela's data it is a better onboarding from January 2025, which
`panela.py` writes as a lower monthly chance of leaving.

## Reading a curve's shape

- **A steep early drop and then a flat tail** is the common shape: many customers try the product
  once and leave, and those who stay become regulars. The level of the tail is the loyal base.
- **A curve that never flattens** means even long-standing customers leave at a steady rate, which is
  a product problem rather than an onboarding one. Panela's curves are of this kind, and for a
  reason worth knowing: `panela.py` gives every customer the same monthly chance of leaving however
  long they have stayed, a simplification that real businesses rarely show.
- **Curves that rise** happen when customers come back after a gap, which is why a churn definition is
  needed before "lost" means anything. The next section writes one.

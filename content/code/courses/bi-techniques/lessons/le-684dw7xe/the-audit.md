The proposal on the table: customers with a churn risk **below 0.2** join a loyalty club with free
delivery. Under this decision, being flagged is no longer a gift, the offer of lesson 17; it is a door
that closes. **The error that hurts is the false alarm**: a customer who was going to stay, flagged as
a leaver and kept out of the club.

An audit computes, for each group, the error rates of the decision the model will drive. The program
reads the scores lesson 17 saved and breaks them down by region.

Read the false alarms column first, because it is the error the club turns into harm. **22.0 per cent
of the North's loyal customers are flagged as leavers, against 6.2 to 9.2 per cent elsewhere.** A loyal
customer in the North is more than twice as likely to be kept out of the club as a loyal customer in
the Southeast.

The margins matter, and they are the reason the program prints them. The North's rate rests on 100
customers, so its margin is about 8 points; the Southeast's rests on 1,594 and its margin is under 2.
Even at the bottom of its margin, 13.9 per cent, the North's rate sits above the top of the Southeast's,
10.3. **The difference is larger than the noise.** Recall is another matter: 58.8 per cent of 17
churners is ten people, and the North's recall cannot be told apart from anybody's.

Two more things the table shows.

- **The North churns less, 14.5 per cent against 15.6 to 16.7, and is flagged more**, 27.4 per cent
  against 17.4 to 18.5. A model that flags a group more often than that group leaves deserves a question
  before anything else.
- **The overall numbers hide all of it.** The North is 117 of 2,558 customers; its 22 false alarms
  barely move a total of 197.

An audit like this is cheap: one `groupby` on a file that already exists. **It should be run on every
group a decision could treat unfairly, before the decision is made**, and the groups to check come from
the decision, not from whatever columns happen to be in the file.

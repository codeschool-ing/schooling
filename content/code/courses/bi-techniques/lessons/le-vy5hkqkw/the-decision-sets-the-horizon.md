title: The decision sets the horizon

**The horizon of a forecast is how far ahead it reaches**, and it is not a property of the data.
It is set by the decision the forecast is for, and the first question to ask of any forecasting
request is what will be decided with it, and when.

At Panela three people ask for "the forecast" and mean three different things:

| who | decides | how far ahead | at what grain |
|---|---|---|---|
| the kitchen | how many boxes to pack and which ingredients to order | one to two weeks | days, by recipe |
| operations | how many temporary staff to hire for December | three to four months | weeks |
| finance | next year's budget | twelve to eighteen months | months, by region |

Each row changes the method. The kitchen needs the weekly rhythm and the holidays of the next
fortnight exactly right, and does not care about the trend. Finance needs the trend and nothing
about Mondays. A single "forecast" built for one of them is wrong for the others in a way nobody
notices, because each looks at a different part of it.

## Two rules that follow

**Forecast at the grain of the decision, or aggregate up to it.** Daily forecasts summed into a
month are noisier than a forecast made monthly, because each day's errors add up; monthly forecasts
split into days need a weekly pattern from somewhere. Forecasting weeks, as lessons 3 to 5 do, suits
operations, and it is a choice that was made rather than a default.

**A forecast is not a target.** The kitchen's forecast says what will probably happen. The sales
team's target says what they will try to make happen. Mixing them up is how a forecast is "revised
upwards" to please somebody and then used to order ingredients that rot. Keep them in different
columns with different names.

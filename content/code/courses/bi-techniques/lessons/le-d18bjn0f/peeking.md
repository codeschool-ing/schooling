The second way to multiply comparisons is in time. A test runs for three weeks, a dashboard shows its
p-value every morning, and somebody stops it the first morning the p-value dips below 0.05. Each of
those mornings is a comparison, and **a p-value wanders**: over 21 days it can cross the line and
come back several times, even when nothing is happening.

The cleanest way to see what that does is to run tests where nothing happens, many times:

**Read once at the planned end, 4.6% of the A/A tests declared a winner**, close to the 5 per cent
promised. **Read every day and stopped at the first crossing, 26.5% did**: more than a quarter of tests
of a change that did nothing would have been shipped as wins. Even a weekly look, three peeks in
total, raises it to 11.3%.

Peeking also biases the effect: a test stopped at its first crossing stops at a moment when chance
happened to push the difference up, so the reported lift is too large, the winner's curse of lesson
10 in another form.

## What to do instead

- **Fix the sample size in advance and read the test once**, at the end. Lesson 8 computed the
  sample; this is why it must be respected. Watching the dashboard for broken plumbing, an SRM or a
  guardrail is fine; deciding on the primary metric before the end is not.
- **If early stopping is genuinely needed**, for a dangerous treatment or an expensive test, use a
  method built for it. **Sequential tests** spend the 5 per cent across planned looks, demanding a
  much smaller p-value at early looks, so that the total risk stays at 5 per cent. Many experimentation
  platforms offer one; the important thing is that the number of looks and their thresholds are
  decided before the test.
- **Stop for harm, not for joy.** A guardrail that is clearly worse is a reason to stop at any time;
  a primary metric that looks good is not.

The interval becomes a decision when it is drawn against two lines: **zero**, and the **minimum
detectable effect** the plan chose in lesson 7, here +0.6 points. Where the interval sits relative to
both gives one of four readings.

| where the interval sits | reading | typical decision |
|---|---|---|
| entirely above the MDE | a real effect, and large enough to matter | ship |
| above zero, and reaching below the MDE | a real effect, of a size that may or may not matter | ship if it is cheap, or keep testing |
| straddling zero, and below the MDE | no effect worth having, and the test was big enough to say so | do not ship; the question is answered |
| straddling zero, and reaching above the MDE | the test cannot tell | inconclusive; it was too small for the effect, if any |

**Panela's three-week result is the second case**: above zero, reaching on both sides of 0.6. Its
weeks-2-and-3 result is the fourth: from −0.36 to +0.51, which includes zero and almost reaches the
MDE, so on its own it cannot say whether the lasting effect is zero, small, or nearly as large as
planned.

The third and fourth cases look alike and mean opposite things. **"No significant difference" is
not "no difference"**: whether it means "the effect is too small to matter" or "we do not know"
depends on whether the interval reaches the MDE. A test that ends in the fourth case was
underpowered for the question, which lesson 8 was supposed to prevent and sometimes cannot.

**Data about recent days arrives late.** An order placed on Tuesday may be paid on Wednesday, a
store may send its sales overnight, a correction may come a week after the fact. So the most recent
points of almost any business series are lower than they will finally be, and the drop looks
exactly like news.

Panela counts an order when it is paid. The file `snapshot.csv` is the series as a dashboard showed
it on the morning of 31 December 2025:

On the dashboard, the last day looks like a collapse: 324 orders against 559 that will finally be
counted, only 58 per cent of the day so far. The day before is at 86 per cent. Summed into the familiar "last
seven days against last year", **the dashboard reports a fall of 8.9 per cent when the real figure is 2.5 per cent**:
a story about a bad Christmas that the data never supported.

## Three rules

1. **Know your data's delay.** For each source, how many days until a day is, say, 99 per cent
   counted? Measure it the way the program above does, by comparing a saved snapshot with the final
   numbers. Panela's last three days are incomplete; another source may be thirty.
2. **Leave incomplete periods out of a model, or mark them.** A forecast fitted on a series whose
   last points are low will start from a level that is too low. Lesson 2 cut the last weekly total
   because 29 to 31 December 2025 were three days of a seven-day week; the same reasoning applies to
   days that are not fully counted.
3. **Never compare a partial period with a complete one.** "This month so far" against "all of last
   month" is the commonest form: it always falls. Compare the same number of days, counted the same
   way, or wait.

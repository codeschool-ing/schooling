Scores become segments through rules that somebody writes and can defend. A common shape crosses
recency with frequency, because those two say most of what matters: is this customer still here, and
are they a habit?

**The 1,087 champions are a quarter of the customers and 46.0 per cent of the year's spend.** They
ordered within the last few days and typically 27 times in the year. The obvious action for them is
none that costs a discount: they are already ordering, and the risk is annoying them.

**The 334 customers at risk are the most valuable target.** They ordered often, a median of 20
times in the year, and have been silent for a median of 158 days, well past the 42-day churn line
of lesson 13. They are customers who had a habit and lost it, and a win-back offer has something to
win back.

**"Lost" and "new or occasional" look alike on frequency and opposite on recency.** Both ordered about
four times in the year; one did so recently and might become a habit, the other long ago and probably
will not. Treating them the same, the commonest mistake of a frequency-only report, wastes the effort
on whichever group it suits less.

The rules are a choice. Somebody could cut at 3 instead of 4, or add monetary. **Whatever the rules,
they are written down beside the report**, so that "at risk" means the same thing next month and to
the next analyst.

ARIMA starts from a different question. Exponential smoothing asks what level, trend and season the
data has; ARIMA asks **how each value depends on the values and the errors just before it**. Its
three letters are three ingredients, and the numbers in `order=(p, d, q)` say how much of each.

- **AR, autoregressive, `p`**: this week is partly a multiple of the last `p` weeks. A busy week
  tends to be followed by a busy week.
- **I, integrated, `d`**: before modelling, subtract each value from the next, `d` times. A series
  that grows has no fixed average to return to, and its differences do. Making a series behave
  the same over time this way is called making it **stationary**, and ARIMA needs it.
- **MA, moving average, `q`**: this week is partly a multiple of the last `q` *forecast errors*.
  The name is old and confusing: it has nothing to do with the moving averages of lesson 2.

A **seasonal** ARIMA adds the same three ingredients at the seasonal lag: `seasonal_order=(0, 1, 0, 52)`
means "subtract the same week of last year", which removes the yearly season and most of the trend
in one step.

The constant, about 1,349 orders, is the average year-on-year growth of a week, and the AR and MA
terms adjust for the weeks just before. The forecasts are in the same range as Holt-Winters', and
they make the same Carnival mistake for the same reason: subtracting last year's week brings last
year's Carnival with it.

**Choosing `p`, `d` and `q` is the hard part of ARIMA**, and it is a craft: plots of how a series
correlates with its own past, tests for stationarity, and comparing fits. That craft is a course in
itself. What a BI analyst needs is to recognise an ARIMA model when a colleague presents one, know
what its three numbers mean, and ask the question of the last section of this lesson.

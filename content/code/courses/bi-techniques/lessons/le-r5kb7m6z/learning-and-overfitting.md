Every kind of learning shares one danger, and it is the one that makes a model look excellent in a
presentation and fail in use. **A model can learn its examples instead of the pattern behind them.**
This is called overfitting, and the only way to see it is to test on examples the model never saw.


The answer column reads: 43.3% of the customers known on 30 June ordered again in the next 90 days.
A **decision tree** predicts it by asking a sequence of questions, such as "has this customer ordered
in the last 21 days?", and the depth limits how many questions in a row it may ask.

**On the training customers, accuracy rises with every extra level of depth**, from 0.944 to 0.992: a
tree with no limit can keep asking questions until it has, in effect, memorised each customer. **On
the customers it never saw, accuracy falls**, from 0.944 to 0.912. The deep tree learnt the noise of
the training set: this customer with 37 orders and a recency of 12 days happened to leave, so every
customer who looks like that must leave. A new customer who looks like that does not.

Three habits follow, and they are the same ones lessons 3 to 5 taught for forecasts.

- **Always keep a test set the model never sees while learning**, and report the score on it, not on
  the training data. A training score is a description of the past.
- **Prefer the simpler model when the test scores are close.** Depth 1 and 2 score as well as
  anything here, and a tree of one question can be explained to anybody.
- **Compare with a baseline.** Predicting "leaves" for everyone would be right 56.7 per cent of the time, the
  share of customers who did not order again. A model's accuracy only means something next to that
  number, which lesson 17 takes further.

---
title: Making the actions happen
version: 1
---

A postmortem's actions are its only output that changes anything, and **they are the part most teams
lose.** The review is written, the meeting is held, everybody agrees, and three months later the same
incident happens with the same unfinished ticket attached to the old document.

Four habits keep actions from dying:

- **Every action has one owner and a date.** *The team will improve the tests* is a wish. *Carla adds a
  card-network error to the payments mock by the 20th* is a task somebody can be asked about.
- **Actions are few and ranked.** A postmortem with fifteen actions finishes none of them. Three that
  remove a missing defence are worth more than twelve that polish.
- **Actions live in the team's normal queue**, with the same priority rules as features. An action
  tracked only inside the postmortem document is invisible to the planning meeting that decides what
  gets done.
- **Somebody checks them.** A monthly look at open postmortem actions, in the same review where pages
  are counted, is what makes the difference between a team that learns and one that writes documents.

The error budget from lesson 15 is the argument when actions compete with features. A team whose budget
is spent has already agreed that reliability work comes first; the postmortem's actions are exactly
that work, written down with owners.

**Share the postmortem widely.** It is the cheapest training a company has: a real failure, explained
step by step, with what was done and why. Teams that read each other's postmortems learn from
incidents they never had. That only happens if the documents are blameless, because nobody publishes
a document that names them as the cause.

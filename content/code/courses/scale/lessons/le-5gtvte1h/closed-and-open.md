---
title: Closed and open
version: 1
---

Every load test in this course so far used `load.py`, and `load.py` is a **closed** model: a fixed
number of workers, each sending a request, waiting for the answer, and only then sending the next.
The rate is not chosen; it is whatever the workers achieve. When the box office slows down, the
workers slow down with it, and fewer requests arrive.

Real buyers do not behave like that. Ten thousand people opening the on-sale page at ten o'clock do
not wait for each other's answers. They arrive at a rate set by the world: by the time, by an email
going out, by a link shared on social media. That is an **open** model: requests arrive at a rate,
and the rate does not care how the system is doing.

The difference decides what a test can show:

- **A closed test cannot overload anything for long.** If the box office takes twice as long, the
  workers send half as many requests. Lesson 9's 128 workers waited a second each and never sent
  more than the box office could answer, which made it look busy rather than broken.
- **An open test can.** At 400 arrivals a second, a box office that answers 200 accumulates 200
  waiting requests every second, and the queue grows until something gives: memory, connections, a
  timeout, or a limit. That is what an on-sale does.

A closed model fits a fixed population that waits for answers: a batch job, a pool of workers, a
service called by one other service. For anything facing the public, the open model is the honest
one. The tool for it here is **k6**.

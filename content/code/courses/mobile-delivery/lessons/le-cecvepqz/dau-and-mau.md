**DAU and MAU count the distinct people active in a day and in the 30 days up to it; their ratio,
DAU/MAU, is called stickiness, and it is quoted far more often than it is understood.** The last line
of the transcript in section "Cohorts" computes it for 26 October 2026:

| | what it counts | the planner, on 26 October |
|---|---|---|
| **DAU**, daily active users | installs that opened the app that day | 59 |
| **WAU**, weekly active users | in the 7 days up to that day | 247 |
| **MAU**, monthly active users | in the 30 days up to that day | 652 |
| **DAU/MAU** | | 9% |
| **WAU/MAU** | | 38% |

## Why 9% is not a bad number here

DAU/MAU measures how many of the month's people come **every day**. For a messaging app that is
the point, and a ratio of 50% is common. For a study planner it is not: a person who studies three
evenings a week, every week, is a perfect user and counts in DAU on three days out of seven. **A
habit of a few days a week puts a ceiling on DAU/MAU that has nothing to do with how good the app
is.**

WAU/MAU asks the question that fits: of the people active this month, how many were active this
week? At 38%, more than a third of the month's people used the planner in the last seven days. That
is the ratio to watch from release to release, alongside the cohort table.

## The ratio is a summary, not a diagnosis

A DAU/MAU that rises can mean people come more often, or that MAU fell because people who came once
stopped coming at all. The ratio cannot tell them apart; the cohort table can. **Read the ratios as a
pulse, and the cohorts as the explanation.**

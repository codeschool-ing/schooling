Swimlanes look like an organisation chart turned on its side, so it is natural to read a pool as a
department and a lane as a smaller one inside it. **A pool is a participant, a party that runs its
own process and decides for itself; a lane is a role inside one participant's process.** The
difference is not one of size. Serra Alta's field team and its quality team are lanes, because one
process directs both. The farm and the bank are pools, because nothing Serra Alta draws can make
them do anything.

## Two kinds of line, two kinds of boundary

The rule that follows from that is short and strict:

- **A sequence flow never crosses a pool's boundary.** Sequence flow is order inside one process,
  and one process does not order another participant's work.
- **A message flow only ever connects two different pools.** Inside one pool, the parts of a process
  are joined by sequence flow, not by messages to themselves.
- Lanes are not boundaries. A sequence flow crosses from one lane to another as often as the work
  changes hands, and that is most of what lanes are for.

A diagram with more than one pool and the messages between them is a **collaboration diagram**. A pool can be drawn as a *white box*, with its process inside, or as a *black box*, an empty band
with a name, when you do not know or do not control what happens inside. Message flows then end on
the edge of the black-box pool rather than on any element in it.

## Joana's farm lane

Joana's second draft had four lanes: field team, quality, finance and *farm*. The farm lane held a
user task, "Sign contract", and a sequence flow ran into it from "Send contract" and out of it to
"Create farm in Coleta and SAP". It read naturally, and it was wrong in a way that would have
mattered the day the diagram was executed.

A lane says the task belongs to this process. An engine running it would have put "Sign contract"
in a task list and waited for somebody to open it and press a button. The farmer, standing next
to the milking parlour in Lima Duarte, has no account on Serra Alta's task list and no reason to
want one. **Serra Alta does not assign work to a farm; it sends the farm something and waits for an
answer.** That is a message going out and a message coming back, so the farm became a pool, the
task became a send task and an intermediate message event, and the sign-and-return happens on the
farm's side of the boundary, where it belongs.

The bank went the same way. Registering the account is a service task inside Serra Alta's process;
what it exchanges with the bank are messages, and how the bank processes them is the bank's
business.

The redraw changed one other thing. With the farm outside the process, it became obvious that
Serra Alta's process has to cope with a farm that never answers, and that the original model did not.
The section on timers and errors is about that case.

## What a lane does and does not do

A lane says who is responsible for the work inside it: the field team checks documents, the quality
team visits, finance registers the account. It is the most useful part of the drawing for the people
in the process, because they find themselves in it.

**In the standard, a lane carries no execution semantics.** An engine does not read lanes to decide
who gets a task; it reads the task's own assignment, a candidate group or a named person set as a
property of the user task. So a task drawn in the quality lane and assigned to finance is a
contradiction no engine will report. When a model is executed, keeping lane and assignment in step
is a review item, and the section on running a model comes back to it.

Lanes also make the handoffs visible, and the handoffs are where the time goes. When Denise and
Joana pulled the dates out of the suite for the farms onboarded in 2025, the work itself, the
document check, the visit, the registration and the contract, came to about four working days per
farm. The rest of the median of 31 days was spent between lanes and between pools: in the quality
team's queue before a technician was free, and waiting for contracts to come back. **Every arrow that
crosses a lane line is a queue, and every message flow is a wait you do not control.** A diagram that
shows where those arrows are is already an analysis of where the time goes.

## How far to draw

There is a temptation, once the farm is a pool, to draw the farm's process too: the farmer reads the
contract, asks a neighbour, signs, posts it back. Leave it out. Serra Alta cannot change it, cannot
observe it and does not run it; drawing it invents a precision nobody has. The black box with the
messages on its edge says everything Serra Alta can know, which is what was sent and what came back.

The same reasoning applies inside the company. A department whose work Serra Alta's process only
hands things to and receives things from, and does not direct, can also be a pool of its own. The
SAP team is not a lane in onboarding: the process does not wait in a person's task list there, it
calls SAP through a service task, and SAP is a system inside Serra Alta rather than a participant.
**A pool is a decision about control, and drawing one is a claim that the other side decides for
itself.**

## One pool, one process

An executable model contains one process per pool, and only the pools you run are white boxes with
something inside. The onboarding collaboration file has three pools and one process; the farm and
the bank are there to show the messages, and an engine ignores them. When the two sides of a
conversation are both processes you own, such as onboarding sending a request to the supplier
registration process, they are two pools exchanging messages rather than one large pool, because
each runs, waits and fails on its own.

BPMN 2.0 also has diagrams that leave the processes out and show only the exchanges between
participants, called conversation and choreography diagrams. They answer a different question, who
talks to whom and in what order, and the section on BPEL explains why that question has a name of
its own.

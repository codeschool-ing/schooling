Farm onboarding has run on IBM BPM since 2014, and lesson 6 asked what owning that suite costs.
Before Joana Brandão can say where the process should run next, she needs to know what it does,
and nobody can tell her in one sentence. Denise Okada, who runs the process office, knows the
version people follow; the suite holds the version that executes; and twelve years of small changes
sit between the two. So the two of them sit down and draw it, in the notation both a process owner
and an engineer can read: **BPMN, the Business Process Model and Notation**.

The common belief is that BPMN is a flowchart with a larger palette, so a box with a slightly
different border is a matter of taste. **It is not a drawing convention; every shape has a defined
meaning, and since version 2.0 a defined behaviour when an engine runs it.** A circle with a thick
border means the path ends there, and the difference between a thin and a double border decides
whether something starts the process or happens halfway through it. Read a diagram as decoration
and you will misread it in exactly the places that matter.

## Where the notation came from

BPMN 1.0 was published in 2004 by the Business Process Management Initiative, BPMI, with Stephen
White of IBM as its main author. BPMI merged into the Object Management Group, OMG, in 2005, and
OMG released **BPMN 2.0 in January 2011**. Version 2.0 is the one that matters: it added a formal
execution semantics and an XML format for exchanging and running models, which is why the name
changed from *Modeling Notation* to *Model and Notation*. It was published as an ISO standard,
ISO/IEC 19510, in 2013. Every modeller you are likely to meet, from a diagram tool to the Camunda
Modeler or bpmn.io in a browser, draws the 2.0 shapes. You do not need one for this lesson; a pencil
works, and so does any tool that draws circles, diamonds and rounded boxes.

## The five families

The specification sorts its elements into five groups, and keeping them apart is half of reading a
diagram:

| family | what it holds |
|---|---|
| flow objects | events, activities and gateways, the things the process passes through |
| connecting objects | sequence flow, message flow and association, the lines between them |
| swimlanes | pools and lanes, which say who is involved |
| data | data objects and data stores, what the work reads and writes |
| artefacts | text annotations and groups, notes for the reader that change nothing |

## Events: something happens

An event is a circle, and **its border says when in the process it happens**. A thin single border
is a start event, a double border an intermediate event, and a thick border an end event. A process
can have more than one end event, and usually should: "farm onboarded" and "farm turned down" are
different outcomes, and a reader should see which one an instance reached.

The marker inside the circle says what kind of event it is. An empty circle is a plain event. An
envelope is a message, a clock a timer, a lightning bolt an error, and there are others for
signals, escalations, conditions and compensation. **A hollow marker catches and a filled marker
throws**: a hollow envelope waits for a message to arrive, a filled one sends one. Onboarding at
Serra Alta begins with a message start event, because what starts it is an application arriving
from a farm, and it waits halfway through on an intermediate message event for the signed contract
to come back.

## Activities: work is done

An activity is a rectangle with rounded corners. A **task** is one unit of work that the diagram
does not break down further. Its type, marked by a small icon in the top-left corner, says who or
what does it:

| task type | who does it | at Serra Alta |
|---|---|---|
| user task | a person, through a task list and a form | a technician records the quality visit |
| service task | a system, called by the engine | the bank account is registered |
| send task | the process, sending a message out | the contract goes to the farm |
| receive task | the process, waiting for a message | (an intermediate event does the same job) |
| manual task | a person, with no system involved | loading the milk sample in the cool box |
| business rule task | a rules engine or decision table | the payment band a new farm starts in |
| script task | a script the engine runs itself | a calculation inside the engine |

A **sub-process** is an activity with its own inside. Drawn collapsed, it is a rounded rectangle with
a small plus sign at the bottom; expanded, it shows its own events, tasks and gateways inside a
larger box. Sub-processes are how a diagram stays readable: the whole of onboarding can be one
collapsed sub-process in a picture of how a farm becomes a supplier, and open into the detail on
its own page. A **call activity**, drawn with a thick border, runs a separate process defined
elsewhere, which is how two processes share one, such as a bank-account check used both by
onboarding and by supplier registration.

## Gateways: the path splits or meets

A gateway is a diamond, and its marker is its rule: an X for exclusive, a plus for parallel, a
circle for inclusive, a pentagon in a double circle for event-based. The next section is about
nothing else, because gateways are where diagrams go wrong.

One rule belongs here, because it is the commonest mistake a newcomer makes. **A gateway does no
work; it only routes on what the work before it produced.** A diamond labelled "Check quality" is
wrong twice: checking is a task, and the gateway after it should ask a question whose answer already
exists, such as "approved?". Joana's first sketch had exactly that diamond, and Denise asked her who
does the checking inside a diamond.

## The lines between them

A **sequence flow**, a solid line with a filled arrowhead, gives the order of the flow objects
inside one process. A **message flow**, dashed with a small hollow circle where it leaves and a
hollow arrowhead where it lands, is a message passing between two participants; the section on pools
and lanes is about where it may go. An **association**, dotted, links a data object, a note or a
compensation handler to the element it belongs to, and carries no order at all.

A **data object**, a page with a folded corner, is information the process uses or produces: the
application, the visit report, the contract. A **data store**, drawn as a cylinder, is information
that outlives the instance, such as Coleta's database or the farm's vendor record in SAP (lesson 1
explained why every farm is a vendor there).

## The happy path, drawn

With those shapes Joana and Denise can draw what happens to a farm whose application goes well.
They draw that first, on purpose: the variations are easier to place once the main line exists, and
a diagram that starts with every exception is one nobody finishes reading.

Read it left to right, the way it was drawn. An application arrives, and a user task has someone in
the field team check the farm's documents. A parallel gateway then starts two things at once. A technician visits the farm to look at the milking and the cooling tank and take a sample, and a
service task registers the farm's bank account, the one Serra Alta will pay into on the 10th of each
month. A second parallel gateway waits for both. An exclusive gateway asks whether the farm was
approved; if it was, the contract goes out, the process waits for it to come back signed, and a
service task creates the farm in Coleta and its vendor record in SAP. If not, the farm is told and
the process ends somewhere else.

**The naming follows a habit worth copying: a task is a verb and an object, an event is a state
that has been reached, and a gateway is a question.** "Check documents", "signed contract
received", "approved?". When a label does not fit its shape's grammar, the shape is usually wrong
too, like the diamond that wanted to check quality.

Onboarding takes about thirty days, and almost none of that time shows on the diagram.
A diagram shows order and dependency, not duration. The waiting is between the shapes, and the
next three sections are about the places where it accumulates: the gateways, the boundaries
between participants, and the tasks that nobody finishes.

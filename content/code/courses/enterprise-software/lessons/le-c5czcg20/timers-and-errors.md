A happy path is the process as it happens when everybody does their part on time. It is the right
thing to draw first and the wrong thing to stop at. **A process that runs for thirty days
spends most of its life waiting, and a model that does not say what happens when the wait goes on
too long has no answer to the commonest thing that goes wrong.**

When Denise pulled the open instances out of IBM BPM in 2026, one farm's application had been
sitting in the task list of a quality technician for 71 days. He had left the company in March. The task was assigned to him by name, the visit had no deadline, and the farm had been
phoning the field team, who could see the instance was *running* and nothing more. A user task with
no timer waits for ever, and it waits politely, without an error and without anybody being told.

## Boundary events

The fix is an event attached to the edge of the activity it watches. A **boundary event** is an
intermediate event drawn on the border of a task or a sub-process, and it can fire only while that
activity is running. When it fires, the token leaves by the boundary event's own sequence flow.

There are two kinds, and the border shows which:

- An **interrupting** boundary event, with a solid double border, cancels the activity. The token
  leaves by the boundary path and the task is withdrawn from whoever had it.
- A **non-interrupting** one, with a dashed double border, leaves the activity running and starts a
  second token on the boundary path. Both carry on.

The quality visit gets a non-interrupting timer. After 10 days without a visit report, a task
appears for Cláudia Mendes, the quality manager, to find out why and reassign it; the visit task
stays open, because cancelling it would throw away a visit that may be booked for tomorrow. An
interrupting timer would have been the right choice if the response to a late visit were to stop
and do something else instead, such as closing the application.

## Writing time down

A timer has one of three definitions, written in ISO 8601 in the model: a date (fire at this
moment), a duration (fire this long after the token arrives) or a cycle (fire repeatedly). Ten days
as a duration is `P10D`; a reminder every five days is the cycle `R/P5D`. A non-interrupting cycle
timer on a visit that stays open for 23 days fires on days 5, 10, 15 and 20: four reminders, and the
fifth would have been due on day 25, after the report arrived.

A timer can also stand in the sequence flow on its own, as an intermediate timer event. Then it is
simply a wait: the token stops there until the time comes. Onboarding has one at its very end in
the full model: a new farm's first collection is scheduled for the next Monday, because the Rota
team publishes truck routes weekly.

## Errors: when a system says no

The bank sometimes rejects an account. The branch number is wrong, or the account belongs to
somebody other than the farm's owner. That is a business outcome with a known response, asking the
farm for the details again, and BPMN models it as an **error**. An error boundary event on the
"Register bank account" task catches the rejection and sends the token to "Ask for the details
again". An error boundary event is always interrupting, because the activity it is attached to has
failed and there is nothing left to continue. Inside a sub-process, an error end event throws the
error, and the boundary event on the sub-process catches it.

**A BPMN error is for an outcome the business has a response to; a technical failure is not one.**
If the bank's service times out, there is nothing to say to the farm. The right response is to try
again in a few minutes, and an engine handles that by retrying the service task and, when the
retries run out, raising an incident for an operator. Modelling every timeout as an error event
fills the diagram with paths the business does not recognise, and hides the ones it does. Lesson 10
comes back to retries and to what happens when one message is delivered twice.

BPMN also has an *escalation* event, which raises something to a higher level without saying
that anything failed, and a *cancel* event for transaction sub-processes. Most models need neither
at first.

## Compensation: undoing completed work

The bank account is registered while the visit is going on, in parallel, so by the time a farm
refuses the contract, the account already exists, linked to a vendor record that will never be
paid. Somebody has to undo it.

The first idea is a rollback, and it is the wrong one. You know from your database courses what a
transaction is; **there is no transaction that stays open for thirty days across a bank, a farm and
two of Serra Alta's systems.** The registration committed weeks ago, in somebody else's system. What
can be done is to run another action that reverses its effect, and that is what BPMN calls
**compensation**.

It is drawn in three parts. A **compensation boundary event**, with the rewind marker of two
triangles, sits on the activity that might need undoing. A dotted association links it to a
**compensation activity**, "Cancel registration", which carries the same marker and has no sequence
flow into it or out of it, because it never runs as part of the normal path. And somewhere later a
**compensation throw event**, intermediate or end, with a filled marker, triggers it. When that
event fires, the engine runs the compensation activity of every activity in scope that completed;
**an activity that never finished has nothing to compensate**, so a farm turned down before its
account was registered triggers nothing.

The same idea, a long-lived sequence of steps where each one has an action that undoes it, has a
name in database research: a **saga**, from a 1987 paper by Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem.
The BPMN version draws the undo next to the thing it undoes, which is where a reviewer looks for it.

## Whichever comes first

The contract goes to the farm, and three things can happen: it comes back signed, the farm says no,
or nothing comes back at all. The third is the one that was missing from the model in the suite, and
it is the one an **event-based gateway** handles. The gateway is followed by catching events, here a
message "signed", a message "refused" and a 30-day timer; the first to occur takes the token, and
the others are withdrawn. A refusal and a timeout both end in a compensation end event, so the
account registration is undone either way, and the field team is told which of the two happened.

**Every waiting point in a model should answer the question "and if it never comes?"** A receive
task, a message event, a user task: each one is a place an instance can stay for ever, and the
answer is a timer, either on the boundary or racing it at an event-based gateway. Denise and Joana
went through the model with that question and found five waiting points; the suite's version had
a deadline on one.

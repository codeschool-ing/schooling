The rule most engineers carry into this decision is short: build what matters, buy what does not.
It sounds like prudence and it sorts the wrong way. Payroll matters enormously — get it wrong and
3,200 people are paid late, and some of them stop coming to work — and almost no company in the
world writes its own. **Importance is not the axis that decides between buying and building.** The
axis is whether the work makes the company different from its competitors, and importance is a
second, separate question about how carefully the work has to be done whichever way it is sourced.

This section gives you both axes, a third way of slicing the estate that a large company will
recognise, and the default answer that comes out of them. It also shows Joana getting the first
step wrong, in the way somebody who has spent eight years building is likely to.

## Core and context

Geoffrey Moore gives the distinction its fullest form in *Dealing with Darwin* (2005). **Core** is any work that
creates differentiation a customer will pay for or choose you for: done better than competitors, it
wins business. **Context** is everything else the company has to do — work that must be done, and
done properly, but that earns nothing extra for being done better than the industry standard. Moore's
point was about where to put scarce talent and attention: on core, while context is standardised,
simplified and handed to whoever does it best and most cheaply.

He added the second axis because the first one alone invites the mistake above. Work is also
**mission-critical** or not, depending on whether getting it wrong does serious and immediate damage.
Payroll is context and mission-critical. So is issuing an NF-e for every sale: a dairy that cannot
invoice cannot ship. Neither earns Serra Alta a single extra litre of milk or a single extra
supermarket shelf by being excellent; both would close the company by failing.

**Context that is mission-critical is the natural home of a bought product.** It is exactly the work
where you want what thousands of other companies already use, tested by their mistakes and kept
current with the law by somebody whose whole business is keeping it current. A dairy that writes its
own payroll is volunteering to track every change to Brazilian labour and tax rules with a team whose
real job is elsewhere.

Two tests help when a function is hard to place:

| test | core if… |
|---|---|
| the customer's view | the people you compete for would notice, and care, if you did it exactly like your competitor |
| the copy test | a competitor who bought the same software would still not have your advantage |

The second test is the one that saves the most money. If a competitor could get your advantage by
installing the same product, the advantage was the product's and not yours, and you can buy it too.
If they could install the product and still not have it, the advantage lives somewhere else — in
your data, your people, your rules — and it may survive being run on somebody else's software.

## Pace layers

Gartner published a related model in 2012, the pace-layered application strategy. It sorts
applications by how fast they need to change, because that rate decides how they should be governed,
funded and sourced:

| layer | changes | at Serra Alta | sourcing it tends towards |
|---|---|---|---|
| systems of record | slowly; years between real changes | SAP ECC's finance, purchasing and stock; payroll; NF-e | a standard package, configured, kept close to the vendor's release |
| systems of differentiation | every few months, as the business competes | how farms are paid for quality; Rota's routes for the milk trucks | a package extended beside, or a build, when no package fits |
| systems of innovation | weeks; most experiments fail | new ideas tried on the Portal B2B | built quickly and thrown away freely |

**The layers are not a ranking.** Systems of record are where the money is counted and the law is
met; they change slowly because they must be right, not because they matter less. What the model adds
is that one governance for all three layers is wrong for two of them. A six-week change approval
suits the ledger and kills an experiment. A weekly release habit suits the portal, and would make
the auditors of lesson 1's month close very nervous.

## Joana's first draft

Joana's first note on the farm-payment replacement put it in the top-right corner of the figure, and
the conclusion followed in the next sentence: core, so build. Her reasoning was not careless. **How
Serra Alta pays for milk quality is how it keeps the best farms.** A farm with low somatic cell
counts and high protein is courted by every dairy in the Zona da Mata, and the bonus tables that
reward it are a commercial weapon. Sebastião's RPG holds those rules, 46 of them, and every one was
argued over by somebody.

Cláudia Mendes, whose quality team writes the tables, asked the question that split the box. Twice a
year her committee revises the bonus for fat, protein, somatic cells and bacterial count. What the
farms notice is the tables, how quickly the money arrives on the 10th, and the field technicians who
visit to help a farm reach the next band. Not one farm has ever noticed how the multiplication is
done.

Put through the copy test, the function comes apart in two:

- **the policy** — the bands, the bonuses, the penalties, the technicians' programme — is core. A
  competitor who bought any software at all would still not have Serra Alta's tables, or the thirty
  years of each farm's lab history that Coleta holds, or its technicians;
- **the calculation** — take the litres, take the lab results, apply the tables, produce the amount
  for each farm, hand it to SAP to pay — is context. Every dairy in Brazil does it, and several
  products already do it for them.

**The differentiator is the policy, and a policy is data.** The question for a product is therefore
not "is farm payment core?" but "can this product express our policy, and let Cláudia's committee
change it twice a year without a project?" That question has a measurable answer, and Joana
measured it. A fit-gap against the SaaS product she shortlisted found that 41 of the 46 rules could be
configured in the product's own rule tables. The other five need an extension written beside the
product, through its published interface. One of them is a bonus paid back over six months to a farm
that holds its somatic cell count below a threshold for that long. Extending beside is the middle
ring of lesson 1's three: configure, extend beside, never modify.

Joana's first draft is a common mistake for a good reason. **Somebody who builds well sees an
interesting problem and a differentiator in the same place, and they are not the same thing.** The
calculation is interesting to build. The policy is what wins farms.

## The default, and the burden of proof

Put the two axes together and the default falls out: **for context, buy; building needs a reason,
and the reason goes in writing.** The default is not a claim that buying is cheaper; section 06 of this
lesson finds a horizon at which the build would win. It is that context bought is context somebody
else keeps current,
and the people Serra Alta pays to build can spend their time on the things that are core.

Reasons that justify building context, each of which can be checked:

- no product can express a rule the business will not give up, and the gap is too large to extend
  beside;
- the data is the advantage and a product would hold it where Serra Alta cannot use it;
- the volume makes every per-unit price absurd at the company's scale;
- the products on the market cannot meet a legal requirement that applies to Serra Alta.

Reasons that sound like reasons and are not:

- "we could build it better" — almost certainly true of the part you can see, and a statement about
  the builders rather than about the business;
- "we do not want to depend on a vendor" — every option depends on somebody, and section 05 of this
  lesson puts a price on the dependence instead of a slogan;
- "our process is unique" — sometimes. Lesson 1's "fit to standard" asks whether the process is
  unique because it wins business, or unique because nobody ever tidied it.

## A third answer

Serra Alta's estate offers something the two-way question leaves out. The company is moving off SAP
ECC to S/4HANA anyway, so the farm payment could be built **inside the system of record it already
owns**, as an extension of SAP. That is option B in the rest of this lesson, beside A (buy the SaaS
product) and C (build it in house). Edson Prado favours it, and his argument is serious: the farms are
already vendor records in SAP, SAP already pays them through the bank files, and one fewer system is
one fewer integration.

Core and context do not choose between A, B and C. They set the starting point — the calculation is
context, so the build has to earn its place — and they say what any option must protect: Cláudia's
ability to change the policy twice a year. Everything after this section is about money, time and
risk, and the first job is to count the money properly.

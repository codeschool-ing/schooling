**A join is the operation that MapReduce made hardest**, and seeing why explains half of what Spark
does differently. Suppose the question is revenue per category: purchases come from the clicks, and
categories and prices from the 3,000 books. The two have to meet on `book_id`.

**The reduce-side join** is the general method. The mapper reads both inputs and emits every record
keyed by `book_id`, *tagged* with where it came from: `B` for a book, `P` for a purchase. The
partitioner sends every record with the same `book_id` to the same reducer, which then sees the book
and all its purchases together, and can multiply. It works for inputs of any size, and it shuffles
both of them in full: every purchase crosses the network to meet a book that is a few dozen bytes.

**The map-side join** is the shortcut when one side is small. Every map task loads the whole small
table into memory before it starts, then reads its slice of the large input and looks each record
up as it goes. Nothing is shuffled. Hadoop Streaming's `-files` is the delivery mechanism: the same
argument that shipped `mapper.py` can ship `books.csv` to every task.

The trade is the one lesson 8 measures in Spark, where the two are called a *sort-merge join* and a
*broadcast join*:

| | moves over the network | needs in memory |
|---|---|---|
| reduce-side join | both inputs, in full | one key's records at a time |
| map-side join | the small input, once per task | the whole small input, in every task |

In MapReduce the programmer wrote one or the other, by hand, for every join. Choosing between them
automatically, from the sizes of the inputs, is one of the first things Spark's planner does, and
lesson 6 shows it deciding.

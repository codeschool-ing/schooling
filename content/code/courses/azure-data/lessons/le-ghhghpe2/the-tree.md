**The first question, the access pattern, splits the stores of this course into six branches**, and
almost every workload falls clearly into one of them. The other five questions then choose within
the branch: which tier, which consistency, how much capacity.

**One item, or a few, by a key, at any scale: Cosmos DB**, lessons 11 to 14. The shape is a
customer's cart, a device's latest reading, a user's profile: read and written by an identifier,
millions of times, by many clients at once. The partition key is the decision that matters, and the
consistency level of lesson 12 and the request units of lesson 13 are the next two.

**Rows that change together inside a transaction: a relational database**, lessons 8 to 10. Azure SQL
Database for a new application, Managed Instance when an existing SQL Server estate needs what the
database service refuses, the flexible servers when the application speaks PostgreSQL or MySQL.
Orders, payments and stock, where a sale either happened completely or not at all, live here.

**The same answer, read very often, faster than a database gives it: a cache**, lesson 15, Azure
Cache for Redis or Azure Managed Redis in front of a store of record. It is never the branch on its
own, which is why the tree draws it beside the database rather than instead of one.

**Whole files, kept for months or years, read in bulk: Blob Storage**, lessons 2 to 7, with the
hierarchical namespace of lesson 3 when the files are a lake. Exports, images, backups, Capture's
Avro, the raw layer of every pipeline. The tier and the life-cycle rule are the decisions that matter.

**Scans and aggregates over months of history: the warehouse or the lake engine**, lessons 16 to 18.
Synapse's pools or Fabric's warehouse and lakehouse, reading Parquet and Delta files whose layout
decides what a query reads. Many rows at once, few queries, seconds of patience.

**Events that never stop arriving: Event Hubs, and then one of the other branches**, lessons 22 to
24. A stream is a way in, not a place to keep things: what arrives is read by a job and lands in a
store from another branch, a Cosmos DB container for the latest state, the lake for history.

## What the tree does not decide

**A workload can sit across two branches, and then it is two workloads.** Quitanda's orders are
written in transactions, which is the relational branch, and analysed over two years, which is the
warehouse branch. The answer is not a store that does both badly; it is the order in a relational
database and a copy in the lake, kept current by a pipeline from lesson 19 or a change stream. The
next section does this for each of Quitanda's workloads, and its last paragraph is the rule that
keeps two copies from becoming two truths.

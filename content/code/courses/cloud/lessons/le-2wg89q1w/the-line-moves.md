IaaS, PaaS and SaaS are usually taught as three boxes to memorise. **They are one idea: a line drawn
across the same stack at three heights.** Below the line the provider does the work, and above it you
do.

Counted off the drawing, the line leaves you six of the ten rows on IaaS, three on PaaS and two on
SaaS. Each step up takes rows off your side, and never adds any.

AWS calls this the **shared responsibility model**, and puts it in two phrases: AWS is responsible for
security *of* the cloud, and the customer for security *in* the cloud. Microsoft and Google publish
their own drawings of the same idea, with their own names for the rows. Once you can read one of them
you can read all three, and this course uses the neutral version above.

## What the line means, and what it does not

**Below the line, the provider does the job and you cannot.** You will never be offered a way to patch
the hypervisor or replace a disk in their building, so there is no point planning for it, and no
point worrying about it either: it is their business, and their contract says how well they do it.

**Above the line, you do the job and the provider will not.** That half is the one that bites. The
provider is not being unhelpful when it leaves your virtual machine unpatched; touching it would mean
logging in to a customer's server, which is precisely what it has promised not to do. Nothing above
the line happens unless somebody on your side makes it happen, and **when nobody does, there is
usually no error message**. The machine keeps running with its old packages, the folder stays shared,
the former employee's login keeps working.

## The two rows it never reaches

The top two rows are yours on every model, and the drawing shows why they have to be. The data is
what your business collected, and only you know which of it matters, how long it has to be kept, and
who is allowed to see it. Identity and access decides who in your organisation may do what, and only
you know who works there. A provider can give you excellent tools for both, and lesson 7 spends a
whole lesson on the tools for access. **It cannot use them for you.**

## The line is drawn per service, not per company

Real systems are not one model. A small company with its email on SaaS, its website on a platform,
and an old accounting program on a virtual machine has three lines, one per system, at three
different heights. The question "are we on the cloud?" has no useful answer for that company. The
question "for this system, where is the line?" has one, and it tells you who to call when something
breaks.

The line is also not perfectly straight inside one model. On IaaS the physical network is the
provider's and the rules of your virtual network are yours, which is why the drawing splits the network
into two rows. A platform may let you pick the runtime version from its list, which is a small piece of
the runtime row coming back to your side. The drawing is the rule; the documentation of each service
says where it bends.

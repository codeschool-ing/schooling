The picture most people arrive with is a ladder: **AWS, Azure and Google Cloud are the real cloud,
and DigitalOcean, Hetzner and Linode are hosting companies for hobby projects.** It is wrong in a
way that costs money in both directions. Teams pay hyperscaler prices for three virtual machines
and a database, and other teams pick a small provider for a design that needs a managed service
only a hyperscaler sells.

The honest split is not big and small. It is **how much of the stack the provider will run for
you**.

## Hyperscalers

AWS, Azure and Google Cloud are called hyperscalers. Each one sells hundreds of managed services:
not only machines and disks, but queues, data warehouses, streaming, machine learning, directories
of users, content delivery, key management. Each one runs regions on several continents, and each
one prices every service separately, so the full price list runs to tens of thousands of lines. You
will count them for AWS at the end of this lesson: one service, EC2, lists more than sixty-five
thousand products in one region alone.

In the vocabulary of lesson 1, a hyperscaler sells every layer. You can rent a bare virtual machine
and run everything yourself, which is IaaS, or you can rent a queue and never see the machine it
runs on, which is PaaS. **The breadth is the product**: whatever your design needs, one of the
three sells it managed.

## Smaller providers

DigitalOcean, Hetzner and Akamai's Linode sell a short list: virtual machines, block disks, object
storage, private networks, load balancers, and in most cases managed databases and managed
Kubernetes. Their regions are fewer. Their bills are simpler, because most of what you pay for is a
machine size at a monthly price, with a quantity of data transfer included.

That short list is **the core that most applications actually use**. A web application with a
PostgreSQL database, some uploaded files and a load balancer in front fits inside it completely.

## Why "toys" is the wrong word

A virtual machine is a virtual machine. The Linux kernel inside a Droplet does not know it is not
inside EC2, and the PostgreSQL you install on it is the same program. Companies with real revenue
run entirely on the smaller providers, and the reason is usually the bill: fewer lines, a price per
month you can read before you sign, and transfer that is included rather than metered.

What the smaller provider leaves you is **the work of running what it does not sell**. Suppose your
design needs a message queue. At a hyperscaler you rent one, and the provider patches it, replicates
it and wakes up at night when it breaks. At a smaller provider you install RabbitMQ on a machine,
and all of that is yours. Neither is wrong. The first costs more per month and less per engineer;
the second is the other way round.

So the comparison between the two kinds comes down to one question: **which pieces of your design
are you willing to operate yourself?** If the answer is "none beyond the
application", you need a hyperscaler, or a design that uses only the core. If the answer is "a
database and a queue, happily", the short list may be all you need.

The middle of the field exists too: Oracle, IBM, Alibaba and OVHcloud sell more than the short
list and less than the three, each with a reason of its own to exist. They get a paragraph each
further down this lesson.

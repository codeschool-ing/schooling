A ranking is not yet a decision. The inbox does not arrive eight odd messages at a time, and
tomorrow there may be none, so "flag the top eight" means nothing. What Ana needs is a **cut-off**:
flag every message whose score is above it.

The tempting way is to look at today's scores and pick a round number that falls in the gap, say
0.55. That number came from the messages it is about to judge, so it is guaranteed to look good
on them and says nothing about tomorrow. It is also tied to this model: lesson 2 showed that
scores from one model bunch in their own narrow band, and 0.55 means something different for
another model.

## Read the cut-off from normal data

The cut-off should come from the only thing the detector is allowed to know, which is what normal
looks like. Score a set of **normal messages the reference has never seen**, and put the line at a
high percentile of their scores: at the 95th percentile, by construction, about one normal message
in twenty lands above it.

Those messages have to be held out. Scored against a reference that contains them, every message
finds itself as its own nearest neighbour, and the first line of the output below shows what that
gives. So the 150 tickets are split the way lesson 4 split them: the 100 training tickets are the
reference, and the 50 test tickets are the held-out normal.

**With k=1, every cut-off from the 90th percentile to the maximum flags exactly the eight odd
messages.** On this inbox, against these tickets, the nearest-neighbour distance leaves a gap wide
enough that the choice of percentile does not matter. Do not expect that on real traffic; expect
the k=5 lines.

With k=5 the trade-off is plain. The 90th percentile, 0.685, catches all eight and flags three
normal messages with them: a damaged hardback, the reading-app font and a request for a copy of
the customer's data. The 99th, 0.766, flags nothing by mistake and lets two odd ones through, the
free-iPhone spam and the job application. The 95th happens to land in between.

## Precision, recall and what a flag is for

The two numbers on each line are the ones lesson 4 used. **Precision** is how many flagged
messages were really odd; **recall** is how many odd messages were flagged. Moving the line right
raises precision and lowers recall, and no position of the line raises both.

Which one to protect depends on what a flag **does**. If a flagged message is deleted, a false
alarm loses a real customer, and the line belongs far to the right. If a flagged message goes to a
person who glances at it, a false alarm costs a few seconds, and the line can sit further left.
The second is the usual arrangement, and the safer one, because the next two sections show
messages that a detector flags or passes for reasons that have nothing to do with whether they
matter.

Two cautions about the numbers. Fifty held-out tickets make a coarse ruler: the 99th percentile
of fifty scores is a point between the two highest, so one unusual ticket moves it. And the
percentile is a promise about **normal** messages only. It says roughly how many normal messages
will be flagged; it says nothing about how many odd ones will be caught, which only a set of
known odd messages, like the eight here, can measure.

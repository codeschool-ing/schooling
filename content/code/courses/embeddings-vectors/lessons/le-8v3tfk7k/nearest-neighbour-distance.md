---
title: Distance to the nearest neighbours
version: 1
---

Instead of asking how far a message is from the middle of normal, ask how far it is from the
**closest normal messages**. A message about a late parcel has another late-parcel ticket right
next to it, wherever the shipping group sits. A recipe question has nothing next to it at all.

The score is the distance to the **k-th nearest** ticket: with k=1 the single closest one, with
k=5 the fifth closest. It is lesson 4's k-nearest-neighbours, used for a distance instead of a vote.

```schooling-example

  "language": "python",
  "file": "knn.py",
  "parts": [
    {
      "code": "import json\nimport numpy as np\nfrom minilm import embed\n\ntickets = [json.loads(l) for l in open(\"data/tickets.jsonl\")]\ninbox = [json.loads(l) for l in open(\"data/inbox.jsonl\")]\nN = embed([t[\"text\"] for t in tickets])\nX = embed([m[\"text\"] for m in inbox])",
      "note": "The same two sets of vectors as before."
    },
    {
      "code": "def knn_score(Q, R, k):\n    S = np.sort(Q @ R.T, axis=1)\n    return 1 - S[:, -k]",
      "note": "Sort each message's 150 similarities. The k-th largest is its k-th nearest ticket, and one minus it is the score."
    },
    {
      "code": "for k in (1, 5):\n    score = knn_score(X, N, k)\n    order = np.argsort(-score)\n    print(f\"k={k}  odd at ranks {[r for r, i in enumerate(order, 1) if inbox[i]['odd']]}\")\n    for r, i in enumerate(order[6:10], 7):\n        m = inbox[i]\n        print(f\"  {r:2}  {score[i]:.3f}  {m['id']}  {'ODD' if m['odd'] else '   '}  {m['text'][:40]}\")",
      "note": "For k=1 and k=5, print where the odd messages ranked, and ranks 7 to 10, where the last odd ones meet the first normal ones."
    },
    {
      "code": "spam = embed([\"You have won a gift card! Click here to claim your prize before midnight.\"])\ndirty = np.vstack([N, spam])\ni33 = [m[\"id\"] for m in inbox].index(\"m33\")\nfor k in (1, 5):\n    score = knn_score(X, dirty, k)\n    print(f\"one spam among the normal, k={k}: m33 scores {score[i33]:.3f}, \"\n          f\"rank {1 + (score > score[i33]).sum()}\")",
      "note": "Slip one spam message into the normal set, as happens when nobody checks the reference, and see what each k does to the free-iPhone spam."
    }
```

**With either k, the eight odd messages take the first eight places.** The spam that hid at 16th
against the centroid is 8th now, because no single ticket resembles it closely, even though the
average of all of them did.

The lines around the boundary show how clean the separation is. With k=1, the last odd message
scores 0.613 and the first normal one 0.530. With k=5 the gap is 0.726 against 0.709, much
narrower: the fifth-nearest ticket is further away for everyone, and a normal message from a
thinly covered topic pays for that as much as the spam does.

## Why anyone uses k above 1

On this data k=1 separates better, and that is a measurement, not a law. The last two lines of the
output show what k=1 costs. They add **one spam message to the normal set**, as happens when
nobody checks what went into the reference, and score the inbox again.

With k=1, m33 drops to a score of 0.369 and 20th place. Its nearest normal message is now the
planted spam, which is about claiming a prize too, so it looks entirely normal. With k=5 it scores
0.707 and comes 9th: the planted message is one of its five nearest, and the fifth is still a
real ticket a long way off.

So k is a choice about **how much you trust the reference**. k=1 is the sharpest when every normal
message really is normal; a larger k shrugs off a few bad ones at the price of a blunter score.
Measure both on your own data, with a set of known odd messages, before choosing.

## What it costs

The centroid is one comparison per message. The nearest neighbours are one comparison per
**normal message**, so the cost grows with the reference: 150 here, which is nothing, and a
million in a busy shop. That is a vector search like lesson 3's, and lessons 11 to 15 are about
doing it without reading every vector.

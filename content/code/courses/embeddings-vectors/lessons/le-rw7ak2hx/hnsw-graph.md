An index in an ordinary database keeps its keys in order, and a search halves the range at every
step. **Vectors have no order to keep.** There is no way to sort 384-dimensional points so that
neighbours sit next to each other in a list, and IVF's cells are one answer to that. HNSW is the
other, and the one you will meet most: Chroma and Qdrant are built around it, pgvector
offers it as `hnsw`, and lesson 13's `HNSW32` is FAISS's version.

## A graph of neighbours

Link every vector to a few of its near neighbours, and a search becomes a walk. Start anywhere,
look at the current vector's neighbours, move to whichever is closest to the query, and repeat
until no neighbour is closer. Each step is a handful of comparisons, and the walk goes straight
towards the query instead of reading the collection.

On one flat graph the walk is slow, because links join near neighbours and a vector far from the
query is many short steps away. **HNSW, the hierarchical navigable small world, adds layers.**
Every vector is in layer 0. When a vector is inserted it is also placed in layer 1 with a small
probability, in layer 2 with that probability again, and so on, and each layer links its vectors
to their nearest neighbours *within that layer*. The top layers hold few vectors, far apart, so
their links are long jumps. The bottom layer holds everything, so its links are short.

## Watching a search

`layers.py` builds FAISS's HNSW index over the 100,000 vectors with 16 links per vector, counts
the vectors in each layer, and then walks the top layers by hand, printing every vector it steps
to and its score against the first query:

## Layer 0 keeps a list

On the bottom layer the search changes from a walk to a **list of candidates**. It keeps the
`ef` best vectors seen so far, takes the best one it has not yet expanded, compares the query with
that vector's neighbours, and adds any that beat the worst on the list. It stops when no candidate
left to expand can improve the list. A single greedy walk would stop at the first vector with no
closer neighbour; the list lets the search step past such a dead end, because the second and
third best are still there to expand.

With FAISS's default list of 16, it reached 0.536, which exact search confirms is the true best match for this query, **after
261 comparisons in all**, out of 100,000 vectors. That is the whole trick: the top layers bring
the search to the right neighbourhood in a few long jumps, and the bottom layer searches that
neighbourhood carefully.

## Building is searching

Inserting a vector is a search: find its nearest neighbours in the graph built so far, on every
layer it belongs to, and link it to them. So **building the index costs a search per vector**,
which is why it took 2.7 s on four cores here while IVF's training was the slow part of the last
section. In exchange, an HNSW index **needs no training**: it starts empty and takes vectors one
at a time, and a vector added next year is linked into the graph like the first.

Taking one out is harder, because other vectors' paths run through it. Lesson 11 met the answer
most databases give, the tombstone, and lesson 18 prices the rebuild that clears them.

**On an enterprise network the access point does not know any password. It relays each device's
sign-in to a server, keeps the door shut until that server says yes, and receives from it a key
made for that one session.** The standard that arranges this is IEEE 802.1X, and the conversation
it carries is EAP, the *Extensible Authentication Protocol*.

## Three roles

- The **supplicant** is the device asking to join: a laptop's `wpa_supplicant`, or the Wi-Fi settings
  of Windows, macOS, Android and iOS.
- The **authenticator** is the access point, or on a cable the switch port. Until authentication
  succeeds it passes only EAP frames and drops everything else. It understands none of the EAP
  conversation; it wraps each message in a RADIUS packet and forwards it.
- The **authentication server** is a RADIUS server, here FreeRADIUS, which checks the credentials
  against a directory such as the LDAP of lesson 12 or Active Directory, and answers *accept* or
  *reject*.

When the answer is accept, the server also sends the access point a 32-byte key in the same RADIUS
message, protected with the secret the two share. That key is the PMK of lesson 15, and the
four-way handshake runs from it exactly as before. The difference is where it came from: **not
from a passphrase everybody knows, but from this person's sign-in, fresh for this session.**

## What that buys Vereda

- **Each session has its own PMK.** The physiotherapist cannot derive the receptionist's keys,
  because they never shared one. Section 03 shows two sessions in a row producing two different
  PMKs.
- **Revocation is one account.** When somebody leaves, disabling their account in the directory locks
  them out of the network at their next sign-in. Nobody types a new passphrase into forty devices.
- **The server knows who connected, when and from which device**, and can place each person on a
  network segment by role: staff on the clinical VLAN, a visiting technician on one that reaches only
  the equipment they came for.
- **It works on cables too.** The network socket in the waiting room wall can require 802.1X, so that
  plugging a laptop into it reaches nothing.

## The link between access point and server

The RADIUS packets between the access point and the server are protected by a **shared secret**,
configured on both. RADIUS dates from 1991 and builds that protection from MD5. In 2024 the
**Blast-RADIUS** research used an MD5 collision, the technique lesson 4 described, to forge an
*accept* on links where somebody could intercept and alter the traffic. EAP messages always carry
the `Message-Authenticator` attribute, an HMAC that defeats the forgery, and the fix for every other
kind of RADIUS request was to require that attribute everywhere.

The practical rules have not changed. The shared secret is long and random, never a word. RADIUS
traffic stays on a management network that users cannot reach. Where it has to cross anything
else, **RadSec**, RADIUS carried inside TLS, replaces the MD5 layer with the protocol of lesson 10.

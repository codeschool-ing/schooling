title: Who accessibility is for
version: 1

**Accessibility is whether a person can use what you built with the body and the tools they have.** The
picture most people hold is a small group of blind users, and it is the wrong picture: it is too small,
and it puts the problem in the person rather than in the page.

The World Health Organization estimates that **about 1.3 billion people, one in six, live with a
significant disability**. Some see little or nothing and use a screen reader, or magnify the screen many
times over. Some cannot use a mouse and use a keyboard, a switch or their voice. Some do not hear the
video. Some read slowly, lose their place, or are tired by movement on the page. And every one of those
also happens to people with no disability at all, for an afternoon: a broken arm, a phone in bright sun,
a train too loud for sound, a medication that blurs the eyes. **A design that works for the first group
works for the second**, which is why accessibility is described as permanent, temporary and situational.

## Why it is the developer's job

Most barriers are not in what a page *shows* but in how it is *built*: a button that is a `div`, an input
whose label is a placeholder, a colour the eye can barely separate from its background. Those decisions
are in the code, the person meeting them cannot fix them, and the person who can is the one who wrote
them. On an education site the cost is plain: **a screen somebody cannot operate is a student who cannot
study**.

It is also, increasingly, the law. Brazil's Lei Brasileira de Inclusão, Law 13.146 of 2015, requires the
websites of companies and public bodies to be accessible. The European Accessibility Act has applied to
many products and services sold in the EU, online shops and banking among them, since 28 June 2025. In
the United States, courts read the Americans with Disabilities Act as covering websites. All of them
point, in practice, at the same standard, which is the next section.

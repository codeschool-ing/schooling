**The `alt` of an image is not a description of the picture; it is what the image is for, in words.** The
same photograph needs different words in different places, and sometimes none. Lesson 10 marked the
planner's book icons decorative with `alt=""`; this is how that decision is made for any image.

| the image | its `alt` | for example |
|---|---|---|
| **informative**: it says something the text does not | that thing, briefly | a photo of a broken screen in a support article: `alt="The screen shows a white bar across its middle"` |
| **decorative**: it adds nothing the text has not said | empty, `alt=""` | the book icon beside the course's name |
| **functional**: it is the only thing inside a link or a button | what the control does or where it goes | a magnifier that submits a search: `alt="Search"` |
| **text in an image** | the text | a logo that is the company's name: `alt="Planner"` |
| **complex**: a chart, a diagram | a short summary, and the full content nearby in text | a bar chart: `alt="Minutes studied per week, rising from 60 to 160"`, with a table under it |

Three rules settle most cases.

- **Never leave `alt` out.** With no `alt`, a reader falls back on the file name, as axe showed in lesson 10;
  `alt=""` is a decision and a missing `alt` is the absence of one.
- **Do not start with "image of".** The reader already says *image* or *graphic*.
- **Read it with the text around it.** If the sentence beside the image already says it, the image is
  decorative here, whatever it would be on another page.

## Images that are not `<img>`

An SVG drawn inline in the page has no `alt`. It takes `role="img"` and an `aria-label`, or a `<title>` as its first
child, which is what every diagram in this course does: open one in your browser's developer tools and the
label is there, saying what the drawing shows. A CSS background image is invisible to the tree by
construction, which is right for decoration and wrong for anything that carries meaning.

**What no tool can check is whether the words are right.** axe confirms that an `alt` exists; it accepts
`alt="image"` and `alt="IMG_2041.jpg"` without a murmur. Writing it is a decision about meaning, and it
belongs to whoever knows why the image is on the page.

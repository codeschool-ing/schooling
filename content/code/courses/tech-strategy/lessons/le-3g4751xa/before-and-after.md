An autopsy is worth its cost only if it changes what happens next. For the portal that means two
things: four steps that would have stopped it while it was still a proposal, and a decision about
the portal that exists now. **Both are cheap compared with 1,100 hours**, and both are easy to skip
because neither produces anything to demo.

## Before: four steps, in this order

**1. Ask the people who would use it.** Not the people who asked for it — the people whose work it
would change. For the portal that was every team that deploys, and the question was how they deploy
today and what hurts about it. Six conversations would have found the 38 services already deploying
on merge.

**2. Measure the pain.** A request that sounds frequent may be rare. Platform could have counted the
deploy requests in its channel for a month and the hours spent answering them. That turns the
proposal into arithmetic: a tool that costs 1,100 hours has to save more than 1,100 hours over its
life, and the count says whether the pain is that large. If it is not, the proposal stops here,
whatever its other merits.

**3. Build the smallest thing that removes the pain.** Lesson 14 called this the thinnest viable
platform. For the portal's real users — the three services without pipelines — the smallest thing
was a pipeline from the template; for Platform's own interruptions, it was a chat command answering
"which version is in production?". Either would have taken a fraction of the hours, and each would
have shown whether anybody wanted more.

**4. Write the kill date before it ships.** The decision about whether the tool has worked is made
in advance, when nobody has spent anything yet and nobody is attached to it. It names a date, a
level of use and what happens if the level is not met. Rafaela's version, now attached to every
Platform proposal, reads like this:

> If, six months after launch, the teams that said they needed this are not using it for their
> normal work, we switch it off, move its users to the path they used before, and write down what
> we learnt. Nobody needs to agree to this at the time; it was agreed now.

A kill date does not mean the tool will be killed. It means the decision will be taken on evidence
at a time chosen in advance, instead of being taken by momentum at no time at all.

## After: deciding about the portal that exists

The R$ 165,000 is gone whichever option Rafaela picks, so it is left out of the comparison. **What is
compared is what each option costs from now on and what it gives the people still using it.**

| option | what it costs from now on | what it gives |
|---|---|---|
| keep it as it is | patches, hosting, on-call and deploy credentials, all for three services | the three users keep a tool that works for them |
| keep the part people open | one read-only page to maintain | the version view, which teams across Coreto did open |
| switch it off with a date | the work of moving three services onto the template's pipeline | nothing to maintain, and the three get what the other 38 have |

Rafaela chose the second and third together. The read-only version page stayed, because it was the
one part of the portal that teams outside the three opened in their own time. The deploy and
approval buttons were given a date, and the three services moved onto the template's pipeline
before it, the same pipeline every new service in lesson 14 could start from.

She also published the autopsy to the whole engineering organisation, with the cost in it. The
engineers who built the portal were named as the people who built something that worked; the
decision to build it without asking was named as the organisation's, which it was.

## What to take into the next proposal

The portal's story ends in a useful page and a lesson. The proposal it teaches you to stop is the
next one, while it is still a document:

| sign in a proposal | the question that tests it |
|---|---|
| the requirements came from requests to the builders | who would change how they work, and were they asked? |
| the pain is described, not counted | how many hours a month does it cost today? |
| the first version is the whole thing | what is the smallest version anybody would use? |
| there is a launch date and nothing after it | on what date, and at what level of use, do we stop? |

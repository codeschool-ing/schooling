Ask what a decision costs and most engineers answer in hours: the seat-hold fix is three weeks of
the group's time, priced at R$ 150 an hour as lesson 11 prices everything. That number is real and
it is the wrong one for choosing. The group is paid whatever it works on, so its hours cost the same
in every order. **What differs between choices is what the hours would have produced elsewhere**,
and that is the opportunity cost: the value of the best alternative given up.

## The cost of putting Pix first

Júlia's case for Pix in instalments first was a fair one: the largest cost of delay, and buyers
asking for it every week. The sheet prices what that order gives up. Compared with the CD3 order:

| item | CD3 order finishes | Pix first finishes | change | delay cost of the change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pix in instalments | week 21 | week 12 | 9 weeks earlier | R$ 432,000 saved |
| Festival seat maps | week 9 | week 18 | 9 weeks later | R$ 270,000 more |
| Seat-hold fix | week 3 | week 21 | 18 weeks later | R$ 324,000 more |
| Partner API | week 25 | week 25 | none | — |

Pix first saves R$ 432,000 on Pix and costs R$ 594,000 on the other two (270,000 + 324,000).
**The difference, R$ 162,000, is the opportunity cost of that order**, and it is the same figure the
last section found by subtracting the totals. Seen this way it has a shape: Pix gains, and the
seat-hold fix pays most of the bill, because it is pushed back by the whole length of Pix.

## Saying it to the person who asked

The table changes the conversation with Júlia, and it is worth being careful about how. "My item is
more important than yours" is the argument the sheet exists to replace, and Davi does not make it.
He says what the order costs and what it buys: **Pix first costs R$ 162,000 of delay overall; in the
CD3 order Pix arrives in week 21, and the seat-hold fix is in production in week 3.** Then he asks whether her estimate for Pix is too low, because a higher estimate is what
would change the answer: if Pix's cost of delay were high enough that its CD3 passed R$ 6,000 a week
of work, Pix would lead.

That last question matters. **The opportunity cost argument is an invitation to check the inputs,
not a verdict.** Júlia may know something about the instalment market that the estimate missed,
and the sheet is the place to put it. People-leadership lesson 22 is about the conflict with
product when the inputs are agreed and the answer is still disputed.

## The request that jumps the queue

Opportunity cost is easiest to miss when it is small and arrives one item at a time. Halfway through
the seat-hold fix, a director asks for a two-week change for a large venue, "just this once, before
anything else". Its hours look modest. Its opportunity cost is not: **every item in the queue waits
two more weeks**, and the queue costs R$ 108,000 a week while it waits, so the two weeks cost
R$ 216,000 of delay (2 × 108,000) before counting anything the change itself is worth.

That is the number to set beside the request. If the change for the venue is worth more than
R$ 216,000, it should jump the queue, and the sheet says so; if it is worth less, the request is
asking Coreto to pay the difference. Lesson 19 builds the refusal around exactly this arithmetic,
the cost of saying yes.

## Where else it appears

Every allocation in this course has an opportunity cost, and it is usually the line nobody writes.
Lesson 11's Reservations team moves four engineers whose work in Checkout and Payments stops; the
work they would have done is the price of the move, and this lesson's arithmetic is how to put a
number on it. An internal tool the Platform team builds is a feature it did not build, and lesson 15
is about one that nobody used.

**The habit is to ask, of any yes, what it is a no to**, and to price the no the same way as the yes.
The drill that follows uses the CD3 sheet on a queue it has not seen.

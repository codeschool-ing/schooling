STRIDE's list of fourteen threats has no order. A forged payment (T01) and a reminder text that
says too much (T08) sit side by side, each a line in a CSV, and nothing on the list says which one
Vereda should fix first or which one the business could live with. That is not a flaw in STRIDE;
it answers the second question and stops there. **PASTA answers the same question from the other
end: it starts with what the business stands to lose, and arrives at threats only after that.**

PASTA is the **Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis**, published by Tony UcedaVélez
and Marco Morana in *Risk Centric Threat Modeling* (Wiley, 2015). It is a sequence of seven
stages, each with defined inputs and outputs, and the seven take three different points of view.

### Threat-centric and risk-centric

The labels are the methods' own, and the difference is where the analysis is anchored:

| | STRIDE | PASTA |
|---|---|---|
| starts from | the system's drawing | the business objectives |
| asks | what can go wrong with each element? | what could stop the business doing what it is for, and how would an attacker get there? |
| produces | a list of threats, unranked | threats ranked by their impact on the objectives, with the attack paths behind them |
| needs in the room | builders and a security person | the same, plus somebody who owns the business objectives |
| costs | an afternoon | days, for a system of the portal's size |

The two are not rivals. Stage 3 of PASTA is decomposition, a DFD with trust boundaries, which is
lesson 2. Stage 4 is threat analysis, and nothing stops it from using STRIDE to enumerate the
threats. **PASTA is a frame around the work of lessons 2 and 3**, with the business at both ends.

### Why the attacker's view

Stages 4 to 6 ask the defender to reason like somebody trying to reach a goal: not "what could
go wrong with this flow", but "if I wanted every patient's record, which route would cost me
least?". That view finds something STRIDE per element does not: **chains**. T03, T12 and T09 are
three separate lines on the STRIDE list. Read as an attacker would read them, they are one path
from a phishing e-mail to every clinical note at Vereda. Stage 6 draws those paths, and lesson 5's
attack trees are the notation for it.

This is reasoning about goals and steps at the level of the design. It never needs a working
attack, and the course never writes one: the output of stage 6 is a map of where a defence would
break a path, which is a defender's document.

Put together, the seven stages at Vereda produced one artefact each. None of them is long; the
value is that each one can be checked against the one before.

| stage | what it produced at Vereda | built on |
|---|---|---|
| 1 objectives | four business objectives, with the LGPD and the 60% of bookings that go through the portal | daniel |
| 2 technical scope | the components, the vendors, the libraries, and the clinics' own network written as out of scope | bruno |
| 3 decomposition | the level 1 DFD and five use cases | lesson 2 |
| 4 threat analysis | the fourteen STRIDE threats, plus three notes on who is attacking clinics now | lesson 3, carla |
| 5 weaknesses | a CWE for each threat with a known weakness | ana |
| 6 attack modelling | three paths to every patient's record, with a control under each step | everybody |
| 7 risk and impact | a ranked list, with the phishing path first and the webhook second | daniel, carla |

Two threats reach two objectives each. T07 and T09 both expose clinical data and both put Vereda
on the wrong side of the LGPD, which is why they ranked higher than their technical description
suggested.

And one threat reaches none. **T04, an exam PDF overwritten in storage, is connected to no
objective**, because nobody in stage 1 wrote that the integrity of exams matters. It does: a
physiotherapist treating a patient from the wrong X-ray report is a clinical risk, not a technical
one. The picture found a missing objective, which is exactly the kind of error stage 1 exists to
catch. The fix was a fifth line in stage 1: *clinical records are accurate*. T04 and T05 both
attach to it.

### What did not change

The threat list itself barely moved. Stage 4 added context to existing threats rather than new
ones, and stage 5 named weaknesses for threats already found. What changed was the **order**, and
the reasons behind it. At a small company, that is the typical outcome: STRIDE finds the threats,
and PASTA's stages 1 and 7 decide which to deal with first.

The insurer renewed its contract with a clause it had not used before: the right to audit how its
beneficiaries' data is handled. Its auditor will spend three days at Vereda in November 2026. This
lesson is about the five weeks before that, and it uses the threat model the way an audit uses
everything: as something to be checked.

`security-fundamentals` lesson 13 describes how an audit runs, from planning to report. Here the
question is narrower: **what does a threat model give an audit, and what does an audit ask of a
threat model?**

This is a **second-party audit**: a customer checking a supplier against what the supplier promised
it. It is not a certification. Nothing will be issued at the end that anybody else can rely on, and
the criteria are the questionnaire Vereda answered in lesson 13, plus the controls those answers
named. That makes the scope a negotiation between two parties rather than a standard's definition.

### Scope is drawn, not described

The insurer's first draft of the scope said "the patient portal and related systems". Each side read
that differently. To the insurer, related systems included the clinics' computers, where staff open
exam PDFs. To bruno, it meant the portal's own servers.

A scope drawn on the model has three advantages over one written in a sentence:

- **Every element is in or out.** "Is the reminder worker in scope?" has an answer you can point at,
  and so does every flow that crosses the line.
- **The line is a trust boundary in another sense.** What is outside is relied on, not tested. The
  gateway is relied on through its SOC 2 report, and the SMS provider through its contract. The
  auditor will ask how each reliance is justified, and the answer is already in lesson 13.
- **The exclusions are visible.** The clinics' premises are out because the insurer chose not to
  audit them, and the drawing says so. A scope that leaves something out silently is the one that
  surprises somebody in the report.

### Period and criteria

A scope has two more dimensions. **The period**: the auditor will look at evidence from 1 September
2026, when the model started, to the fieldwork. Anything older does not exist for this audit, and
anything promised for after it will be recorded as a promise. **The criteria**: the 60 answers in
the questionnaire. An audit measures a gap between what was said and what is so, and the
questionnaire is what was said.

That last point is why lesson 13 insisted on the plan column. Vereda answered "planned for phase 1"
to the second-factor question, not "yes". The auditor will check that the plan exists and is moving.
Had it answered yes, the same evidence would be a finding.

Eleven controls came out of lesson 8's requirements, each with a cost and an estimate of what it
removes from which risk. They live in `controls.csv`, one line per control and risk, because one
control can cut several risks:

```
control,name,cost_per_year,risk,reduction
C1,second factor for staff,3000,T03,0.8
C2,console on the clinic network only,6000,T03,0.5
C3,webhook signature and amount check,1400,T01,0.95
C4,ownership check on exam downloads,2000,T07,0.9
C5,least-privilege account for the worker,500,T13,0.9
C6,isolated viewer for exam PDFs,18000,T14,0.8
C7,sign-in rate limit and breached passwords,2500,T02,0.6
C8,reminder text without the clinic,200,T08,0.9
C9,upload size and type limit,800,T10,0.9
C10,daily cap on reminder messages,800,T11,0.8
C11,receptionist role without clinical notes,3500,T03,0.3
```

`prioritise.py` computes each control's value on its own, divides by its cost, and sorts:

### What the ranking says

**The best value is the cheapest control.** C5, a database account for the worker that can read
bookings and nothing else, costs R$ 500 a year and removes R$ 13,500 of expected loss from T13. A
change of permissions is cheap because nothing has to be built.

**C1 saves the most in absolute terms**, R$ 60,000 a year, because it cuts the largest risk. A team
looking only at savings would put it first; a team looking only at ratios puts it second. Both
agree it is near the top, and that agreement is worth more than the exact position.

**C6 is the only control that costs more than it saves**: R$ 18,000 a year for an isolated PDF
viewer that removes R$ 7,200 of T14. On the numbers, Vereda should not buy it. That does not
close T14; it moves it to a different decision, accept or transfer, which lesson 12 records.

**C3, the webhook fix this course started from, is seventh.** It saves four times its cost, which is
an easy yes, and it ranks low only because T01's expected loss was modest in lesson 9.

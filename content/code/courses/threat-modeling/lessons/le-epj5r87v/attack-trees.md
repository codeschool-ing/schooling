An attack tree puts **one goal at the root** and every way to reach it underneath, broken down
until each leaf is a single step somebody could estimate. Bruce Schneier described the notation in
1999, and lesson 4's stage 6 used a simplified version of it. The defender's reason for drawing one
is specific: **to see which defences cut which routes, and what the cheapest remaining route
costs.**

### How a tree is built

Each node below the root is a sub-goal, and each has one of two relationships with its children:

- **OR**: any one child is enough. "Mark a booking paid without paying" can be reached by forging
  the webhook, *or* through the console, *or* through the database.
- **AND**: every child is needed. "Forge the webhook" needs both learning its address *and* sending
  a request the portal accepts.

Leaves carry an estimate the team agrees on: effort in days, cost in money, skill needed, or just
possible and impossible. Here is the tree for one goal at Vereda, with effort in days:

The goal comes from the business, as PASTA's stage 1 would have it: marking a booking paid without
paying is money lost and a booking that looks legitimate. The three branches are three threats
already on the list, read as steps. Two leaves have no threat id, because they are not weaknesses:
an address can be learnt, and a process can be taken over, wherever there is software.

### Keeping it a defender's document

An attack tree describes routes at the level of the design: "take over a staff account", not how.
That is enough for every decision the tree supports, and it is all this course draws. A tree whose
leaves are tools and commands has turned into a plan for an attack, which nobody deciding where to
spend a budget needs, and which is the wrong document to leave in a repository.

Three habits keep a tree useful:

1. **One goal per tree.** "Compromise the portal" is not a goal; "read every clinical note" and
   "mark a booking paid" are, and their trees share some branches and not others.
2. **Stop decomposing when a leaf can be estimated.** A leaf two people can agree on is detailed
   enough. Deeper than that, the tree is guessing at an implementation.
3. **Write the controls next to the leaves they make impossible.** Then the tree answers "what if
   we did this?", which the next section computes.

### Where trees come from

Rarely from nothing. The leaves of a good tree are threats from STRIDE and steps from the use cases
of PASTA's stage 3, rearranged around a goal. A team that has done lesson 3 has most of the leaves
already, and building the tree is mostly deciding which ones connect.

### What the drawing already says

Before any method is applied, the drawing says four things that the sketch did not:

- **Eleven of the twelve flows cross a boundary.** Only flow 11, the worker reading the database,
  stays inside one zone. That is normal for a web application, and it means almost every arrow
  will collect threats in lesson 3.
- **Three different processes write to one data store.** The portal, the console and the worker
  all reach the records database, flows 5, 9 and 11. If they share one database account, the
  weakest of the three decides what an attacker who takes it over can do. Lesson 1's third fact
  says at least one of them uses the owner's password.
- **Flow 7 is the only flow from a vendor into Vereda.** Everything else Vereda sends out or
  receives from people. Payment state arrives this way and nowhere else, which is why the
  question of who may send it is so sharp.
- **Flows 9 and 10 carry every patient's data to the console.** A receptionist needs the agenda;
  do they need clinical notes and exams? The drawing does not answer it. It makes the question
  impossible to miss.

### Drawn as it should be, checked as it is

The clinic network boundary around the staff is drawn as the design intended: the console is for
the clinics. Lesson 1's first fact says the console also answers from the internet. There are two
honest ways to draw that, and both are better than drawing the intention and saying nothing: add
a flow from the internet to the console and mark it as a finding, or keep the drawing and write
the gap in the model's list of assumptions to check. Lesson 6, on the attack surface, takes the
first route and measures what it adds.

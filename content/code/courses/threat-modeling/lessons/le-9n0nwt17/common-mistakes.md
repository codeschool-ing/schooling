A badly drawn DFD does not look wrong. It looks like a DFD, and the threats it hides are the ones
nobody asks about later. Three mistakes break the rules of the notation and are easy to spot once
you know them:

Four more keep the rules and still hide something, and they are the ones worth practising:

**One box for "the backend".** A single process standing for the portal, the console and the
worker merges three things with different users, different privileges and different exposure.
The boundary between the public portal and the staff console disappears, and so does the
question of whether a patient can reach staff functions.

**Arrows without direction, or with two heads.** A line with an arrowhead at each end says "they
talk". Threats depend on direction: who sends the webhook and who receives it is the whole
question of flow 7. Draw a request and its response as two flows when they carry different data
(flows 1 and 2), and as one when the response carries nothing that matters.

**A store with no reader.** Logs written by the portal and read by nobody look harmless in the
drawing and are not: somebody reads them eventually, often a support tool or a vendor, and the
flow they leave by is the one carrying every patient's e-mail address. If you draw a store, draw
who reads it, or write down that nobody does.

**Boundaries drawn round boxes rather than round trust.** A boundary around "the database
server" because it is a separate machine is an infrastructure diagram. The question is whether
trust changes, and the private network boundary at the portal is drawn because reaching it
should require being inside Vereda's cloud, not because the database runs on its own machine.

### A drawing two people disagree about

The best sign that a DFD is useful is an argument about it. "The worker doesn't read the database
directly, it calls the portal's API": then the drawing is wrong, flow 11 goes to the portal, and
the worker never needed the database password at all. Whether that is true is a fact about the
system, and the drawing is what made somebody check it. A diagram that everybody nods at in
silence has usually not been read.

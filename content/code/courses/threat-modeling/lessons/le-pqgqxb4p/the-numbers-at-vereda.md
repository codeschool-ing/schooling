The arithmetic fits in a short program. `risk.py` reads `risks.csv`, multiplies the two best
guesses for each risk, sorts by the result and prints each risk's share of the total:


Three things in that table were not visible before it existed:

- **One risk is more than half of the total.** T03, the phished staff account reaching every
  record, is R$ 75,000 of R$ 139,400, 53.8%. That is the attack path of lesson 4, and its first
  place in PASTA's stage 7 now has a number behind it.
- **The next three are close.** T07, T13 and the pair T02 and T14 sit between R$ 9,000 and R$
  16,000 a year. At the precision of these estimates, that is a four-way tie, and ordering them by
  this table alone would be reading more into it than it holds.
- **The bottom four together are under R$ 16,000.** T01, T08, T10 and T11 are real, and all four
  are cheap per year. If their fixes are cheap too, they get done because they are easy; if not,
  this table says they can wait.

### T01, at the bottom

The forged webhook, the threat this course started from, is sixth of nine. The estimate behind it
is modest: once in two years, R$ 12,000 an event, because the clinics would notice unpaid sessions
within weeks. A ranking that surprises the team is worth a second look rather than an immediate
correction, and the second look found the estimate defensible. The webhook still gets fixed, and
lesson 11 says why: its fix is the cheapest on the list.

### Committed with its reasons

`risks.csv` is a file somebody will argue with, and that is what it is for. Each change to an
estimate arrives in its own commit, and the commit message carries the reason: "T02 down to 4 a
year: rate limiting shipped in March". A year of such commits is a record of what the team learnt,
which no spreadsheet overwritten in place can show.

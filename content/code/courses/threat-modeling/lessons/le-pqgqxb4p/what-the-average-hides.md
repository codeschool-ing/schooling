way would be a mistake. T02 is a patient account taken over six times a year at R$ 1,500 each. T14
is a crafted PDF attacking a clinic computer about once in thirteen years at R$ 120,000. The
average is identical and the years are not.

The chance that each happens at least once in a given year makes the difference concrete:

```
(.venv) ana@vm:~/tm/portal-model$ python3 -c 'import math; print(1 - math.exp(-6), 1 - math.exp(-0.075))'
0.9975212478233336 0.07225651367144714
```

**T02 happens in practically every year: 99.75%. T14 happens in about 7% of years.** In 93 years
out of a hundred, T14 costs nothing, and in the others it costs R$ 120,000 or more.

### Why it matters for decisions

A loss that arrives every year in small amounts is **a cost of doing business**. It belongs in the
budget, it can be planned for, and the question is whether a control is cheaper than the loss.

A loss that arrives rarely in a large amount is **a question of survival**. For a company of
Vereda's size, R$ 120,000 in one year may be a quarter's profit. The question is not only whether a
control pays for itself on average, but whether the company can absorb the bad year if it comes,
and whether somebody else, an insurer, should carry part of it. That is lesson 12's "transfer".

### What to record beside the average

Expected loss is the right number for adding risks up and comparing controls. **It is not enough
on its own for any risk whose loss could be large relative to what the company can take.** For
those, record a second number: the loss in a bad year, the kind that happens one year in ten or one
in twenty. Lesson 10's simulation produces exactly that number, for every risk and for all of them
together, from the ranges this lesson wrote down.

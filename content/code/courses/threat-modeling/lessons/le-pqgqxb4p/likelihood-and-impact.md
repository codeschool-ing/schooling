title: Likelihood and impact

Lessons 4 and 5 ranked threats with words and with scores out of ten, and lesson 5 showed two
careful people giving the same threat averages 2.2 points apart. The way out is not a better
scale. It is **asking questions whose answers are quantities**, which can be estimated, argued
about with evidence, and checked against what actually happens.

Risk has two halves, and DREAD was right to separate them:

- **Likelihood**: how often the event is expected. Written as **events per year**, not as a
  percentage or a word. "0.3 a year" means about three times in ten years, and can be compared
  with Vereda's own history and with what carla reads about other clinics.
- **Impact**: what one event costs when it happens. Written in **reais**, with everything that
  goes into it listed, so somebody else can disagree with a line rather than with the total.

### Why events per year

"Likely" means different things to different people, and so does "30%". Thirty percent of what:
of years, of attempts, of attackers? A frequency has a unit, and the unit makes the claim
checkable. T02, patient accounts taken over, is estimated at **6 a year** because the support
inbox received seven such complaints last year and five the year before. That is evidence, and
a number the team can update next year.

### What goes into an impact

The cost of one event is a list, and listing it is most of the work. For T03, a staff account
phished and every record read, the team wrote:

| | R$ |
|---|---|
| the investigation: an outside specialist for two weeks | 40,000 |
| notifying the ANPD and nine thousand patients, as the LGPD requires | 15,000 |
| legal advice, and defending complaints | 60,000 |
| patients who leave, a year of their sessions | 100,000 |
| staff time, rebuilding accounts, the clinics' lost mornings | 35,000 |
| **total** | **250,000** |

Every line is a guess, and every line can be challenged on its own. The patients-who-leave line is
the widest: daniel thought fifty patients would leave, carla thought two hundred, and they wrote
down the middle and noted the disagreement. That note matters more than the total, and lesson 10
gives it a place.

### Which threats get numbers

Not all seventeen. Putting numbers on a threat costs an argument, and the argument is worth having
for the threats where the order of magnitude is in doubt or the decision depends on it. Vereda
estimated nine: the ones with the largest possible losses, plus T02, T08, T10 and T11, which were
cheap to estimate because they had already happened.

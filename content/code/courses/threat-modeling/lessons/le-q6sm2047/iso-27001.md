ISO/IEC 27001:2022 asks for two things, and a threat model meets both of them in different places.
`security-fundamentals` lesson 14 describes the standard; what matters here is where the model's
files land when an auditor or a customer reads them.

### The clauses: the model is evidence of a process

The body of the standard, clauses 4 to 10, describes a management system. Two of its requirements are
what this course has been doing since lesson 3:

| clause | what it requires | where it is in Vereda's repository |
|---|---|---|
| **6.1.2** risk assessment | identify risks, with owners; analyse their consequences and likelihood; evaluate them against criteria | `threats.csv`, `risks.csv`, `fair.py` and daniel's appetite |
| **6.1.3** risk treatment | choose treatment options and the controls they need; compare them with Annex A; produce a statement of applicability; get the risk owners' approval of the plan and of the residual risk | `controls.csv`, `prioritise.py`, `mapping.csv` and `decisions/` |

The last part of 6.1.3 is worth reading twice: **the risk owners approve the residual risk**. That
is RA-001 and RA-002 from lesson 12, signed by the person who owns each risk and dated. A company
that accepted risks in a corridor has nothing to show for that clause; Vereda has three files and
their git history.

Eleven controls land on **ten Annex A controls**, three organisational and seven technological. Two
pairs share a reference: C1 and C7 are both about authentication, so both are 8.5; C3 and C9 are

requirements. One reference, 8.7, protection against malware, is reached only by the control Vereda
decided not to buy.

### What the empty themes say

**No control is about people or premises.** That is not an omission in the mapping; it is a fact
about the model. The model drew a web portal and asked what could go wrong in it, so its answers are
things the portal does.

It matters most at T03, the largest risk: a receptionist phished into giving away a password. The
model's answer is C1, a second factor, which removes 80% of the expected loss. Annex A has another
answer to phishing, **6.3, information security awareness, education and training**, and the
mapping shows that Vereda has nothing filed there. The insurer's questionnaire asks about training
in question 31. The honest answer is no, and the mapping found that before the insurer did.

Whether to add training is a decision like the others, made on what it would remove and what it
would cost. The mapping does not make it; it puts it on the table.

### The statement of applicability

The document that answers every one of the 93 is the **statement of applicability**: for each Annex
A control, whether it applies, why, its status and where the evidence is. The model fills only a few
of its rows, but those rows are the best-argued ones in it:

| control | applies | justification | status | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.5 secure authentication | yes | T03, T02 | C1 phase 1, C7 phase 2 | R05 and R03, R04 tests |
| 8.7 protection against malware | yes | T14 | **partly**: C6 not bought | RA-001, signed 2026-10-01 |
| 7.1 physical security perimeters | yes | the clinics' premises, outside this model | decided outside this model | not in this repository |

The 8.7 row is the one auditors read most carefully, because "partly" is where gaps hide. Here it
points at a signed acceptance with a review date and the measures in place instead, which is what
the row should point at. The 7.1 row is the reminder that a threat model of one system is **an input
to the statement of applicability, not the statement itself**.

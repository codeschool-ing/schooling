title: Reserves for what is known and what is not
version: 1
---

Expected values are most useful for one decision: **how much margin to put in the plan**. A margin that is a guess — "add 20%" — is either too large, and spends money on nothing, or too small, and fails at the first surprise. A margin built from the risk register can be explained.

## Contingency reserve: for the known unknowns

The **contingency reserve** covers risks that have been identified: the known unknowns. Its size comes from the register. For the Agenda team the four threats' expected values add up to **9 working days**. The opportunity, if exploited, would give back 1.6, so the net expected cost is **7.4**.

A team that adds about 9 days to its 20.5-day PERT mean from lesson 9 is not padding the estimate. It is pricing four specific events, each of which has a name, an owner, a probability and a response. When risk C does not happen — the data turns out clean — its share of the reserve is released, and the plan can say so.

The reserve is held at the level that owns the risks, often by the project manager or the team as a whole, and **not spread across tasks**. A reserve divided among tasks is spent by them, whatever happens, because work expands to fill the time available; a reserve held centrally is spent only when a risk actually materialises.

## Management reserve: for the unknown unknowns

No register is complete. The **management reserve** covers what nobody foresaw — the unknown unknowns. It is not computed from the register, because by definition its contents are not on it; organisations set it as a policy, often a percentage of the budget, and it is controlled above the project, by the sponsor or the board. Using it is a decision somebody senior makes, with a reason recorded.

## Reserves and the critical path

A reserve of time is only useful where time matters. In lesson 6's network, a day added to activity B, which had a day of slack, changes nothing; a day added to D, on the critical path, moves the end date. **Risks on the critical path deserve the reserve; risks off it may be absorbed by slack.** The practice in critical chain project management, Eliyahu Goldratt's method from 1997, is to collect the margin into a single buffer at the end of the critical path rather than in each task, for the reason given above.

## What to tell the sponsor

The conversation that follows is the one lesson 9 described for estimates: a range with its reasons. "20.5 days of expected work; about 9 more if the four risks we have identified behave as we expect, with an owner and a response for each; and a management reserve held by you for what nobody has thought of." That is a plan a sponsor can understand and challenge, which a bare number with 20% added is not.

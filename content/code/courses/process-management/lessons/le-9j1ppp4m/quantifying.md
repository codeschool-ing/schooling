Five risks came out of the Agenda team's pre-mortem. Four are threats and one is an opportunity:

| risk | probability | impact, working days |
|---|---|---|
| A — the payment provider changes its API | 30% | 10 |
| B — the one developer who knows billing leaves | 10% | 20 |
| C — clinic data needs cleaning before migration | 50% | 6 |
| D — the app store rejects the first release | 20% | 5 |
| E — an existing calendar library fits as it is | 40% | −4 |

There are two common ways to turn that table into an order of attention.

## The matrix

The quick one places each risk on a **probability-impact matrix**: a grid, usually five by five, where each axis runs from very low to very high, and the cells are coloured by how much attention they deserve.

The matrix is easy to explain and good for a first sort. Its weakness shows in the Agenda team's own grid: B, the developer leaving, has the largest impact of all and still sits in the middle band, because its probability is low. **A matrix multiplies two coarse ranks**, and in doing so it can hide a rare catastrophe among everyday nuisances. It also cannot add risks up, because a rank of 8 plus a rank of 9 means nothing.

## Expected value

The quantitative way multiplies the actual numbers. The **expected monetary value**, or EMV, of a risk is its probability times its impact; with impacts in days, it is an expected number of days.

A, at 30% of 10 days, is worth **3 days**. B, at 10% of 20, is worth **2**. C, at 50% of 6, is worth **3**. D is worth **1**. The opportunity E, at 40% of minus 4, is worth **minus 1.6**. Unlike ranks, expected values can be added: the four threats together are worth **9 days**, and with the opportunity, **7.4**.

## In the workbook

With the risks in rows 2 to 6 — description, probability, impact — one column computes each expected value and three cells give the totals. These are what LibreOffice returned:

```localised
D2   =B2*C2                         3
D6   =B6*C6                         -1.6
=SUMIF(C2:C6,">0",D2:D6)            9
=SUM(D2:D6)                         7.4
=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B6,C2:C6)            7.4
```

In Portuguese the functions are `SOMASE`, `SOMA` and `SOMARPRODUTO`, with semicolons between the arguments.

## What an expected value means

An expected value of 9 days does not mean the project will lose 9 days. Each risk either happens or it does not, so the project will lose 0 days, or 6, or 10, or some combination. The chance that **none** of the four threats happens is 70% × 90% × 50% × 80%, about **25%**; in three cases out of four at least one of them will. The expected value is the right number for **sizing a reserve** across many risks, which is the next-but-one section. For a single large risk such as B, it is the wrong number to plan around: nobody loses "2 days" of a departing developer. B needs a response of its own.

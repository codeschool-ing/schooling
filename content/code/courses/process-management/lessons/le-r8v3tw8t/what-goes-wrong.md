---
title: What usually goes wrong
version: 1
---

Scrum's rules are few, so most of what goes wrong is not a rule being broken but a rule being followed in letter and missed in purpose. Six patterns account for most of the trouble, and each one has a symptom you can see from outside the team.

## The Daily Scrum becomes a status report

Each Developer reports to the Scrum Master, or worse to a manager in the room, what they did yesterday. Nobody talks to anybody else, and nobody adapts the plan. **The symptom is that people look at one person while they speak.** The fix is to bring the meeting back to its purpose — inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal — which usually means walking the board from right to left, asking what is closest to done.

## The Product Owner is a proxy

The person called Product Owner has no authority to decide; every real decision goes to a director who never attends. The team waits days for answers, and the backlog order changes after every steering meeting. **The symptom is "I need to check" as the answer to most questions.** Scrum cannot fix this from inside the team; it is an organisational decision about who owns the product.

## There is no Sprint Goal

The Sprint Backlog is a list of unrelated items chosen because they fit. With no goal, the team can only succeed by finishing everything, and every surprise becomes a failure. **The symptom is a review that walks through tickets instead of showing a change in the product.**

## The Sprint is a small waterfall

Analysis in the first days, coding in the middle, testing in the last two days. The burndown is flat until the end and drops in a cliff, and the testers work overtime every second Thursday. The fix is to finish items one or two at a time, with testing inside each item rather than after all of them.

## Velocity becomes a target

Management asks for velocity to rise each Sprint, so it does: estimates grow, and the same work is worth more points. **A velocity that rises steadily with no change in what is delivered is inflation, not improvement.** Lesson 10 explains why points cannot be compared across teams or used as a target, and lesson 13 returns to what happens to any measure that becomes one.

## The retrospective changes nothing

The same three complaints appear every fortnight and nothing is done about any of them. The guide's remedy is concrete: the most helpful improvement goes into the next Sprint Backlog, with somebody responsible for it. A retrospective that produces one change that actually happens is worth more than one that produces ten ideas.

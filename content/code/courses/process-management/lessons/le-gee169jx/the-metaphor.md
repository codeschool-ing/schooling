title: What Cunningham meant by debt
version: 1
---

**Technical debt** is the most successful metaphor software has given to management, and one of the most misused. It came from Ward Cunningham, in a 1992 experience report about a financial system called WyCash. A team that ships code reflecting its current, partial understanding of a problem, he wrote, is **borrowing**: it gets the feature sooner, and it will have to pay later, when the understanding improves and the code has to change to match it. A little debt speeds development, as long as it is paid back promptly. The danger is debt that is not repaid, because **every minute spent working on not-quite-right code is interest on that debt**.

## Principal and interest

The metaphor is useful because it maps onto two numbers that a manager already understands:

- **The principal** is what it would cost to fix the code now: the refactoring, the rewrite of a module, the migration to a supported library.
- **The interest** is what the code costs every week it is not fixed: the extra hours each change takes, the incidents it causes, the developer who spends a morning understanding a function before touching it.

A debt with a high principal and no interest — a messy module nobody ever touches — costs nothing to keep. A debt with a small principal and high interest — a flaky test suite that wastes half an hour of every developer's day — is the one to pay first. **The interest, not the size of the mess, decides the priority**, and this lesson's fifth section puts numbers on it.

## Cunningham's own correction

Years later, Cunningham said that the metaphor had been widely taken to mean something he never intended: permission to write bad code knowingly and clean it up later. What he meant was code written well, as well as the team's understanding at the time allowed, that **needs to change as the understanding grows**. The debt is the gap between what the code says and what the team now knows. That distinction matters, because it means **all teams that learn accumulate debt**, including excellent ones, and that the cure is not to stop borrowing but to keep paying.

## The quadrant

Martin Fowler, in 2009, sorted the ways debt arises along two axes: whether it was taken on **deliberately** or **inadvertently**, and whether it was **prudent** or **reckless**.

The prudent, deliberate cell is a business decision: ship now to meet a date, knowing the cost, with a plan to pay. The prudent, inadvertent cell is learning: *now we know how we should have done it*. The reckless cells are something else — corners cut out of haste or ignorance — and calling them debt can make them sound like a decision somebody made.

Most outages are caused by a change: a deployment, a configuration edit, a patch, a certificate renewed with the wrong name. So operations organisations have always controlled change, and ITIL's practice for it is one of the most visible parts of the framework. Its name tells a story. Earlier versions called it **change management**; ITIL 4 first called the practice *change control* and then renamed it **change enablement**, a word chosen to say that its purpose is to let changes happen safely, not to stop them.

ITIL 4 defines a change as **the addition, modification or removal of anything that could have a direct or indirect effect on services**. That covers code, infrastructure, configuration and documentation alike.

- A **standard change** is low-risk, well understood, done often and **pre-authorised**: once its procedure has been approved, each instance is simply carried out and recorded. Adding a new clinic to the app from a tested template is a candidate.
- A **normal change** has to be assessed and authorised before it is made. Who authorises it depends on its risk: a peer for a small change, a manager for a larger one, a group for the riskiest. ITIL 4 calls whoever decides the **change authority**.
- An **emergency change** must be made as soon as possible — to fix a priority 1 incident, or to close a security hole being exploited. It is authorised quickly by a smaller authority, and reviewed afterwards with particular care.

## The change advisory board

Earlier versions gave normal changes a default authority: the **change advisory board**, or CAB, a group of people from operations, development and the business who met regularly — often weekly — to review the changes planned for the coming days. Many organisations still run one. ITIL 4 no longer prescribes it; it asks for authority to be placed where it can decide well and quickly, which for most changes is close to the work.

## The useful move

The most valuable thing change enablement can do is to **move changes from normal to standard**. A change that has been made successfully fifty times with the same automated procedure is not risky, and making it wait for a weekly meeting adds a week of lead time and no safety.

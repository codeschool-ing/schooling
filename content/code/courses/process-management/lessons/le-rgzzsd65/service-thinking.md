Every method in lessons 1 to 7 is about building something: a project with a start and an end, or a product team adding features. The software they produce then runs for years, and somebody has to keep it running, answer the people who use it and change it without breaking it. **ITIL is the most widely used description of that work.** Where PMBOK is the vocabulary of projects, ITIL is the vocabulary of operations, and a large organisation's IT department is usually organised around it.

## A short history

ITIL — originally the *IT Infrastructure Library* — began in the late 1980s as a set of books from a UK government agency, the same family that produced PRINCE2 in lesson 7. Its third version, in 2007, organised everything around a **service lifecycle** of five stages: strategy, design, transition, operation and continual improvement. **ITIL 4**, published in 2019, replaced the lifecycle with a more flexible model and brought in ideas from agile, Lean and DevOps. Like PRINCE2, it is now owned by PeopleCert.

## What a service is

ITIL 4 defines a service as **a means of enabling value co-creation by facilitating outcomes that customers want to achieve, without the customer having to manage specific costs and risks**. The sentence is dense, and its last clause is the important one. A clinic that uses the Agenda app wants patients to book appointments. It does not want to run servers, patch databases or keep backups; the service is what takes those costs and risks off its hands.

That definition shifts attention from **what is built** to **what is delivered, continuously**. A feature finished in a Sprint is the start of a service, not the end of a job.

## The parts of ITIL 4

ITIL 4 describes a **service value system**, and four of its parts are worth knowing by name:

- **Seven guiding principles**: focus on value; start where you are; progress iteratively with feedback; collaborate and promote visibility; think and work holistically; keep it simple and practical; optimise and automate. Several would fit in the agile manifesto, which is deliberate.
- **The service value chain**: six activities — plan, improve, engage, design and transition, obtain or build, deliver and support — that are combined in different orders for different kinds of work.
- **Practices**: 34 sets of organisational resources for particular work, such as incident management, problem management, change enablement and service level management. They replace the processes of earlier versions.
- **Continual improvement**, which applies at every level.

The next four sections take the practices an architect meets most often, in the order a user meets them: something breaks, somebody finds out why, somebody changes the system, and somebody agrees what the service must provide.

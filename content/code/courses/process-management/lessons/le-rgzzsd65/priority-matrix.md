When ten incidents arrive in an hour, somebody has to decide which to work on first, and the worst way to decide is by who complains loudest. ITIL's usual answer is to compute **priority** from two questions that can be answered separately.

## Impact and urgency

**Impact** is how much damage the incident does: how much of the service is affected, and for how many users. Booking down in all thirty clinics is high impact; one receptionist unable to print a report is low.

**Urgency** is how quickly the damage grows if nothing is done. A failure in the booking screen at eight in the morning, when patients are calling, is urgent; the same failure in a monthly report due next week is not.

High impact and high urgency give **priority 1**. One step down on either axis gives priority 2, and so on to **priority 5** for low impact and low urgency. Each priority then carries a target, set in the service level agreement of this lesson's sixth section: for example, a priority 1 must be worked on within fifteen minutes and resolved within four hours, a priority 4 within two working days.

## Why compute it at all

Writing the matrix down turns an argument into two questions somebody can answer: *how many users does it affect?* and *how fast does it get worse?* It also protects the people answering the phone. A director whose report fails gets priority 4 like anybody else, and the help desk can point to the table rather than to its own judgement.

The matrix only works if impact and urgency have **definitions** the organisation agrees on. "High impact" might mean more than one clinic, or more than 10% of users, or any failure that stops payments. Without definitions, people inflate both to get attention, every incident becomes priority 1, and the matrix sorts nothing.

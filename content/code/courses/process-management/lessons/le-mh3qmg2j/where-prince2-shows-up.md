Outside the United Kingdom, many people working in software have never seen PRINCE2. It is still worth recognising, because it appears in specific places, and an architect who meets it should know what is being asked.

## The usual places

PRINCE2 is common in the **UK public sector** and in organisations that work with it, in **Europe**, in **Australia** and in several **international organisations and agencies**, where a method that clearly separates who directs, who manages and who delivers suits a world of funders, contractors and auditors. In Brazil it appears mainly in multinationals that adopted it elsewhere, and in projects funded by international bodies that require it. A tender or a contract that mentions a *Project Board*, a *business case* reviewed at every stage, or *end stage reports* is written in PRINCE2's vocabulary whether or not it names the method.

## PRINCE2 Agile

In 2015 the method's owners published **PRINCE2 Agile**, which combines PRINCE2's governance with agile delivery. The idea is a sensible division of labour. The upper levels keep PRINCE2's structure: the business case, the Project Board, stages and tolerances. The delivery level runs Scrum, Kanban or another agile method inside each stage. The tolerances are adjusted so that the parts agile work expects to vary — scope, mainly — get wide tolerances, while time and cost are held tighter, which is the fixed-date, variable-scope arrangement lesson 1 described for contracts.

## What an architect does in a PRINCE2 project

An architect is rarely named in PRINCE2's roles. In practice the architect contributes in three places:

- **The business case**, where the cost and risk of the technical options is part of the justification. A business case that ignores the cost of running the system for five years is incomplete, and the architect is usually the person who can say so.
- **Product descriptions**, which carry quality criteria. Non-functional requirements belong there, in the same way lesson 2 put them in a Definition of Done.
- **Project assurance**, the independent check on behalf of the board that the project is being run properly. Technical assurance — is the design sound, are the risks real — is a role an architect is often asked to play.

The Senior Supplier on the board is the person to work with. That role answers for whether the solution can be built, and an architect's assessment is the evidence the Senior Supplier brings to the board.

title: Reading a process written in another vocabulary
version: 1

An architect joining a large organisation or bidding for a public contract will meet documents written in the vocabularies of lessons 6 and 7, and often in several of them at once. The words differ; most of the ideas underneath are the same handful. Recognising them is a practical skill: it tells you which decisions have already been made, who makes the next one, and where your work will be judged.

## A translation table

| you read | the idea | the nearest thing in a Scrum team |
|---|---|---|
| business case (PRINCE2), charter (PMBOK) | why the project exists and what it must achieve | the Product Goal |
| Project Board, sponsor, steering committee | who decides on behalf of the organisation | the Product Owner, one level up |
| stage, phase, milestone | a point at which the project is reviewed and continued or stopped | the Sprint Review, at a larger scale |
| tolerance, exception | how far the plan may move before somebody above is told | the Sprint Goal's room to renegotiate scope |
| product description, work package | a defined piece of output with its quality criteria | a backlog item with acceptance criteria and the Definition of Done |
| elaboration, lifecycle architecture milestone | the risky parts proved in running code | spikes and a walking skeleton in the first Sprints |
| change control, change request | how the baseline is changed | replanning at Sprint Planning |

## Three questions for any process document

Whatever the vocabulary, three questions get to what matters:

- **Who can stop the project, and when do they look?** A Project Board at stage boundaries, a steering committee each quarter, a Product Owner every Sprint. That tells you when a technical risk has to be on the table.
- **How is a change approved?** A change request with an impact assessment, or the next planning session. That tells you how expensive it will be to change your mind about a design.
- **What counts as finished?** An approved document, a demonstrated product, a passed acceptance test. That tells you what evidence your work will be judged by.

## Working inside it

The temptation for somebody trained in agile methods is to fight the vocabulary. It is usually more effective to **use it**: write the architecture's risks into the business case, put the quality criteria into the product descriptions, ask for an elaboration-style stage whose deliverable is a working skeleton rather than a document. Each formal method in these two lessons has a place where that work fits, and finding it is faster than arguing about which method the organisation should have chosen.

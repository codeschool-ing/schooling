The **Rational Unified Process** came from Rational Software in the late 1990s, from the same circle of people who created UML: Grady Booch, Ivar Jacobson and James Rumbaugh, with Philippe Kruchten leading the work on the process itself. IBM bought Rational in 2003 and RUP became an IBM product, sold with tools and a large web of guidance. It was the most influential software process of the years just before agile, and much of its vocabulary is still around.

RUP described itself in three phrases: *use-case driven*, **architecture-centric**, and *iterative and incremental*. All three matter, and the second is the one that makes RUP interesting to an architect.

## Four phases, each ending in a decision

RUP divides a project into four phases. Each ends at a **milestone** where the project is reviewed and a decision is made to continue or not.

- **Inception** establishes the business case and the scope: what the system is for, who uses it, the main use cases, a rough estimate. It ends when the stakeholders agree on the objectives.
- **Elaboration** analyses the problem and **establishes the architecture**, by building and testing the parts of the system that carry the most technical risk. It ends with the **lifecycle architecture** milestone: an executable architecture that has been proved to work.
- **Construction** builds the rest of the system on that architecture, in iterations.
- **Transition** delivers the system to its users: beta tests, training, migration, the fixes that real use reveals.

## The hump chart

The famous RUP diagram puts the phases across the top and the **disciplines** — requirements, analysis and design, implementation, test, deployment and others — down the side, and draws the effort of each discipline as a band that swells and shrinks over time.

The chart's message is that **a RUP phase is not an activity**. Requirements work peaks early but does not stop; testing runs through every phase; there is implementation in elaboration, because building the risky parts is how the architecture is proved. Every phase is made of iterations, each of which includes some of every discipline. That is the opposite of the waterfall of lesson 1, where each phase was one activity.

## Architecture-centric

Elaboration is RUP's lasting contribution. Its rule is that **the architecture is a deliverable that is demonstrated, not a document that is approved**. By the end of elaboration the team has built a thin, working skeleton of the system that exercises the riskiest decisions — the integration with the payment provider, the data model under load, the security model — and has measured it. Construction is then mostly a matter of filling in.

Kruchten also gave architects a lasting tool from this period: the **4+1 view model** of 1995, which describes a system's architecture from four views — logical, process, development and physical — tied together by a fifth, a set of scenarios. It is still taught in architecture courses, and the scenarios are RUP's use cases.

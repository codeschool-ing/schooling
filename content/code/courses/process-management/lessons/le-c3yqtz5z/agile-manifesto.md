In February 2001 seventeen people who had each been promoting a lighter way of building software — Extreme Programming, Scrum, DSDM, Crystal, Feature-Driven Development and others — met at a ski resort in Utah and wrote one page. It is short enough to quote whole, and quoting it whole matters, because the part usually left out changes its meaning.

- **Individuals and interactions** over processes and tools
- **Working software** over comprehensive documentation
- **Customer collaboration** over contract negotiation
- **Responding to change** over following a plan

> That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.

**Every line is a preference, not a rejection.** The authors did not say documentation is worthless or plans are a mistake; they said that when the two collide, they would rather have the working software or the change. A team that reads it as permission to stop writing anything down has kept the first half of each line and thrown away the sentence that qualifies all four.

## The bet the manifesto makes

Read the four lines together and the assumption is clear: **requirements will change, and finding out early is cheaper than predicting**. So working software is the measure of progress, because it is the only artefact that tells you whether you understood the problem. The customer is a collaborator rather than a counterparty, because the customer is where the changes come from. And responding to change beats following a plan, because the plan was made when everybody knew least.

That is the opposite bet from the sequence in this lesson's third section, and it is right in the opposite conditions: a product whose users are still discovering what they need, a market that moves, a technology nobody on the team has used.

## Twelve principles, and five that carry the weight

The manifesto has a second page of twelve principles. Five of them do most of the work in the lessons that follow:

- **Deliver working software frequently**, "from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale". That sentence is where the sprint comes from.
- **Welcome changing requirements, even late in development.** This one is expensive to honour, and lesson 4 shows that it needs technical practices to be affordable.
- **Business people and developers must work together daily.** Scrum turns this into a role, the product owner, in lesson 2.
- **Working software is the primary measure of progress.** A status report that says 80% done is not working software.
- **Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility.** It is the most ignored of the twelve, and the one an architect should quote most often.

## What the manifesto does not say

It does not name a ceremony, a role, a length of iteration or a tool. Every one of those comes from a specific method — Scrum, XP, Kanban — that predates the meeting or was shaped by it. When somebody says "agile requires a daily stand-up", they mean Scrum requires one. The difference matters on the day a team wants to drop a ceremony that is not working: dropping it breaks a Scrum rule and no agile value.

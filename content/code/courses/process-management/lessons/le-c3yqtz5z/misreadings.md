title: Four misreadings that cost teams time
version: 1

The vocabulary of this lesson is used loosely, and some of the loose uses do damage. Four come up so often that it is worth meeting them before the lessons on each method.

## "Agile means no planning"

Agile methods plan more often, not less. A Scrum team plans every sprint, refines its backlog every week and reviews the plan with its stakeholders at the end of each cycle. What they avoid is a **detailed** plan for work that is months away, because detail far ahead is mostly invented. A team that answers "we are agile" to the question *"when will it be ready?"* has confused not planning with planning in a different way, and lesson 10 shows how an agile team answers it with a range.

## "Agile means no documentation"

The manifesto says working software is valued **more than** comprehensive documentation. A system that other people will operate for years still needs its decisions written down, its interfaces described and its runbooks kept current. What agile teams drop is the document written to satisfy a phase gate and never read again.

## "Waterfall is dead"

Fixed-price contracts, public tenders, regulated systems and hardware projects still plan sequentially, and many organisations that run their teams in sprints still fund them in annual budgets that assume a sequence. Calling it dead leaves you unable to work inside it, which is where a great deal of well-paid architecture happens.

## "Our process is agile because we have sprints"

A team can hold every Scrum ceremony and still work sequentially: requirements in sprint 1, design in sprint 2, code in sprints 3 to 6, testing in sprint 7. The calendar is cut into fortnights and nothing works until the end. The test is the one the manifesto gives: **is there working software at the end of each cycle, and has anybody outside the team seen it?** If not, the sprints are a sequence with shorter labels. Lesson 2 names this failure among the common ones.

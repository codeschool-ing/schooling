The argument between the sequential bet and the agile one is really an argument about information: **how much of what the project needs to know is knowable on the first day**. Two pictures put numbers on it, and both are worth knowing because people will quote them at you.

## The cone of uncertainty

Barry Boehm's 1981 book on software cost estimation measured how far estimates made at different stages of a project landed from the effort that was actually spent. Steve McConnell later redrew the result as the **cone of uncertainty**, and it is the version most people have seen.

At the initial idea, the real effort lands anywhere between **a quarter and four times** the estimate — a sixteen-fold range. Once the product is defined, the range is half to double. With the requirements done, it is about two thirds to one and a half. By the detailed design, it is within about ten per cent either way.

Two readings of the cone are common, and only one is right. The wrong one says that **time narrows it**: wait a few months and the estimates get better. McConnell is explicit that the cone narrows only when **decisions are made** that remove the variability: what the product will and will not do, which interface, which integration. A project that has been running for six months without deciding anything is still at the wide end.

The right reading has a practical consequence for anybody asked for a date. An estimate given at the initial idea is a range, and a single number quoted from that stage hides most of the range. Lessons 9 and 10 show how to give the range instead.

## The cost of a late change

The second picture is the **cost-of-change curve**: a defect or a change costs more the later it is found, and by the time the system is in production it costs many times what it would have cost in the requirements. Boehm's work is again the usual source, and numbers like "a hundred times more" circulate widely.

The direction of the claim is reasonable, and it is the strongest argument for the sequential model: if late change is that expensive, spend a great deal of effort getting the requirements right first. The precise multipliers are another matter. Laurent Bossavit's *The Leprechauns of Software Engineering* (2012) traced the most quoted figures back through their citations and found much thinner evidence than their popularity suggests, often a single old study quoted at second or third hand.

Kent Beck's *Extreme Programming Explained*, in 1999, turned the argument around. If the curve is steep, plan up front. But if a team can **flatten** it — with automated tests, simple design and continuous integration, so that changing working code late is cheap — then the reason to plan everything up front disappears. That is the technical premise under agile, and lesson 4 is about the practices that buy it.

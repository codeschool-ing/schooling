The sequential model is usually told as a straw man: a naive method from the 1970s that nobody uses any more. Both halves of that are wrong. It was not naive when it was described, and it is still how a large share of contracted software is planned, especially where a client pays a fixed price for a fixed scope.

## The shape

Work moves through phases, and each phase finishes before the next begins: **requirements, design, implementation, verification, operation**. Each phase ends with a document that the next phase takes as its input — a requirements specification, a design, the code, a test report. The documents are what make the hand-over possible, because the people in one phase are often not the people in the next.

## What Winston Royce actually wrote

The paper always cited as its origin is Winston Royce's *Managing the Development of Large Software Systems*, from 1970. Royce drew the straight sequence and then wrote that the implementation he had described **"is risky and invites failure"**. The trouble, as he put it, is that testing comes at the end, and it is the first moment the system is exercised as a whole; anything it finds that is not a coding mistake sends the work back to design or to requirements.

The rest of the paper fixes that. He added feedback between neighbouring phases, asked for a preliminary design before the analysis was finished, and recommended building the whole thing twice: a first version to learn from and a second one to deliver. The word *waterfall* is not in the paper at all; it was attached to the diagram later, and what spread was the figure without the text around it.

## What the sequence assumes

The model is a reasonable bet when three things are true:

- **The requirements can be known up front and will hold still.** A tax rule published by law, an interface to a machine that already exists, a migration whose source and target are fixed.
- **Changing something late is expensive**, so getting it right early is worth a lot of effort. Hardware, embedded systems and anything that needs certification fall here.
- **The hand-over between phases is cheap compared with the work inside them**, because the documents are good and the people reading them were not in the room when they were written.

When those hold, the sequence buys predictability: a date and a cost a client can sign, decided before most of the money is spent. When they do not, it buys the opposite. The plan is made at the moment the team knows least, and the test phase becomes the place where every wrong assumption from the first month arrives at once.

## Where it still lives

Regulated work, public procurement and fixed-price contracts tend to keep the shape even when the team inside works differently. A bid has to state a scope and a price before anybody has built anything, so the contract is sequential whatever the developers call their fortnightly meeting. An architect on that kind of project works in two vocabularies at once, and lesson 6 gives the formal one a name.

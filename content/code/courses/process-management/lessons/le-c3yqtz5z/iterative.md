The alternative to a single pass is older than the word agile by several decades. Craig Larman and Victor Basili's history of the idea, published in 2003, traces iterative and incremental development back to projects of the 1950s and 1960s, and to work at IBM and for NASA long before 2001. What changed later was the name and the publicity, not the practice.

Two words are used for it, often as if they meant the same thing. They do not.

## Incremental: finished pieces, one after another

An **incremental** plan splits the product into pieces and builds each one completely before starting the next. The first delivery is a third of the product, finished; the second adds another third. Each piece goes through every phase, so testing happens for every piece rather than once at the end, and the client can use part of the product early.

The risk incremental work leaves open is that the split was wrong. If the first piece was built on an assumption the third piece breaks, the finished first piece has to be reopened.

## Iterative: the whole thing, rough, then better

An **iterative** plan builds a rough version of the whole and then revisits it, round after round. The first round may be a prototype nobody would ship; the second fixes what the first taught. Each round is a chance to learn something about the problem and change the plan.

The risk iterative work leaves open is that nothing is finished for a long time. A client who sees three increasingly convincing prototypes and no working feature is entitled to ask what they are paying for.

## Doing both

Most teams that call themselves agile do both at once: each delivery is a **thin vertical slice** that works from the screen to the database, and the next delivery both adds a slice and improves the ones already there. That combination is what makes a short cycle useful. A slice that works can be shown to a user, and what the user says can change the next slice.

## The spiral, and risk as the driver

Barry Boehm's spiral model, described in 1986, added a reason to the loop. Each turn of the spiral starts by asking **what is the riskiest thing we do not know yet**, and the turn is spent reducing that risk: with a prototype, a simulation, a study or a piece of the real system. The spiral was written for large, expensive projects, and its lasting idea is the order of work. The unknowns that could sink the project are tackled first, while changing course is still cheap.

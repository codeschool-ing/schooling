Scaling frameworks are sold to organisations that already have the problem. The most useful thing an architect or a lead can do is often to ask whether the problem has to be solved by coordination at all.

## The criticism of SAFe

SAFe has been criticised publicly by several of the people who shaped agile methods. The arguments are consistent: that it adds layers of roles and planning that recreate the hierarchy agile set out to remove; that ten-week plans committed in a room become fixed promises; that certification by the framework's owner gives it a commercial reason to grow. Its defenders reply that large organisations will not abandon their structure overnight, and that SAFe gives them a way to start that they can actually adopt. **Both can be true in the same company.** The useful question for a given organisation is whether SAFe's events are reducing dependencies over time or only managing them forever.

## Other names you will meet

**Nexus**, from Scrum.org, extends Scrum to three to nine teams with an integration team and a few added events. **Scrum@Scale**, from Jeff Sutherland, scales by building a network of Scrum teams with scaled versions of the Scrum Master and Product Owner. **Disciplined Agile**, now owned by PMI, is a toolkit for choosing a way of working rather than a framework.

And the **Spotify model** — squads, tribes, chapters and guilds — comes from a 2012 paper by Henrik Kniberg and Anders Ivarsson describing how Spotify organised its engineering teams at the time. It was never published as a framework, the company changed it afterwards, and people who worked there have written that it described an aspiration more than daily practice. Copying the names without the conditions that made them work is a common way to rename teams and change nothing.

## Three questions to ask first

Before adopting any of these, three questions cut the problem down to size:

- **Can the work be split so that teams depend on each other less?** Reorganising into feature teams, or redrawing service boundaries so that each team owns a coherent part, removes coordination rather than managing it.
- **Does this product really need this many people?** Brooks's law applies to organisations as well as projects: fewer people with fewer dependencies frequently deliver more.
- **Which dependencies cost the most?** A month of PI Planning effort spent on a dependency that a one-week change to an interface would remove is a poor trade.

What is left after those questions is the coordination that genuinely has to happen. A framework is the right tool for that remainder, and a costly one for anything more.

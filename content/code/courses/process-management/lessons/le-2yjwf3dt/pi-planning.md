The event SAFe is best known for is **PI Planning**. A PI was a *Program Increment* until version 6.0 renamed it a **Planning Interval**; the abbreviation stayed. It is a fixed period, usually 8 to 12 weeks, made of several iterations, and it opens with two days in which every person on the release train plans the interval together.

## The shape of an interval

The common shape is ten weeks: **four two-week development iterations and a fifth, the Innovation and Planning iteration**. The IP iteration is deliberately left without planned features. It holds the train's **Inspect and Adapt** event — a demonstration of everything the train built, a look at the measures, and a problem-solving workshop — and the next PI Planning. It also absorbs what the plan did not foresee, which makes it the train's slack, and teams under pressure are tempted to fill it with features. A train that does so has a plan with no margin.

## The two days

PI Planning follows a set agenda. On the first morning the business owners present the context and the vision, Product Management presents the top features, and the System Architect presents the architectural vision and the runway. The teams then plan in breakouts: which features they can take on, broken into stories across the iterations, with their **dependencies on other teams** identified and negotiated face to face in the same room.

At the end of the first day, the draft plans are reviewed, and management resolves the problems the teams could not: scope that does not fit, a dependency nobody can meet, a risk with no owner. On the second day the teams adjust, and the train finishes with three things:

- **PI objectives** per team — what each team intends to achieve, with a business value from 1 to 10 assigned by the business owners;
- the **ART planning board**, formerly called the program board, showing every feature against the iteration in which it is planned and a string between any two teams that depend on each other;
- a **confidence vote**: everybody shows from one to five fingers for how confident they are in the plan. Low votes are discussed and the plan is reworked until the train commits.

The risks raised during the event are sorted into four groups with a mnemonic, **ROAM**: *resolved*, *owned*, *accepted* or *mitigated*. Lesson 11 gives that sorting its general form.

## Why it works, when it works

The value of PI Planning is less the plan than **the room**. Dependencies between teams, which are the expensive part of scaling, are discovered and negotiated in hours between the people who will meet them, instead of in weeks of messages. An architect who attends sees in two days every place where the design forces teams to wait for each other.

The risk is that the plan becomes a contract. Ten weeks is long enough for most of the cone of uncertainty in lesson 1 to apply, and a train that treats its PI objectives as promises rather than intentions has rebuilt the fixed plan that agile methods set out to replace, with a two-day ceremony attached.

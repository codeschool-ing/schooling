Everything in lessons 2 to 4 assumes one team of about ten people working on one product. Most software that matters is built by more than one team, and the methods do not say what to do then. Scrum's guide describes one Scrum Team; Kanban describes one board. **Scaling frameworks exist to fill that gap**, and before looking at two of them it is worth being precise about what goes wrong as teams multiply, because the frameworks are judged by how well they treat it.

## The number of conversations

Six people who all may need to talk to each other have 15 pairs between them. Twelve have 66. The number of pairs is n(n−1)/2, and it grows with the square of the headcount, while the people available to do the work grow only in proportion.

Fred Brooks made the consequence famous in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975): **adding people to a late software project makes it later.** New people need training from the people already there, and every person added is new paths through which work has to be coordinated. Splitting a large group into teams is the usual answer, and it trades one problem for another: conversations inside a team are cheap, and conversations between teams are slow, scheduled and lossy.

## Dependencies are the real cost

The cost that scaling frameworks are mostly about is **dependencies between teams**. A feature the Agenda team wants may need a change from the payments team and another from the team that owns the clinics' data. Each of those teams has its own backlog and its own priorities. The feature is now finished when the slowest of three backlogs reaches it, and the date nobody can predict is set by work nobody on the Agenda team controls.

## Conway's law

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that **organisations design systems that mirror their own communication structure**. Three teams build a system in three parts, with interfaces where the teams talk to each other. The observation cuts both ways. If the teams are split by technical layer — a screens team, an API team, a database team — every feature crosses all three, and every feature carries three-way dependencies. If the teams are split by product area, most features stay inside one team. The deliberate use of this, arranging teams to get the system boundaries you want, is sometimes called the **inverse Conway manoeuvre**, and it is the point where team design and architecture become the same decision.

## Two ways to respond

Faced with these costs, an organisation can add **coordination**: shared planning events, roles that manage dependencies, a common cadence so that teams can plan together. Or it can reduce the **need** for coordination: reorganise so that teams depend on each other less. SAFe leans on the first; LeSS leans hard on the second. The next sections take them in that order.

## Component teams

A **component team** owns a technical component or layer: the screens, the API, the database, the payment integration. It is the arrangement organisations reach for first, because it matches the architecture diagram and lets specialists work with specialists.

Its cost appears with the first feature. A feature that users notice — *receptionists can move an appointment* — needs a change in the screens, the API and the database. With component teams, that is three backlogs, three priorities and two hand-overs, and the feature is finished when the last of the three gets to it. Every feature carries the dependencies that this lesson's first section described, by construction.

## Feature teams

A **feature team** is a long-lived, cross-functional team that can take a customer-centred feature from start to finish, across every layer it touches. The same feature now sits inside one team. LeSS requires feature teams; SAFe recommends them, while accepting that some trains will keep component teams for a while.

Feature teams have costs of their own, and an architect should name them:

- **Shared code needs shared care.** When several teams change the same component, nobody owns its design. The usual answers are component *communities* — the people from several teams who know a component well, meeting regularly — and code review by somebody who knows that part of the system.
- **Deep specialisms are spread thin.** A team cannot hold a database specialist for each of eight teams. Specialists end up working across teams, as teachers as much as doers.
- **Some components should stay with one team.** A platform that every feature team builds on, or a part with strict regulatory constraints, may be better run as a component team that serves the others like an internal supplier.

## Where the architect comes in

The choice between the two is an architectural decision dressed as an organisational one, which is Conway's law from this lesson's second section at work. A system meant to evolve as independent services wants teams aligned with those services; teams split by layer will produce a system with strong layers and every feature cutting across them. An architect who is consulted on team boundaries is being consulted on the system's structure, whether or not anybody calls it that.

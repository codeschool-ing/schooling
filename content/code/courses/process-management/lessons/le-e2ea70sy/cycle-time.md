The Agenda team finished twenty items in the four weeks from 2 March 2026. Their start and finish dates are the table in this lesson's fifth section, and with them in your workbook, two numbers describe how the team delivers.

## Cycle time

The Days column holds each item's cycle time. Sorted, the twenty values are 2, 3, 3, 3, 3, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 9, 10, 10, 11, 15, 18 and 19. Three summaries, with what LibreOffice returned for each:

```localised
=AVERAGE(D2:D21)                 7.75
=MEDIAN(D2:D21)                  7
=PERCENTILE.INC(D2:D21,0.85)     11.6
```

In a spreadsheet set to Portuguese the same three are:

```localised
=MÉDIA(D2:D21)                   7,75
=MED(D2:D21)                     7
=PERCENTIL.INC(D2:D21;0,85)      11,6
```

**The mean is pulled up by the three slow items** at 15, 18 and 19 days, which is why it is above the median. Cycle times are almost always skewed this way: most items finish quickly, a few take much longer, and nothing can take less than a day.

**The 85th percentile is the number to quote.** It says that 85% of items finished in 11.6 days or less — in practice, 17 of the 20 finished within it. A team asked "how long does an item take?" can answer *"most items finish within about twelve days"*, which is a statement about the team's own history that a stakeholder can plan around. The Kanban Guide calls a statement like that a **service level expectation**.

## The items above the line

The three items above the 85th-percentile line in the figure are where the team learns the most. An item that took 19 days did not take 19 days of work; it waited somewhere. Asking *what happened to AG-099?* in the team's next review usually finds a blocked dependency, an item that was too big, or a card that sat in a done half-column for a week. Kanban's feedback loops exist to ask that question while the answer is still remembered.

## Throughput

Throughput is the number of items finished per unit of time. Counting the finish dates week by week gives **4, 6, 5 and 5** — twenty items in four weeks, five a week on average. Throughput is the measure that answers *how many* rather than *how long*, and lesson 13 shows how the two combine. Notice that neither number needed an estimate: both come from dates the board recorded anyway.

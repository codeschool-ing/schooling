*Kanban* is Japanese for a signboard or a visual card. In Toyota's factories of the 1950s, organised by Taiichi Ohno, a card travelled with each batch of parts; when a station used the batch up, the card went back upstream as a request for more. **Nothing was produced until a card asked for it.** That is a pull system, and it kept the factory from filling up with half-finished parts nobody needed yet.

Software borrowed the idea half a century later. David J. Anderson applied it to a software maintenance team at Microsoft in 2004 and at Corbis in 2006 and 2007, and his 2010 book *Kanban: Successful Evolutionary Change for Your Technology Business* gave the **Kanban Method** its shape. A shorter and stricter text, *The Kanban Guide* by Daniel Vacanti and Jim Coleman, followed in 2020. The two disagree on details and agree on the core.

## Start with what you do now

The first thing that separates Kanban from Scrum is where it starts. Scrum asks a team to adopt new accountabilities, events and artefacts from the first day. **Kanban asks a team to change nothing on the first day except to make its current work visible.** There are no new roles, no fixed iterations and no prescribed meetings. Change comes afterwards, one small experiment at a time, because what the board shows suggests it.

That makes Kanban easy to start and easy to do badly. A board on the wall with no limits on it is a picture, not a method.

## The practices

Anderson's method lists six general practices. They are worth knowing by name, because each one is the answer to a specific failure:

- **Visualise the work.** Every item in progress is a card in a column the team can see.
- **Limit work in progress.** Each column, or the whole board, has a maximum number of cards. This is the practice that makes the rest work, and this lesson's fourth section is about it.
- **Manage flow.** The team watches how items move and acts when they stop.
- **Make policies explicit.** What it takes for a card to move from one column to the next is written on the board, the same way Scrum writes down a Definition of Done.
- **Implement feedback loops.** Regular reviews of the board and of the numbers it produces.
- **Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally.** Change one thing, measure, keep it or drop it.

The Kanban Guide compresses the same ideas into three: define the workflow, actively manage the items in it, and improve the flow. It also names four measures every Kanban team should keep — **work in progress, throughput, work item age and cycle time** — and this lesson computes two of them on the Agenda team's real board.

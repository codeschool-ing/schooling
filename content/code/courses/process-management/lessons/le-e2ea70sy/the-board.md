A Kanban board is a set of columns, one for each stage work passes through, and a card for every item in progress. That much every team draws. What turns it into a Kanban board is three things most boards leave out: **the limits, the split columns and the two lines that start and stop the clock**.

## The limit on each column

The number under each heading is its **work-in-progress limit**, written WIP. Developing may hold three cards at most and Review two. When a column is at its limit, nobody may pull a new card into it — and that rule, more than the columns, is what Kanban is.

On the morning in the figure, Developing and Review are both full. The developer who finishes a task in Developing cannot push it into Review, because Review has no room. The useful move is the one a board without limits never prompts: **help clear Review**, by reviewing one of the two cards waiting there. That is the slogan the Kanban community repeats — *stop starting, start finishing* — turned into a rule the board enforces.

## Doing and done inside a column

Developing is split into *doing* and *done*. A card in the done half has finished development and is waiting for Review to have room. Without the split, a finished card and a card being worked on look identical, and nobody can see that work is queuing. With it, the queue is visible: AG-111 has been ready for review since the evening before, and it is waiting on capacity, not on a developer.

## Where the clock starts and stops

Two dashed lines cross the board. The first is the **commitment point**: when a card moves out of Ready, the team has committed to finishing it, and its clock starts. The second is the **delivery point**: when the card reaches Done, the clock stops. The time between the two is the item's **cycle time**, and it is the number this lesson computes in its seventh section.

Ready is outside the clock on purpose. A card waiting there is an option, not a promise; the team may reorder Ready or drop a card from it without anything being late. Teams that also want to measure how long an idea waited before anybody started it measure a longer interval, **lead time**, from the moment the request arrived. Both are legitimate; what matters is that a team says which one it reports, because the same item can have a cycle time of five days and a lead time of five weeks.

## The policies

Each column also carries its **exit policy**, the condition for moving a card on: *Review: approved by somebody who did not write it, no open comments*; *Testing: the tester has run the acceptance criteria on staging*. They are usually written small at the top of the column. They do for each stage what a Definition of Done does for the whole item.

Don Reinertsen's *The Principles of Product Development Flow* (2009) gives prioritisation its most useful single question: **how much does it cost us to have this later rather than now?** His advice was blunt — if you only quantify one thing, quantify the cost of delay.

## Why it is the right question

Value alone does not order a queue. Two features may each be worth R$ 100,000 a year. If one of them is worth that only from May, because it is tied to the clinics' contract renewal, and the other is worth it whenever it arrives, then doing the first one first and the second one second captures more value in total than the reverse. **Cost of delay converts value into urgency**, which is what a sequence needs.

It is also the number that makes trade-offs discussable. "This feature is important" invites argument; "every week without SMS reminders costs the clinics about forty missed appointments" invites a decision.

- **Standard**: value is lost steadily for every week of delay. Most features.
- **Urgent**: value is lost fast from the start, as with a defect that is losing customers today.
- **Fixed date**: nothing is lost until a deadline, then everything is. A regulatory change, or a feature that must be ready for the renewal in May. The right time to do it is late enough to fit other work in, and early enough to be sure.
- **Intangible**: little is lost now and much more later. Technical debt and the upgrade of a platform nearing the end of its support usually look like this, which is why they are put off until they become urgent.

## Estimating it

The cost of delay is rarely known precisely, and that is fine. What matters is that the people comparing items estimate it **in the same terms** — reais per week, appointments lost per week, or a relative scale — so that the comparison is consistent. Even a rough estimate makes a conversation better than a debate about whose item is "more important". The next section turns it into an order.

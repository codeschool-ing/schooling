The techniques so far treat value as a single number. Noriaki Kano, a Japanese professor of quality management, showed in 1984 that customers do not value features on one scale. **Some features are noticed only when they are missing; others delight precisely because nobody expected them.** The Kano model sorts requirements by how satisfaction responds to them.

## Three main kinds

- **Basic** (Kano's *must-be*) features are expected. Doing them well earns no thanks; doing them badly or not at all causes intense dissatisfaction. For the Agenda app: a booking that is saved correctly, a confirmation the patient receives. Nobody praises a booking app for not losing bookings.
- **Performance** (*one-dimensional*) features satisfy in proportion to how well they are done. The faster the booking flow, the better; the fewer taps, the better. Most of what competitors compete on is here.
- **Delighters** (*attractive*) are unexpected. Their absence disappoints nobody; their presence pleases out of proportion to their cost. A reminder that tells the patient which room to go to and how long the walk from the car park is might be one.

Kano also named two others: **indifferent** features, which nobody cares about either way, and **reverse** features, which some users actively dislike — an auto-play video, a mandatory account for a one-off booking.

## What it adds to prioritisation

The model changes how items are compared. A basic feature is not competing for priority with a delighter; it is a precondition. **No number of delighters makes up for a missing basic.** So the order of work is roughly: every basic to an acceptable level first, then performance features where they matter most, then a few delighters.

It also explains why priorities age. **Delighters decay into performance features and then into basics** as competitors copy them and users get used to them. Online booking was once a delighter for a clinic network; in many markets it is now a basic, and a clinic without it is losing patients rather than failing to impress them.

## Finding out which is which

Kano proposed a questionnaire that asks each user two questions per feature: how would you feel if it were present, and how would you feel if it were absent. The pair of answers classifies the feature. A lighter version — asking a handful of receptionists and patients both questions — is often enough to move an item from one column to another and change its place in the backlog.

---
title: Why deciding what comes first is hard
version: 1
---

Every backlog has more in it than the team can build, and so every team prioritises, whether it admits it or not. A backlog with no explicit order is still ordered: by who asked last, by who is most senior, by what is most fun to build. The techniques in this lesson exist to replace those orders with one that can be explained.

## Four things pull at once

The difficulty is that four different considerations bear on every item, and they are measured in different units:

- **Value** — what the item is worth to users or to the business, in revenue, in time saved, in satisfaction.
- **Cost** — how much effort it takes, which lessons 9 and 10 estimate.
- **Risk** — how much uncertainty it removes, which lesson 11 named: a spike or a migration test has little value of its own and a lot of value in what it prevents.
- **Time** — whether the value is the same next quarter or disappears if the item waits.

A technique that considers only value builds the expensive wish list first. One that considers only cost builds what is cheap. One that ignores risk leaves the integration that could sink the release until last. One that ignores time misses the contract renewal in May.

## Priority is not a property of the item

The most common mistake is to treat priority as something an item *has*, like a colour: "this is a high-priority story". Priority is a **comparison** — this item rather than that one, now rather than later — and it changes when the other items change. Scrum's insistence in lesson 2 that the backlog is **ordered**, with no two items sharing a place, makes the comparison explicit. Twenty items marked "high" contain no information about which to start on Monday.

## Who decides

In Scrum it is the Product Owner; in PRINCE2's terms, the Executive; in SAFe, Product Management. Whoever it is, they need input from people who see different parts of the picture: users and sales on value, the team on cost, the architect on risk. **An architect's main job in prioritisation is to make technical risk and technical debt visible in the same terms as features**, so that they can be weighed rather than argued about. This lesson's eighth section is about doing that.

The next five sections are techniques, from the simplest to the most quantitative. None of them makes the decision; each makes it easier to say why.

title: Points, time, or counting items
version: 1
---

Story points are not the only way to forecast, and since the mid-2010s a vocal part of the agile community has argued that they are not the best one either. The three options are worth knowing because a team, or the organisation around it, will choose one, and each has a cost.

| approach | what is estimated | what the forecast uses |
|---|---|---|
| time | hours or days per item | the sum of the estimates |
| story points | relative size per item | velocity in points per Sprint |
| counting items | nothing, beyond splitting items to a similar small size | throughput in items per week |

## Time

Estimating in hours or days is what most people do without thinking about it, and what stakeholders understand without explanation. Its weakness is the planning fallacy of lesson 9: absolute estimates of one's own work are biased short. It also mixes the size of the work with who is doing it: a task is two days for one developer and four for another.

## Points

Points answer both weaknesses, as this lesson's second section explained: comparison is more reliable than measurement, and size is separate from speed. Their weakness is that they are abstract. Every conversation with somebody outside the team needs a translation back into time, and the temptation to make the translation a fixed rate — one point is a day — destroys the reason for using them.

## Counting items

The third approach skips sizing entirely. The team splits work into items of roughly similar, small size, and forecasts from **how many items it finishes per week** — its throughput, from lesson 3. If the Agenda team finishes about five items a week and the release is about thirty items, it needs about six weeks, with a range from the slowest and fastest weeks of its history.

This is the core of what the **#NoEstimates** movement, associated with Woody Zuill and Vasco Duarte, has argued: that for teams which keep items small, counting forecasts as well as points do, and the time spent in estimation meetings can go into the work. The evidence is that throughput and point velocity tend to track each other closely once items are similar in size, which is what makes counting work. Its precondition is real discipline in splitting; a team whose items range from half a day to three weeks cannot forecast by counting them.

## Choosing

None of the three is wrong. Points suit a team that values the sizing conversation for what it uncovers. Counting suits a team with a steady flow of small items and good records. Time suits short, well-understood work and stakeholders who will not accept anything else. **What matters more than the unit is that the forecast is a range built from the team's own history**, whatever the unit is.

A spreadsheet will happily divide 150 points by the average velocity of the last six Sprints, 25.1667, and report **5.9603 Sprints**. Four decimal places, from inputs that were each a team's rough judgement on a scale with gaps between 13 and 20. The number looks more certain than anything that went into it, and that appearance is the **fallacy of precision**: treating the precision of a number as if it were the accuracy of the knowledge behind it.

## Precision is not accuracy

**Accuracy** is how close a number is to the truth. **Precision** is how finely it is stated. An estimate of "between 5 and 8 Sprints" is imprecise and, for the Agenda team, accurate: its own history says the outcome will very probably land there. "5.9603 Sprints" is precise and almost certainly inaccurate, since nothing about the work justifies the fourth decimal, or even the first.

The danger is in how people read numbers. A figure with decimals is read as measured; a round figure is read as estimated. A director given "5.96 Sprints" will plan for 5.96 Sprints. A director given "5 to 8" will plan for a range, which is what the team actually knows.

## Where it shows up

- **Velocity with decimals.** "Our velocity is 25.17" averages a handful of Sprints whose individual values swing by twelve points. "About 25" says the same thing honestly.
- **Points that are not on the scale.** A story estimated at 7.5 points, because the team could not decide between 5 and 8, claims a distinction the scale was designed to refuse.
- **Percent complete.** "The project is 73% complete" from a count of tasks that are themselves rough is the same mistake as lesson 6's earned value built on 90%-done packages.
- **Converting units.** Turning 37 points into "296 hours" by multiplying by 8 manufactures precision out of a guess, and the next section explains why the conversion is wrong in principle too.

## The rule of thumb

**State a number with no more digits than you would bet on.** If the team would not bet on the difference between 5.96 and 6.2 Sprints, say 6, or better, 5 to 8. The same rule applies to every number in this course: the PERT mean of lesson 9 was stated as 20.5 because it came out of an exact formula on given inputs, and the forecast built on it was still stated as a range, because the inputs were judgements.

---
title: What a story point is, and is not
version: 1
---

A **story point** is a unit of relative size. A team picks a small, well-understood piece of work, calls it 2 points, and from then on sizes everything else **by comparison**: twice as big is 4 or 5, much bigger is 13. A point is not an hour, not a day and not an amount of effort by any particular person. It is a team's shared sense of how big one piece of work is compared with another.

## Why relative

The argument for relative sizing is an observation about people: **they are much better at comparing than at measuring**. Asked how many hours a task will take, people anchor on an imagined plan and fall into the planning fallacy of lesson 9. Asked whether this task is bigger or smaller than the one they finished last week, they usually answer well, because the comparison carries the actual experience of the last task, delays included.

Relative size has a second advantage. It **separates the size of the work from the speed of the team**. A story that is 5 points today is still 5 points if the team gets faster, slower, or loses a member; what changes is how many points the team finishes in a Sprint, which is velocity, this lesson's fourth section.

## The scale

Most teams size on a scale that grows faster the bigger the item: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100, close to the Fibonacci sequence. The gaps are deliberate. Between a 2 and a 3 a team can tell the difference; between a 21 and a 22 nobody can, and the scale does not pretend otherwise. An item sized above about 13 is usually a signal that it should be split before anybody commits to it.

## Who invented them, and what he says now

Story points grew out of Extreme Programming teams around the turn of the century. Ron Jeffries, one of the people at the C3 project from lesson 4, has written that he may have invented them, and that if he did, he is sorry now. His regret is not about relative sizing. It is about what organisations did with the numbers afterwards: compared teams, set targets and converted points back into hours, which is the subject of this lesson's seventh section.

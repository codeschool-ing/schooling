This lesson is the one in the course that ages fastest. Names, levels, prices and exam formats change every few years, and some changed in 2026 while this course was being written. Everything below was checked in October 2026; **before paying for any exam, read the current requirements on the certifying body's own site**, because that page, not this one, is what the exam will follow.

## Three things a certificate can show

A certificate shows, at most, three things:

- **That you learned a vocabulary.** A foundation-level certificate says you can use the words of a framework correctly: you know what a Product Backlog is, what an ITIL incident is, what PRINCE2 means by a tolerance. That is genuinely useful when you join an organisation that speaks that vocabulary.
- **That you have experience of a certain kind.** Some certificates require documented experience before you may sit the exam. The PMP is the best-known: it asks for years spent leading projects, and the application can be audited.
- **That you passed an exam.** Everything else a certificate claims depends on how hard the exam was to pass without the knowledge it tests, which varies a great deal.

## What it does not show

No certificate shows that you can run a good project, coach a team through a bad Sprint or argue a technical debt into a backlog. Lessons 1 to 14 are about those, and they are learned by doing them. An organisation that hires on certificates alone gets people who know the words; one that ignores them entirely misses a cheap signal that somebody took the trouble to learn the words.

## Who asks for them

Certificates matter most where somebody needs to **filter** candidates without knowing the work: recruiters reading hundreds of applications, public tenders that score a bid by the qualifications of the proposed team, consulting firms that sell people by their credentials, and contracts that require a certified project manager. They matter least inside a team that already knows what you can do. That is the main argument of this lesson's seventh section: choose a certificate for the door it opens, not for the knowledge, which you can get without it.

## The cost that is not the fee

Most certificates expire. They have to be renewed by collecting continuing-education credits, by taking a newer exam, or by paying a fee, every one to three years. A certificate is a subscription more often than a purchase, and the renewal is worth counting before the first exam.

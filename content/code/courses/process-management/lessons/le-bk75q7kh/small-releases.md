XP's planning practices are older than most of what Scrum teams use today, and several of them were adopted so widely that people forget where they came from. The **user story** is one.

## The story

A story is a small piece of behaviour a user would value, written on a card in a sentence or two: *a receptionist can move an appointment to another day without cancelling it*. The card is not a specification. Ron Jeffries described a story as three things, the **three Cs**: the **card**, which is a reminder; the **conversation** between the people who will build it and the people who want it, which is where the detail is decided; and the **confirmation**, the tests that say when it is done.

The popular template — *as a receptionist, I want to move an appointment, so that the patient keeps their place in the queue* — came later, from teams at Connextra around 2001. It is useful because it forces somebody to name who benefits and why. It is harmful when the sentence becomes the whole requirement and the conversation never happens.

## The planning game

XP splits planning between two groups with different knowledge. **The customer decides what has value and in which order**; the developers **estimate what each story costs** and say how much fits in the next iteration. Neither may make the other's decision. A customer who says how long a story should take, or a developer who decides which feature matters more, is breaking the game. Scrum's split between the Product Owner, who orders the backlog, and the Developers, who forecast what fits in a Sprint, is the same rule with new names.

XP also wanted the customer to be **on site** — a real user or a person who can speak for them, sitting with the team and answering questions the same day. Few teams achieve it literally. The principle survives as a test of how long a developer waits for an answer: hours is healthy, weeks is how a team ends up building what it guessed.

## Small releases

XP releases working software to real users as often as the business can absorb it, which in 1999 meant far more often than the projects around it. Each release is the outermost feedback loop in this lesson's figure — the moment the whole team finds out whether the product is any use. The longer between releases, the more the team builds on assumptions nobody has checked.

The practice has a precondition that is easy to miss: **a release has to be cheap**. If releasing takes a weekend of manual steps, nobody will do it often. That is why small releases depend on the previous section's continuous integration, and why lesson 13 measures how often a team deploys as one of four headline numbers.

## Sustainable pace

The first edition called it the *40-hour week*; the second calls it *energized work*. The rule is that the team works only as many hours as it can sustain, and that overtime two weeks in a row is a sign the plan is wrong, not that people should work harder. It belongs with planning because overtime is how a bad plan hides: the date is met, the cost is paid in defects and in people leaving, and nobody revises the estimate that caused it.

The most widely used delivery measures in the industry come from one research programme. The **DevOps Research and Assessment** group, DORA, surveyed tens of thousands of technology professionals from 2014 onwards in the annual *State of DevOps* reports, and Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble and Gene Kim summarised the findings in *Accelerate* (2018) — the book that lesson 8 quoted on change approval. Google acquired DORA in 2018, and the reports continue.

The research found that four measures, taken together, separate organisations that deliver software well from those that do not, and that the better ones also do better on organisational outcomes such as profitability and market share.

## Two pairs

- **Deployment frequency** — how often the team deploys to production.
- **Lead time for changes** — how long a committed change takes to reach production.
- **Change failure rate** — what proportion of deployments cause a failure that needs fixing.
- **Time to restore service** — how long it takes to recover from such a failure. Since the 2023 report it has been called *failed deployment recovery time*.

The first two measure **throughput**, how fast changes flow. The last two measure **stability**, how often they hurt. The central finding of the research is that the two pairs **do not trade off**: the organisations that deploy most often also fail least and recover fastest, because small, frequent changes are easier to test, easier to understand and easier to undo.

## The Agenda team's March

The team deployed **24 times** in the 20 working days of March. Three of those deployments caused failures that needed fixing, restored in 45, 50 and 180 minutes.

The five changes that took **more than a day** are where to look next. If they are the changes that waited for a weekly approval, lesson 8's argument for standard changes applies; if they waited for a code review on Friday afternoon, the fix is in the team's own habits.

## What the four numbers do not say

DORA's metrics measure the **delivery pipeline**: from a committed change to a change running in production. They say nothing about whether the right thing was built, whether users are satisfied, or how long an idea waited before anybody committed code for it. A team can have excellent DORA numbers and a product nobody uses. They are a measure of one capability, a valuable one, and the research never claimed more.

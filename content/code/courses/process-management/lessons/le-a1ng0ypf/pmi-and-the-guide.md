The **Project Management Institute**, founded in the United States in 1969, is the largest professional body for project managers, and its book is the most widely cited description of how projects are run. *A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge* — the **PMBOK Guide** — first appeared as a full book in 1996 and has been revised roughly every four or five years since.

The word that matters in the title is *guide*. The PMBOK Guide does not describe a method that an organisation adopts the way it adopts Scrum. It describes **what the profession considers good practice**, in a vocabulary shared across industries, and leaves each organisation to decide which parts it needs. That is why a construction company, a bank's IT department and a government agency can all say they follow the PMBOK Guide while running projects very differently.

## Two editions you will meet

Two editions shape most of what people mean by PMBOK, and they are built differently:

- **The sixth edition (2017)** is process-based. It organises project management into **five process groups** and **ten knowledge areas**, with 49 processes placed in a grid between them, each with inputs, tools and techniques, and outputs. It came bundled with a separate *Agile Practice Guide*.
- **The seventh edition (2021)** is principle-based. It replaces the grid of processes with **twelve principles** and **eight performance domains**, and treats predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches as equally legitimate.

Most people's working vocabulary — process groups, knowledge areas, WBS, critical path, earned value — comes from the sixth edition and the ones before it. That vocabulary is still in contracts, job descriptions, tender documents and the certification exams, and it is what this lesson teaches first. The seventh edition's view is the lesson's eighth section. PMI keeps revising the guide, so check which edition an organisation or an exam refers to before quoting one.

## Why an architect should know it

Large organisations and public bodies run their programmes in this vocabulary. The proposal an architect writes for a new system will be judged by people who think in terms of a **charter**, a **scope baseline**, a **schedule** and a **cost baseline**, and who will ask what the critical path is. An architect who can answer in those terms gets a hearing that a list of technologies does not. The rest of this lesson is the minimum of that vocabulary, with the Agenda team's online-booking project as the example.

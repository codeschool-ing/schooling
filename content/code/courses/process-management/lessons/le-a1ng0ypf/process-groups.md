The sixth edition sorts project management work into **five process groups**: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. The names sound like phases, and the most common misreading of PMBOK is to treat them as a sequence that a project passes through once, like the waterfall of lesson 1.

## They are not phases

A **phase** is a slice of the project's life — design, build, pilot — that ends in a deliverable. A **process group** is a kind of management activity. A project with three phases goes through all five process groups **in each phase**: it initiates the phase, plans it, executes it, monitors and controls it, and closes it. The guide says so explicitly, and it is why the groups are compatible with iterative work: an iteration is a small phase, and each one can be initiated, planned, executed and closed.

## What each group does

- **Initiating** defines a new project or phase and obtains authorisation to start it. Its main outputs are the **project charter** and the list of stakeholders.
- **Planning** establishes the scope, refines the objectives and defines how they will be met: the scope baseline, the schedule, the budget, the plans for quality, risk, communications and procurement.
- **Executing** does the work defined in the plan, and manages the people and the stakeholders while it is done.
- **Monitoring and controlling** tracks progress against the plan, identifies where it deviates and starts the changes needed. It runs underneath the whole project, which is why the figure draws it as a band rather than a box.
- **Closing** formally completes the project or phase: acceptance of the deliverables, release of the people and resources, lessons learned, archived records.

## Where change happens

Planning is not done once. When monitoring finds a deviation — a task that took twice as long, a requirement nobody had understood — the plan is updated through a formal route called **integrated change control**: the change is requested, its effect on scope, schedule and cost is assessed, and somebody with authority approves or rejects it. Agile methods handle change by replanning at every iteration; PMBOK handles it by approving each change against a baseline. Both are ways of letting the plan move, and an architect who works in both settings needs to know which route a change has to take.

Once the work packages are broken into activities, each with a duration and a list of what must finish before it can start, the schedule becomes a network, and one question about it matters more than any other: **which activities, if they slip by a day, move the end date by a day?** Those activities form the **critical path**, and the method for finding them, the critical path method or CPM, dates from the late 1950s.

## The Agenda team's network

Six activities make up the online-booking project, with durations in working days. A — agree the booking rules with two clinics, 3 days — has to come first. After it, B, the slots API (5 days), and C, the design of the screens (2 days), can run in parallel. D, building the screens (4 days), needs C. E, integration and testing end to end (3 days), needs both B and D. F, the pilot in one clinic (2 days), comes last.

## Forward and backward

The method is two passes through the network.

The **forward pass** finds each activity's earliest start and finish. A starts at 0 and finishes at 3. B and C can start at 3; B finishes at 8 and C at 5. D starts when C finishes, at 5, and finishes at 9. E has to wait for both B and D, so it starts at the later of their finishes, **9**, and finishes at 12. F finishes at **14**: the project takes 14 days.

The **backward pass** starts from the end and finds the latest each activity can start and finish without delaying day 14. F must finish by 14, so start by 12; E by 9; D must finish by 9, so start by 5; C must finish by 5. B must finish by 9 — E's latest start — so it can start as late as 4.

The difference between latest and earliest start is the activity's **slack**, also called float. A, C, D, E and F have zero slack, so they are the critical path: **14 days**. B has **one day** of slack: the slots API can slip a day and nothing moves.

## What it tells a team

The critical path says where attention pays. A day saved on B saves nothing; a day saved on D saves a day of the project. It also says something less comfortable: the longest path through the work, not the total effort, sets the date. The six activities add up to 19 days of work, and the project takes 14 because some of it runs in parallel. Adding people shortens the project only if they shorten something on the critical path, which Brooks's law from lesson 5 says is harder than it sounds.

## Its limit

CPM assumes the durations are known. Every one of them is an estimate, and lesson 9 shows that estimates are ranges. A path with a little slack can become critical when its activities run long, and the classic extension, PERT, was invented in the same years to put ranges on the durations. Lesson 9 uses its three-point formula.

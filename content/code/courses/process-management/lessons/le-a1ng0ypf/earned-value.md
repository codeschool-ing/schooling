A project six weeks into a ten-week plan has spent R$ 125,000 of a R$ 200,000 budget. Is it doing well? The two numbers cannot say. It may be ahead of schedule and spending fast, or behind schedule and over budget, and a status report with only those two numbers will be read as whichever the reader hopes. **Earned value management** adds the third number that separates the cases.

## Three numbers

- **Planned value (PV)** — the budgeted cost of the work that was **scheduled** to be done by now. The Agenda team's plan expected R$ 120,000 of work to be finished by week 6.
- **Earned value (EV)** — the budgeted cost of the work **actually done** by now. Half the project's work is done, and half of the R$ 200,000 budget is R$ 100,000.
- **Actual cost (AC)** — what has **actually been spent** to do it: R$ 125,000.

The budget for the whole project, R$ 200,000, is the **budget at completion**, or BAC.

## Two variances and two indices

The schedule and the cost are read separately, each against earned value:

| measure | formula | the Agenda team | reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| cost variance | EV − AC | 100,000 − 125,000 = −25,000 | over budget |
| schedule variance | EV − PV | 100,000 − 120,000 = −20,000 | behind schedule |
| cost performance index (CPI) | EV ÷ AC | 100,000 ÷ 125,000 = 0.80 | each real spent buys 80 cents of planned work |
| schedule performance index (SPI) | EV ÷ PV | 100,000 ÷ 120,000 = 0.83 | work is being done at 83% of the planned pace |

An index of 1 means exactly on plan. Below 1 is worse than planned, above 1 better.

## A forecast from the past

The most used forecast assumes the rest of the project will be done at the cost efficiency seen so far. The **estimate at completion** is then the budget divided by the CPI:

```localised
EAC = BAC / CPI = 200,000 / 0.80 = 250,000
```

At the current rate, the project will cost **R$ 250,000**, R$ 50,000 over budget, and R$ 125,000 more will need to be spent to finish it. That is a statement a sponsor can act on in week 6, when there is still time to cut scope, add money or stop.

## What it needs, and what it does not measure

Earned value is only as honest as the measure of work done. "Half the work is done" has to mean something checkable, which is why it is usually counted by finished work packages, with a rule such as 0% until a package is complete and 100% when it is. Teams that count 90%-done packages produce earned value that is always nearly on plan, for the same reason a burndown in lesson 2 refuses to count them. And it measures cost and schedule against the plan, not whether the plan was worth doing: a project can deliver the wrong product exactly on budget. Lesson 13 comes back to what a measure can and cannot tell.

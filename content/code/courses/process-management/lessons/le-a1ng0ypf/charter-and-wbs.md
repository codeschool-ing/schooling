Two documents carry most of the weight at the start of a PMBOK-style project, and both have equivalents in agile work under other names.

## The project charter

The **charter** is the document that formally authorises the project and gives the project manager authority to use the organisation's resources on it. It is issued by the **sponsor** — the person or group who provides the money and is accountable for the project's success — and it is short: the purpose, the measurable objectives, the high-level requirements and risks, a summary budget and milestones, the people involved and the criteria for success.

Its value is that it is signed. A project with no charter is a project whose purpose anybody can reinterpret, and whose manager has to negotiate for every person and every hour. A charter for the Agenda team's online booking would say, in a page, that the project exists to let patients book physiotherapy appointments online in the clinic network's thirty clinics by a given date, for a given budget, sponsored by a named director.

## The work breakdown structure

The **work breakdown structure**, or WBS, is the **hierarchical decomposition of the total scope** of work the team must do to deliver the project. The top is the whole product; the next level is the major deliverables; below them, the work is broken down until each piece — a **work package** — is small enough to estimate, assign and track.

Two rules make a WBS useful rather than decorative:

- **The 100% rule.** Each level contains all the work of the level above it and nothing more. If the WBS is complete, the project is everything in the tree; anything not in the tree is not in the project. That is what makes the WBS the reference when somebody asks whether a piece of work is in scope.
- **Deliverables, not activities.** The branches are things that will exist — the slots API, the booking screens — rather than verbs like *programming* or *testing*. Activities come afterwards, when each work package is planned.

Each work package is described in a **WBS dictionary**: what it contains, its acceptance criteria, who is responsible and its estimate. The scope statement, the WBS and the dictionary together form the **scope baseline**, which change control protects from then on.

## The agile equivalents

An agile team does the same decomposition with different names. The product vision or Product Goal plays the part of the charter's purpose; epics and features are the upper levels of the tree; stories are close to work packages. The real difference is **when** the lower levels are filled in. A WBS is broken down to work packages during planning, for the whole project. A product backlog is broken down just in time: the next few Sprints in detail, the rest left as large items, which is the cone of uncertainty from lesson 1 again.

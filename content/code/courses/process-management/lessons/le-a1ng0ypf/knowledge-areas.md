Where the process groups say **when** a kind of management work happens, the knowledge areas say **what it is about**. The sixth edition names ten, and each one groups the processes that deal with one subject across the life of the project.

| knowledge area | what it covers | an example of its work |
|---|---|---|
| Integration | holding the whole together | the charter, the overall plan, change control |
| Scope | what is and is not in the project | the work breakdown structure |
| Schedule | when the work happens | the activity network and the critical path |
| Cost | how much it costs | the budget and earned value |
| Quality | how good it has to be, and how that is checked | quality standards, reviews, testing |
| Resource | the people and the equipment | who works on what, and when |
| Communications | who needs to know what | reporting, meetings, the communication plan |
| Risk | what could go differently from the plan | the risk register and the responses |
| Procurement | what is bought from outside | contracts with suppliers |
| Stakeholder | everybody affected by the project | identifying them and keeping them engaged |

## The ones an architect touches

An architect rarely owns a knowledge area, but four of them meet architectural work directly. **Scope**, because the boundary of the system is the boundary of the project. **Quality**, because non-functional requirements are quality requirements in this vocabulary, and lesson 2 showed where they go in Scrum. **Risk**, because the riskiest unknowns are usually technical, and lesson 11 is about them. And **procurement**, because a decision to buy a component rather than build it creates a contract with its own terms, dates and exit conditions.

## Integration is the project manager's own

Integration is the area that has no equivalent in a single team's agile process, and it is worth understanding why it exists. In a large project, the scope plan, the schedule, the budget, the risk register and the procurement contracts are kept by different people, and each can be correct while the combination is impossible: a schedule that assumes a supplier delivers in March and a contract that says June. Integration management is the work of keeping the parts consistent with each other, and change control is its main tool. In an agile team, the Product Owner and the team do much of that work in every planning session, on a much smaller project.

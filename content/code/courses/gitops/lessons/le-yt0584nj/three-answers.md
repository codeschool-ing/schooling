---
title: Three places to keep a secret
version: 1
---

Everything in this course so far has followed one rule: **the cluster's state is in Git**. A secret
breaks the rule twice over. It cannot go in Git as it is, because everybody who can read the
repository, and every clone and backup of it, would hold it. And it cannot stay out of Git, because
then the cluster has a piece of state nobody reviewed, nobody versioned and nobody can rebuild.

There are three answers, and this lesson and the next two are about them:

| | what Git holds | where the plaintext is | who decrypts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sealed Secrets** | the secret, encrypted to one cluster's key | in the cluster, as a Secret | a controller in the cluster |
| **SOPS** | the secret, encrypted to keys you choose | in the cluster, as a Secret | Flux, with a key you give it |
| **Vault and an operator** | a reference to the secret | in Vault, and in the cluster as a Secret or not at all | nobody: the operator reads it from Vault |

The first two **encrypt the secret and keep it in Git**. The repository goes back to being the whole
truth, and the price is a key: whoever holds it can read every secret ever committed, in every old
commit, forever. The third **keeps the secret out of Git** and puts in its place a reference, the
name of an entry in a secret store; the repository says *which* secret and the store says *what*.
The price is a service that has to be running, reachable and unsealed for anything to start.

Neither family is the right one everywhere. A small team with one cluster is well served by Sealed
Secrets. A team that wants a pull request to show which keys changed, and to decrypt a file on a
laptop when it has to, uses SOPS. A company with dozens of clusters and an audit department ends up
with a secret store. This lesson runs Vault and teaches it to recognise the cluster; lesson 10 adds
the operator that reads from it, and from the cloud providers' stores.

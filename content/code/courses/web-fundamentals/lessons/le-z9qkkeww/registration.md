You did not buy a domain. Nobody does. You bought the **exclusive right to have that name
delegated to your servers, for a period**, and everything odd about domains follows from that
sentence.

The **registry** runs one top-level domain and holds the authoritative list of what is registered
under it. One organisation per TLD, and you will almost never speak to one.

The **registrar** is who you buy from — the shop. They are accredited to write into the registry's
list on your behalf, they take your money, they run the control panel you log into, and there are
hundreds of them competing on price and on how unpleasant their upsells are.

The **registrant** is you: the person or company the name is recorded to.

The distinction matters at exactly two moments, and both are bad days. When a registrar goes out of
business or locks your account, the registry still has the record and the name can be moved. And
when somebody claims the name is theirs, the argument is settled under the registry's rules, not
the shop's.

## Renewal is the whole risk

A registration runs from one to ten years and then stops.

What happens after that is the most expensive thing in this reading. The name does not quietly stay
yours. It expires, spends a grace period during which you can still recover it — usually at a
penalty — and then goes to a public auction or back onto the market, where software has been
watching for it since the day it was registered.

Companies have lost their own name this way. A card expires, a renewal notice goes to an address
nobody reads any more, and a domain that carries a company's mail and its site changes hands. The
site is the visible loss; the mail is the serious one, because whoever holds the name now receives
every password reset ever sent to it.

Two defences, and they cost nothing. **Automatic renewal**, with a payment method somebody checks.
And a **registrant address that is not on the domain itself**, so that losing the domain does not
also lose the only channel by which you would be told.

## The lock, and the transfer code

Two mechanisms exist to stop a name being moved without you, and they are worth recognising in a
control panel.

A **transfer lock** is a flag at the registry that refuses transfers outright. It is on by default
on most names, and turning it off is the first step of a legitimate move — and of a hostile one,
which is why turning it off should be something a person does deliberately rather than a setting
somebody leaves off.

An **authorisation code** is a per-domain secret the gaining registrar must present. Treat it as a
credential, because it is one.

The same applies to whoever controls the account. A domain is a credential in the shape of a
purchase, and it is usually protected by the weakest password in the company, in an account
belonging to somebody who left.

## Choosing where to buy, in four checks

Registrars compete on price, and price is the least interesting thing about them. Four questions
separate the ones you will be glad of.

**What does renewal cost?** A first year at a striking discount followed by renewals well above the
market is the oldest arrangement in this business, and by the time you notice, moving is work.

**Is DNS included, and is it any good?** Most registrars will host your records. Some do it well;
some offer a control panel with no record types you need and no way to script it. You are allowed
to buy the name in one place and keep the records in another, and plenty of people do.

**What does a transfer out cost, and how hard is it?** A registrar that makes leaving slow has told
you something about the rest of the relationship.

**Who can log in?** This is the account that holds the name that holds the mail that receives every
password reset. It should have a second factor, and it should belong to the company rather than to
whoever happened to buy it.

## What is public about you

Registration records were historically public, in full: name, postal address, telephone, email.
Anybody could look up who owned a name.

Privacy law and registrar services have changed that, and what shows publicly today is usually a
proxy — the registrar's own contact details in place of yours. The registry still holds the real
ones, and they are disclosed in a dispute or to a legal request.

So the practical position is: **not secret, and no longer a phone book.** Assume a determined party
can find out, and do not assume an ordinary visitor can.

## What it costs, and what that says

The price of a name is set by the registry and marked up by the registrar, and ranges from a few
currency units a year to several hundred. The variation says nothing technical: `.com` and a new
word-shaped TLD behave identically to every piece of software in this course.

Which leaves the choice as something other than a technical one. The two things worth weighing are
whether the TLD carries a meaning you want — a country's TLD reads as being from that country,
whether you are or not — and whether the registry has rules about who may hold names under it,
because some require a local presence or a business registration and will take a name back from
somebody who does not have one.

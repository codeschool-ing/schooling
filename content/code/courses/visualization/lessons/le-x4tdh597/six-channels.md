every panel the regions run from **1**, the largest (Southeast, 77,567), to **5**, the smallest

Try to answer one question in each panel: **is region 2 more than twice region 4?** The real
numbers are 39,924 and 16,413, so it is, by a little.

- **Position.** Five dots on one scale. The distances can be compared directly, and you can nearly
  read the ratio.
- **Length.** Five bars from a common baseline. Just as easy, because a bar's length is the
  distance from the baseline to its end.
- **Angle.** Five pie slices. You can see that 1 is the largest. Whether 2 is more than twice 4 is a
  guess.
- **Area.** Five circles, sized so that area matches the number. Circle 1 has about 6.5 times the
  area of circle 5, and it does not look it. Most readers judge it at three or four times.
- **Lightness.** Five squares from strong to faint. The order survives; the amounts do not. Nobody can
  say how much darker is twice as dark.
- **Hue.** Five colours. **The order is gone.** Red is not more than blue, and a reader who does not
  look at a legend cannot tell which region is largest at all.

## What the experiment shows

**Channels differ in how precisely the eye reads them**, and the difference is large enough to
change what a reader concludes. The first two panels answer the question; the middle two invite an
estimate; the last two cannot.

Lesson 2 puts numbers on that ranking. It comes from experiments in which people were shown pairs
of marks and asked to judge their ratio. Position beat length, length beat angle, and angle beat
area, and the order held when the experiments were repeated decades later.

## And shape?

The lesson's title names **shape**, and it is missing from the figure on purpose. A circle is not
more than a triangle. Shape is like hue: it **tells things apart and says nothing about amount**. It
earns its place when a chart needs a second way to separate categories, for example so that a
reader who cannot tell two colours apart can still tell the series apart (lesson 14).

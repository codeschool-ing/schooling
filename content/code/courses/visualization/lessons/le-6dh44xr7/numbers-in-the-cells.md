Colour tells the reader where to look. It cannot tell them what they will find there, because lesson 2
put shading at the bottom of the ladder. The fix is simple and underused: **write the number in the
cell**.

Three decisions make this version readable.

**Half the matrix.** The upper half repeats the lower one, so it is left out. The diagonal stays, as a
border that shows where the mirror would be.

**The number in every cell**, to two decimal places. The colour picks out 0.95 and 0.88 at once; the
numbers let a reader check whether 0.26 matters, which no shade could tell them.

**Text that reads against its cell.** A dark cell needs light text and a pale cell needs dark text.
One text colour for every cell fails on half of them, and a reader with low vision fails on more. Lesson
14 gives the contrast a number.

## A colour scale that matches the values

Correlations run from −1 to +1, with **0 as a meaningful middle**: no relationship. So the colour scale
has two sides: one hue for negative, another for positive, and pale at zero. That is a **diverging**
scale. A heatmap of order counts has no meaningful middle, only more and less, and uses a
**sequential** scale, one hue from faint to strong, as the week heatmap does.

Lesson 12 is about choosing between them. For now, one rule settles most cases: **a quantity with a
meaningful centre gets a diverging scale, and a quantity that only goes up gets a sequential one.**

## Always show the scale

A heatmap without a colour legend makes the reader guess what a strong colour means. Put a small scale beside it
with its ends labelled, as both figures in this lesson do, and say in words what is being measured.

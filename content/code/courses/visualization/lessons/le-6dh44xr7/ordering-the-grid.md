Lesson 3 showed that a bar chart's order is a free channel. A heatmap has two of them, the order of
the rows and the order of the columns, and the right choice can turn noise into a pattern.

When the rows and columns already have an order, keep it. **Days of the week and hours of the day run
in their own sequence**, and the week heatmap would be unreadable if they were sorted by value.

A correlation matrix is different. Its variables are in whatever order the file happened to list
them, and that order means nothing.

On the left, in the file's order, the two strong pairs are two strong cells far from each other, and
the eye has to hunt for them. On the right the variables are reordered so that related ones sit next
to each other: items beside basket, minutes beside km. The strong pairs become **two strong blocks along
the diagonal**, and the structure of the data is the picture: there are two groups of variables, the
size of the order and the length of the trip, with rain on its own.

## How to choose the order

- **By hand**, for a small matrix: put the strongest pair together, then add the variable most related
  to either of them beside it, and so on.
- **By clustering**, for a large one: algorithms that group similar rows, called hierarchical
  clustering, produce an order and often draw a tree, a **dendrogram**, along the edge to show which
  variables were joined first. Libraries such as seaborn offer it as one function, `clustermap`.

Either way, **the rows and the columns use the same order**, so the diagonal stays a diagonal.

## Grids that are not matrices

The same idea applies to any heatmap whose rows have no natural order. A heatmap of states by month,
listed alphabetically, scatters the similar states across the grid; sorted by region, or by total
orders, the regional patterns line up.

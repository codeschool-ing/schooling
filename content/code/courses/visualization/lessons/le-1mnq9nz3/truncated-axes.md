Lesson 3 gave the rule: a bar is read by its **length**, so its axis starts at zero. This section
measures what breaking the rule costs, because the people who break it on purpose count on nobody
measuring.

numbers with the axis starting at zero and at 65,000:

On the right, the 2025 bar is 12,567 units above the baseline and the 2024 bar 2,663, so it looks
almost five times as tall. Nothing on the chart is false: every tick is labelled. But the picture says
"exploded" and the numbers say "grew a little", and the picture is what people remember.

## Measuring it

Edward Tufte gave this a number in *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983): the **lie
factor**, the size of the effect shown in the graphic divided by the size of the effect in the data. A
faithful chart scores 1. This program works it out for three baselines:

```schooling-example
{"language": "python", "file": "liefactor.py", "parts": [{"code": "import csv\nimport matplotlib.pyplot as plt\n"}, {"code": "totals = {}\nwith open(\"monthly.csv\") as f:\n    for row in csv.DictReader(f):\n        if row[\"region\"] == \"Southeast\":\n            year = row[\"month\"][:4]\n            totals[year] = totals.get(year, 0) + int(row[\"orders\"])\n", "note": "Add up Southeast's orders for each year: the month's first four characters are the year."}, {"code": "a, b = totals[\"2024\"], totals[\"2025\"]\nchange = (b - a) / a\nprint(f\"Southeast 2024: {a:,}   2025: {b:,}\")\nprint(f\"change in the data: {change:+.1%}\")\n", "note": "The change the data shows, as a fraction of 2024."}, {"code": "for start in (0, 50_000, 65_000):\n    drawn = (b - start) / (a - start) - 1\n    print(f\"axis from {start:>6,}: bars differ by {drawn:+7.1%}, lie factor {drawn / change:4.1f}\")\n", "note": "For each place the axis could start, the bars show only the part above it. Their relative difference, divided by the data's, is the lie factor."}, {"code": "fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(7, 3))\nfor ax, start in zip(axes, (0, 65_000)):\n    ax.bar([\"2024\", \"2025\"], [a, b], color=\"#2b52c9\")\n    ax.set_ylim(start, 80_000)\n    ax.set_title(f\"axis from {start:,}\", loc=\"left\")\nfig.savefig(\"liefactor.png\", dpi=150, bbox_inches=\"tight\")\n", "note": "Draw the honest chart and the truncated one side by side, so the difference can be seen as well as computed."}]}
```

```
Southeast 2024: 67,663   2025: 77,567
change in the data: +14.6%
axis from      0: bars differ by  +14.6%, lie factor  1.0
axis from 50,000: bars differ by  +56.1%, lie factor  3.8
axis from 65,000: bars differ by +371.9%, lie factor 25.4
```

The data changed by 14.6%. Starting the axis at 50,000 makes the bars differ by 56.1%, almost four
times the truth; starting at 65,000 makes them differ by 371.9%, a lie factor of 25.4. Tufte's own
rule of thumb was that anything outside 0.95 to 1.05 is a distortion worth fixing.

## Lines are different

A line is read by its **position** and its slope, not by its distance from the bottom of the
chart, so it may start where the data is. With the axis from zero, Southeast's monthly orders sit in
the top third of the chart and every change is squeezed into a narrow band:

So the rule is about the mark, not about axes in general:

- **Bars, areas and anything filled from a baseline start at zero.** Always.
- **Lines and dots may start where the data starts**, as long as the axis is labelled. A temperature
  chart from 15 °C to 35 °C is fine.
- **If small differences between bars matter**, do not truncate: switch to dots, or chart the
  differences themselves, such as growth in percent.

A chart that starts somewhere other than zero should say so plainly. A broken-axis symbol on a bar is
a confession rather than a fix: the bars are still the wrong length.

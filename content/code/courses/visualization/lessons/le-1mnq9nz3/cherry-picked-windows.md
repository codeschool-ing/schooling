Every chart of a time series starts and ends somewhere, and the start and end are choices. A chart
can be accurate in every point and still mislead by **where it begins**.

Horta's total orders fell from 16,663 in December 2024 to 13,367 in January 2025. That is a fall of
19.8%, and a chart of just those two months makes a dramatic headline:

The whole series tells the opposite story: orders grew in both years, and every December spikes with
holiday shopping and falls back in January. A January below December is the business behaving
normally.

## How windows mislead

- **Starting at a peak or a trough.** Begin at the December spike and any later month looks like a
  fall; begin at the January dip and anything looks like growth.
- **Too short a window.** Two points make a trend out of noise, and the December pattern from
  lesson 4 disappears.
- **Stopping before the inconvenient part.** A chart of a share price that ends the week before it
  fell is true to its last pixel.

## What an honest window looks like

- **Show the whole period the data covers**, or say why it does not.
- **Compare like with like.** For a seasonal business, compare December with December, or the year to
  date with the same months last year.
- **Say the period in the subtitle**, so a reader knows what they are not seeing: "Monthly orders,
  January 2024 to December 2025".
- **When you zoom in, show the context too**, as the shaded band in the right-hand chart does. A small
  overview beside a detail costs little and answers the question a reader should ask.

A window is the hardest of these distortions to spot because nothing on the chart is wrong. The only
defence is asking what happened just outside it.

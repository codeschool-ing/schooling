Lesson 4 showed what a dual-axis chart does by default: matplotlib fits each axis to its own series,
so two lines of very different sizes come out looking the same size. That is the accident. This
section is about the same chart used **on purpose**.

Each axis on a dual-axis chart has a range, and somebody chose it. Together the two ranges decide where
the lines sit relative to each other, so they decide whether the lines cross, and when. Here are
Northeast and North again. Northeast stays on the same left scale every time; only North's right-hand
range changes:

Three headlines, each with a chart that "proves" it, from the same 48 numbers. None of the charts is
false: every tick is labelled. What misleads is that the crossing, the thing the eye goes to first,
was put there by whoever typed the two numbers for the right axis.

## Reading one you did not draw

When somebody else's chart has two scales, three questions take the trick apart:

- **Do the two axes measure the same thing?** Degrees Celsius against Fahrenheit is one scale written
  twice, and nothing can cross that should not. Orders against orders on two ranges, or orders against
  money, is two scales.
- **Does either axis start at zero?** If not, the slopes are exaggerated by an amount nobody can see.
- **Does the story depend on a crossing or on the lines moving together?** Both are produced by the
  ranges. Neither is evidence.

## Drawing it honestly

The fixes are the ones lesson 4 gave, and they are worth repeating because a dual axis is so easy to
ask for:

- **Index both series to 100** at a common start and draw them on one axis. North ends at 325 and
  Northeast at 260, a comparison nobody tuned.
- **Stack two panels** that share the horizontal axis, each with its own honest scale.
- **Plot one against the other** as a scatter plot (lesson 6) when the question is whether they move
  together.

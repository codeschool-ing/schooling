Spreadsheets offer a 3D version of almost every chart, and it is the one effect in the menu that is
never an improvement for a chart that shows numbers. Depth adds ink that carries no data, and the
perspective that makes it look solid changes the sizes the reader compares.

Two things happen when a pie is tilted:

- **The slices at the front grow and the ones at the back shrink.** Squashing the circle into an
  ellipse keeps the areas in proportion, but the front slices now show their side wall as well as
  their top.
- **The side wall belongs only to the front.** Drinks is 14.0% of revenue, the second-smallest
  category, and it ends up with 21.9% of the ink. Vegetables, the largest at 21.0%, sits at the back
  and gets 16.1%.

So the reader, who was already comparing angles poorly (lesson 2), is now comparing angles that have
been bent. Rotating the pie a few degrees changes which category looks largest, which means the
picture is reporting the rotation rather than the revenue.

## 3D bars

Bars with depth have a quieter version of the same problem. The top of a 3D bar is drawn above its
front face, so the reader does not know which edge to read against the gridline, and a value of 50
can appear to reach 52 or 48 depending on the viewing angle.

## The rule

Use a third dimension only when the data has three dimensions and the reader can turn the picture
around, as in an interactive scientific plot. On a page or a slide, draw it flat.

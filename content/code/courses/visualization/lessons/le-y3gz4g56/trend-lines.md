A **trend line** summarises a cloud with a line. The usual one is the **least-squares line**, the
straight line that makes the vertical distances from the points to the line, squared and added up,
as small as possible. Every spreadsheet draws it with one click and every plotting library with one
function.


Read the output in three parts.

- **The equation**: a delivery takes about 22.8 minutes plus 2.17 minutes per kilometre. The 22.8 is
  what a trip of no distance would take, which is the time spent picking and handing over. Horta's
  data was generated with 2.1 minutes per kilometre, so the line has found the real rule to within a
  few seconds.
- **The fit**: *r* is the correlation, 0.88 here, between −1 and 1. Its square, 0.78, is the share
  of the variation in minutes that the line accounts for. The other 22% is rain, basket size and
  chance.
- **The predictions**: at 5 km the line says 33.7 minutes, and most deliveries are near 5 km, so
  that is a fair summary. At 20 km it says 66.2, from a part of the cloud with only six deliveries
  beyond it. At 60 km it says 153.1, and **no delivery went further than 34.3 km**.

Drawing the line past the data is **extrapolation**: assuming the pattern continues where nobody
looked. Maybe it does. Maybe a 60 km trip uses the motorway and is faster per kilometre, or needs a
second driver and is slower. The chart cannot know.

The honest drawing **stops the line where the data stops**, as `scatter.py` does by ending it at the
longest delivery. If a prediction beyond the data is needed, draw that part differently, dashed or
faded, and say in words that it is a guess.

## When a straight line is the wrong summary

- **The cloud curves.** A straight line through a curve is wrong at both ends and in the middle.
  Look at the cloud first; fit a line only if the cloud is straight.
- **There are groups.** Two clouds, say city and countryside deliveries, can each rise while one line
  through both falls, or the reverse. Colour the groups and fit each separately.
- **One point pulls it.** A single extreme value far to the side can tilt the line on its own. Fit
  with and without it and see whether the story changes.

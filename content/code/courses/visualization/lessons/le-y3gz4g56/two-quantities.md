title: Two quantities, one point each
version: 1
---

A **scatterplot** puts one quantity on each axis and draws one point per item. It is the chart for a
single question: **how does one quantity change as another one does?** Both channels are position
on a common scale, the top of lesson 2's ladder, which is why a scatterplot can show a relationship
more precisely than any other chart.

Each of the 400 points is one Horta delivery: how far it went and how long it took. Four things
can be read from the cloud, in this order.

- **Direction.** The cloud rises: longer trips take longer. A falling cloud would mean the opposite,
  and a shapeless one would mean the two have little to do with each other.
- **Form.** It rises in a roughly straight band, not a curve. A curve would mean that each extra
  kilometre costs more, or less, than the one before.
- **Strength.** The band is fairly tight. Most deliveries sit within ten minutes of where the distance
  alone would put them, and the rest is rain, the size of the order and chance.
- **Exceptions.** A few points sit well above the band: short trips that took a long time. Those are
  the deliveries a manager wants to ask about, and a scatterplot is the only chart that shows them as
  individuals.

## Which variable goes where

By convention, **the variable that might explain goes across**, and **the one being explained goes
up**. Distance may explain time; time does not explain distance. Readers are used to reading the
vertical as the outcome, and swapping the axes makes a chart they will read backwards.

## What a scatterplot does not say

**A rising cloud is not a cause.** Here the cause is plain: longer roads take longer. But a scatterplot
of ice cream sales against drownings also rises, because both go up in summer. The chart shows that
two quantities move together; it cannot say why. The `statistics` course covers how to argue for a
cause, and lesson 15 of this one covers how to title a chart without claiming more than it shows.

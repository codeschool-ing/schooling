A scatterplot has two positions, and both are taken. A third variable has to go into a channel
lower down the ladder, and the usual choice is **area**: the points become circles of different
sizes, and the chart is called a **bubble chart**.

The two positions carry the two variables the question is about: how many people a state has, and
how many orders Horta gets per thousand of them, which is how well Horta does there. The bubble's
area carries the third, total orders, which is the size of the business at stake. São Paulo is the
giant; the Federal District, small and high, is where Horta does best per person.

## Three rules for bubbles

**Size by area, never by radius.** A state with four times the orders gets a bubble with four times
the area, so twice the radius. Sizing by radius gives it sixteen times the area, and lesson 2's
power law means even the honest version already looks smaller than it is. matplotlib's `scatter`
takes its size argument `s` as an area, which is the right default; some tools take a radius.

**Put the important variables on the positions.** Area is read poorly, so the variable the reader
must compare precisely goes on an axis, and the bubble carries the one where a rough sense is enough.

**Draw the big ones first.** A large bubble drawn last covers the small ones behind it. Sorting from
the largest down, with some transparency, keeps every state visible.

## When not to use bubbles

When the third variable is the point of the chart, a bubble chart buries it in the weakest channel.
Draw it as bars instead, or as a second chart. And with more than about thirty bubbles the
overlaps make the chart unreadable whatever the order.

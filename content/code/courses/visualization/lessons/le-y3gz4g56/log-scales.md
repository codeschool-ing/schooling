Some quantities span a range so wide that a normal axis cannot show them. Horta's state data is one.

`states.py` measures how wide:

```schooling-example
{"language": "python", "file": "states.py", "parts": [{"code": "import csv\n"}, {"code": "with open(\"states.csv\") as f:\n    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))\norders = sorted(int(r[\"orders\"]) for r in rows)\npeople = sorted(float(r[\"population\"]) for r in rows)\nprint(f\"orders:     {orders[0]:6} to {orders[-1]:6}, ratio {orders[-1] / orders[0]:.0f}\")\nprint(f\"population: {people[0]:6} to {people[-1]:6}, ratio {people[-1] / people[0]:.0f}\")\nbelow = sum(1 for o in orders if o < orders[-1] / 10)\nprint(f\"{below} of {len(orders)} states have under a tenth of the largest\")\n", "note": "Sort the orders and the populations, print the smallest and largest of each with their ratio, and count the states with under a tenth of the largest."}]}
```

```
ana@vm:~/viz$ .venv/bin/python states.py
orders:        140 to  61040, ratio 436
population:    0.6 to   44.4, ratio 74
18 of 27 states have under a tenth of the largest
```

São Paulo has 436 times the orders of Roraima. On an ordinary axis from 0 to 65,000, **18 of the 27
states sit in the bottom tenth**, squashed into a corner where nothing can be told apart.

A **logarithmic axis** spaces its ticks by multiplication instead of addition: 100, 1,000, 10,000,
100,000, each the same distance apart. A state with ten times the orders of another sits one step
higher, whatever the starting point. On the right of the figure the 27 states spread out into a
band that rises with population, and the states that do better or worse than their size, such as
the Federal District above the band, stand out.

## What a log axis changes

- **Equal distances mean equal ratios.** The gap from 100 to 1,000 is the same as from 10,000 to
  100,000. Readers used to ordinary axes read distances as differences, so **say on the chart that
  the axis is logarithmic**, in words, not only through the tick labels.
- **Zero cannot appear.** The logarithm of zero is not defined, so a log axis has no zero and a
  value of zero cannot be drawn. A state with no orders would vanish from the chart without warning.
- **Bars do not work.** A bar on a log axis has no baseline, since zero is infinitely far down, and
  its length means nothing. Use points.

## When to reach for one

When the data covers **more than about two orders of magnitude** (a hundredfold), when the question
is about ratios rather than differences (growth rates, multiples), or when a few giants squash
everything else. For data within a factor of ten, an ordinary axis is clearer.

In matplotlib it is one line: `ax.set_xscale("log")`. In a spreadsheet, it is a checkbox in the axis
options, usually labelled **logarithmic scale**.

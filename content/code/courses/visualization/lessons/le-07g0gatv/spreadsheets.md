Excel, LibreOffice Calc and Google Sheets are where most charts in most organisations are made, and
for good reason: the data is already there, and selecting a range and choosing a chart type gives a
picture in a few seconds. For a quick look at a table, nothing is faster.

The chart that appears is the tool's default, and this course has spent nineteen lessons on what
defaults get wrong. The common ones in a spreadsheet chart:

- **The title is the column's name**, or the word "Chart". Replace it with the claim (lesson 15).
- **The bars are in the order of the rows.** Sort the data first, or the chart will show the order
  somebody typed the rows in (lesson 3).
- **A legend appears for a single series.** Delete it (lesson 17).
- **Gridlines, borders and sometimes a gradient or shadow** come with the style. Most styles can be
  turned to a plain one in a click (lesson 17).
- **Colours are assigned by position**, so adding a series can recolour the others (lesson 13).
- **3D versions sit next to the flat ones in the menu.** They are never the better choice for a
  chart of numbers (lesson 16).

## What spreadsheets do less well

- **Repeating the work.** A chart made by selecting, clicking and dragging is remade by doing it
  again. Next month the steps are repeated from memory, a little differently each time.
- **Many small charts.** Twelve small multiples with a shared scale (lesson 10) take a lot of manual
  copying and aligning.
- **Larger data.** A spreadsheet holds about a million rows per sheet in Excel; charting even a
  fraction of that slows it down.

None of that makes a spreadsheet the wrong tool. It makes it the right tool for a chart that is made
once, looked at, and either thrown away or rebuilt properly somewhere else.

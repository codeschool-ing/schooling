**A reader assumes that every difference on a chart means something.** That is the right assumption
to make about a chart, and it is why decorative colour is not harmless: a colour that carries no
information still gets read, and the reader looks for the meaning that is not there.

On the left, each of the six categories has its own colour. The names are already on the axis, so the
colours repeat what the reader already knows, at best. At worst they suggest groupings (are the warm
colours related?), importance (is red a problem?) or a legend somewhere that explains them. On the
right, one colour: the bars are equal in kind, and the chart says so.

## Meaningful and decorative

- **Meaningful colour** carries something the chart needs: which category a mark belongs to when
  position cannot say it, how large a value is on a heatmap, the one series to look at.
- **Decorative colour** carries nothing: a different hue per bar because the software does it by
  default, a gradient fill, a coloured background, a brand colour applied to everything.

The test is short: **if this colour were removed, would the reader lose any information?** If not, it
is decoration, and it should go.

## Where the default comes from

Spreadsheets and some BI tools colour each mark differently when a chart has one series and "vary
colours by point" is switched on. It is the default for pies, and an option easily left on for
bars. matplotlib colours a single bar series in one colour, which is the better default. **In every
tool, check what the colours are doing before the chart leaves your hands.**

## The cost

Colour is the most powerful pre-attentive channel: the eye finds a red mark among grey ones before
the reader decides to look for it. That power is limited, in the sense that **one chart can only have
one or two things that stand out**. Spend it on decoration and there is nothing left for the point.

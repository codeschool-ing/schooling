---
title: Colours that already mean something
version: 1
---

Some colours arrive with a meaning before the chart gives them one. Using them against that meaning
costs the reader a correction every time.

- **Red and green** read as bad and good, stop and go, loss and profit, in most business settings. A
  chart that colours the largest region red will be read as flagging a problem.
- **Political, sporting and brand colours.** In a country where a party or a football club owns a
  colour, using it for an unrelated category invites a reading nobody intended.
- **Natural associations**: blue for water and cold, green for vegetation, brown for land, orange and
  red for heat. A temperature map in blue for hot and red for cold has to be explained, and will still
  be misread.

## Use them when the meaning fits

Semantic colours are a gift when the data matches them. Profit in a cool colour and loss in red needs
no legend at all, and a chart of sales by product category can borrow the colours customers already
associate with each: green for vegetables, a warm colour for bakery. The reader decodes faster because
the decoding was done before they arrived.

## Two cautions

**Culture.** Red signals danger or loss in most Western business reports, but it signals luck and
prosperity in China, and rising prices are drawn in red on many East Asian stock charts. A chart
travelling between audiences should say what its colours mean in words.

**Red and green together.** The pair that carries the strongest meaning is also the pair the
commonest colour blindness confuses. Lesson 14 is about what to do instead; in short, keep the meaning,
change one of the hues, usually green to blue, and add a second cue such as a sign or a label.

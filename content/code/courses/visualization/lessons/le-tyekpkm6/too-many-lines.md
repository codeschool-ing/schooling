line per category, and it is rarely what the reader needs.

On the left the reader has to do three jobs before learning anything: match five colours to five
names in the legend, follow each line through the places where they cross, and decide which of the
five matters. South and Northeast tangle in 2025, exactly where the interesting thing happens.

On the right **one line is the story**: Northeast, drawn in a strong colour, named at its end, climbing
past South during 2025. The other four are grey. They are still there, so the reader can see that
Northeast's path is unusual against the others, and they no longer compete.

## Three techniques worth keeping

- **Highlight one, grey the rest.** It answers "what should I look at?" before it is asked. Lesson 13
  comes back to it as the central use of meaningful colour.
- **Label the ends of the lines.** The name sits where the eye arrives, and the legend disappears. A
  legend makes the reader look away from the data and back again, once per line.
- **Split the chart.** When every line matters equally, five small charts side by side, one per
  region, on the same axes, are easier to read than one chart with five lines. That is lesson 10.

## How many is too many?

There is no exact number, but a working rule: **beyond four or five lines, a single chart stops
being readable** unless all but one or two are grey. Colours run out first, since few people can
tell more than six or seven hues apart quickly, and crossings multiply after that.

**Indexing** puts series of different sizes on one scale by dividing each one by its own starting
value. Every series begins at 100, and from then on a value of 150 means "half as much again as at
the start", whatever the series measured.

The chart answers a question the raw numbers bury: **which regions are growing fastest?** North,
the smallest, has more than tripled; Southeast, the largest, has grown by about 59%. A dual-axis
chart could have suggested either story depending on its ranges. The indexed chart has one axis,
and no choice left to make except the starting month.

## In a spreadsheet

Put the months in rows and the regions in columns, then add a column per region with the formula
"this month divided by the first month, times 100", locking the first month's cell with `$` so the
formula can be copied down. Draw a line chart of the new columns.

## Three things indexing hides

**Size.** North at 325 and Southeast at 159 says nothing about which sells more, and Southeast still
sells more than five times as much. Say so beside the chart, or the reader will assume the top line
is the biggest region.

**The base month.** Indexing to a weak month makes everything after it look strong. January 2024 is
an ordinary month here; indexing to December, the peak, would make every region seem to shrink in
January. **Pick an ordinary starting point and name it on the axis**, as the figure does.

**Small bases.** A series that starts near zero produces huge indices from tiny changes: going from 2
orders to 10 is an index of 500. Index only series whose starting values are large enough to mean
something.

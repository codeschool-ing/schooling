**The two boxes are identical**: lowest 10, quartiles 22 and 38, median 30, highest 50. The dots
underneath tell two different stories.

- **Group A** has one peak. Most values sit near 30, and they thin out towards the ends. The box
  describes it well.
- **Group B** has two clusters, one around 24 and one around 36, and **no value at all between 26
  and 34**. Its median, 30, is the average of the two middle values, 26 and 34, and **no single
  value in the group is anywhere near it**. The box describes something that does not exist.

Real data does this whenever two populations are mixed: deliveries by bicycle and by van, customers
in the city and in the countryside, a product before and after a price change. A boxplot of the
mixture shows one ordinary-looking box.

## Three more things a box cannot see

- **How many values there were.** A box from 6 values and a box from 6,000 look the same, and the
  first one could change completely with one more delivery. Write the count beside each box.
- **Gaps and clumps inside the box.** The middle half is drawn as a solid rectangle whatever is
  inside it.
- **Rounding.** If times were recorded in whole quarter hours, every value sits on 15, 30 or 45, and
  the box looks the same as if they had been measured to the second.

## The cure

**Show the data with the summary.** When there are few enough points, draw them behind the box, as
dots with a little jitter. When there are too many, draw the shape as a violin. The next section
does both.

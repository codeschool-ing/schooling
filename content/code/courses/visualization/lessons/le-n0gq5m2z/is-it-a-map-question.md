---
title: Is it a map question?
version: 1
---

A map is the most persuasive chart there is, because it looks like the world. That is also why it is
chosen when it should not be. Data with a place attached, a state, a city, a postcode, is not
automatically a map; it is a map **only when where things are is part of the answer**.

Ask what the reader will do with the chart.

- **"Which states buy the most?"** That is a ranking. A sorted bar chart of 27 states answers it more
  precisely than any map, because lesson 2 put position and length above colour.
- **"Is there a pattern by region?"** Now geography matters: neighbours, coasts, the interior. A map
  shows that the North does worse than the South at a glance, because the states that do badly sit
  together.
- **"Where should the next depot go?"** Distance and adjacency are the whole question. Only a map
  will do.

## What a map costs

On a map, **position is used up by geography**. The two best channels on lesson 2's ladder are spent
on saying where each state is, so the number has to go into colour or into the area of a symbol,
both low on the ladder. A map is a trade: the reader gets the spatial pattern and gives up precision.

There is a second cost, particular to areas: **big places dominate the picture**. Amazonas covers
more of Brazil than São Paulo by a factor of about six, and on a shaded map it gets six times the
ink, whatever its value. The eye weighs a colour by how much of the page it covers.

## A good habit

When a map is right, **pair it with a ranked bar chart** of the same values. The map shows the
pattern; the bars give the exact order and the values the colour cannot. Dashboards (lesson 18) do
this constantly, and for good reason.

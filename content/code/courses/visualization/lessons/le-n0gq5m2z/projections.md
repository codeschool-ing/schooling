Every flat map starts with a decision most people never see: how to flatten a sphere. There is no way
to do it without stretching something, and the method chosen, the **projection**, decides what gets
stretched.

The figure draws the same globe twice, with identical small circles placed on it at the equator and
at 30 and 60 degrees north and south.

- **Mercator**, on the left, keeps shapes correct in any small area, which is why it was made for
  navigation in 1569 and why web maps still use a version of it: a street corner looks like a street
  corner. The price is area. At 60 degrees the circles are twice as wide and **four times the area**
  of the ones at the equator, and near the poles the inflation grows without limit. On Mercator,
  Greenland looks about the size of Africa, which is roughly fourteen times larger.
- **An equal-area projection**, on the right, keeps every circle's area and pays in shape: towards
  the poles the circles are squashed into flat ellipses.

## Why it matters for a shaded map

A choropleth asks the eye to weigh colour by area. **On a projection that inflates areas, high
latitudes get more weight than they deserve.** For a map of Brazil, which sits mostly between 5
degrees north and 34 degrees south, Mercator's distortion is modest, but on a world map it is
severe: Canada, Russia and Scandinavia dominate any choropleth drawn on Mercator.

The rules:

- **for a shaded map, use an equal-area projection**, such as Albers for a country or Equal Earth for
  the world;
- **for navigation and street-level maps, Mercator is fine**, because nobody compares areas there;
- **never compare areas** across a Mercator map, by eye or by measuring.

## In practice

Most tools pick the projection for you. BI tools and web maps default to the web's version of
Mercator, and Python's mapping libraries ask you to choose one. When you can choose, choose
equal-area for anything shaded; when you cannot, keep the map small in latitude range and say which
projection it uses.

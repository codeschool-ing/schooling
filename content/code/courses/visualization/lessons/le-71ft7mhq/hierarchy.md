The left-hand layout is common because it is easy: a grid of equal tiles, filled in the order the
charts were made. Every tile shouts at the same volume, so the reader has to read them all to find
the one that matters, and the total orders, the number the director opens the dashboard for, sits
in the bottom right corner.

## Where the eye goes

Readers of languages written left to right tend to start at the top left and sweep across the top
before moving down. Eye-tracking studies of web pages have found variations of this, an F shape on
pages dense with text and a Z on sparse ones, but the starting point is consistent:

That gives the layout its order:

1. **The top row holds the few numbers the reader came for**, most important on the left.
2. **The main chart goes beneath them and takes the most space.** Usually it is the trend, because
   "are we growing?" is the first question.
3. **Supporting charts are smaller and go to the right or below.** They answer "why?" and "where?"
   once the main question has an answer.
4. **Detail tables, notes and filters' fine print go last**, bottom or right, or behind a click.

## Size is a claim

A chart twice the size of its neighbour says it is twice as important, whether or not anybody meant
it to. Before making two charts the same size, ask whether they really matter equally. Usually one
of them is the answer and the other is the explanation.

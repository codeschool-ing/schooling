A dashboard is a set of charts on one screen, read by somebody who has a decision to make and a few
minutes to make it in. Most bad dashboards go wrong before the first chart is drawn, because they
start from the data ("what can we show?") rather than from the reader ("what do they need to
know?").

So before any layout, write down three things:

- **Who reads it, and how often.** A director glancing at it every Monday morning needs something
  different from an analyst who keeps it open all day.
- **The questions it answers**, as sentences. "Are we growing?" "Which region is behind?" "Are
  deliveries getting slower?" Three to five is plenty. If there are fifteen, there are three
  dashboards.
- **What the reader does with the answer.** A number nobody would act on, whatever its value, does
  not need a place on the screen.

For Horta's director, that might be:

| question | the answer on the screen |
| --- | --- |
| Are we growing? | orders this year against last |
| Was December normal? | December's orders against last December |
| Are customers waiting too long? | median delivery time, and the share over 45 minutes |
| Where is growth coming from? | growth by region |

## What a dashboard is not

- **Not a report.** A report explains; a dashboard is glanced at. The explanation goes elsewhere.
- **Not a collection of every chart anybody asked for.** Each extra chart takes attention from the
  others.
- **Not an exploration tool by default.** Filters and drill-downs are useful, but a dashboard whose
  first screen answers nothing until the reader clicks has made them do the work.

The four questions above become four places on the screen. The rest of this lesson is about how
big each one is and where it goes.

Most people see colour with three kinds of cone in the eye, sensitive to long, medium and short
wavelengths of light. When one kind is missing or shifted, some colours that differ for most people
look alike. That is **colour vision deficiency**, usually called colour blindness, although almost
nobody affected sees no colour at all.

## How common it is

Red-green deficiency is the commonest form by far. It is inherited on the X chromosome, so it affects
men much more than women: about **8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European descent**, with
lower rates in many other populations. In a team of 25 men, two are likely to have it; in an audience
of a thousand, several dozen.

The main forms:

| form | cones affected | what gets confused |
|---|---|---|
| **deuteranomaly / deuteranopia** | medium (green) shifted or missing | red, green, orange and brown; the commonest form |
| **protanomaly / protanopia** | long (red) shifted or missing | the same pairs, and reds look darker |
| **tritanopia** | short (blue) missing | blue with green, yellow with pink; rare |
| **achromatopsia** | all | everything is grey; very rare |

The left chart uses red and green, which are the first two colours many tools offer for two series.
The right is the same chart passed through a **simulation of deuteranopia**: both lines become an olive
brown, and where South and Northeast cross in 2024 the reader cannot tell which went up. The legend is
no help, because its two swatches have the same problem.

Note what survives: **lightness**. The two olives differ slightly in lightness and not at all in hue,
which is why lesson 11 said lightness is the dimension that reaches most readers. A palette whose
colours differ in lightness, and not only in hue, already works for most forms of colour blindness.

## Simulating it yourself

The figure was computed with the matrices published by Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes in 2009, which
transform a colour into what a person with a given deficiency perceives. The next section's script
uses the same matrix. Without code, two easy checks exist:

- browsers' developer tools can **emulate vision deficiencies** on any page, including a dashboard;
- free simulators such as **Coblis** take an uploaded image and show it as each form would see it.

Run your charts through one before anyone else sees them.

title: The baseline is zero
version: 1

Almost everything in this course is judgement. This section is the exception. **A bar or a column
encodes its number as a length measured from the baseline, so the baseline has to be zero.** Move it,
and the lengths stop being the numbers.

Northeast had 39,924 orders in 2025 and South had 34,926: Northeast is **14% larger**. On the left,
with the axis from zero, the columns say so. On the right the axis starts at 34,000, so the visible
part of each column is only what is above 34,000: 5,924 for Northeast and 926 for South. The eye
compares those, and sees one column **6.4 times** the other.

## "But the difference is too small to see"

That is the usual reason for cutting the axis, and it deserves a straight answer: **if a difference
is too small to see on an honest bar chart, then it is small.** The chart is telling the truth. If
the small difference matters anyway, there are honest ways to show it:

- **Plot the difference itself.** A column of +4,998 orders, from zero, shows the gap directly.
- **Plot the change as a percentage**, again from zero: +14% for Northeast over South.
- **Use a dot plot** instead of bars. A dot marks a position rather than a length, so its axis may
  start anywhere, and lesson 2 put position at the top of the ladder.
- **Write the numbers on the chart**, beside bars that start at zero.

## Lines and dots are different

A line chart or a dot plot does **not** need a zero baseline, and lesson 4 explains why: they
encode values as positions, not lengths, and the reader compares positions against the axis
labels. A line chart of a temperature that runs between 18 and 24 degrees should show 18 to 24.
The rule in this section belongs to every mark whose **length or area** carries the number: bars,
columns, area charts.

## Breaks in the axis

Some charts draw a zigzag on a column to say "part of this bar has been removed". It is honest in
intent and still makes the reader compare lengths that are not the numbers. If one value is so large
that the others are squashed, **a second chart, or a logarithmic scale on a dot plot** (lesson 6),
serves the reader better than a broken bar.

## Where the truncated axis comes from

It is not always somebody trying to deceive. Some spreadsheet programs choose an axis minimum above
zero by themselves when all the values are close together, and the person making the chart never
looks. **Check the axis every time**, in every tool, and set the minimum to zero by hand when it is
not.

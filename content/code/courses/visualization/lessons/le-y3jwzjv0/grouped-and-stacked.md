## Grouped bars compare the parts

On the left, each region has two columns, 2024 and 2025, side by side and sharing the baseline.
Every column starts at zero, so **every part can be compared with every other part**: Southeast
grew from 67,663 to 77,567, and the more interesting story is visible too: **Northeast passed South**,
from 27,564 against 28,279 in 2024 to 39,924 against 34,926 in 2025.

What grouped bars do not show well is the **total**. To compare 2024 with 2025 as a whole, the reader
would have to add five columns in their head.

## Stacked bars compare the totals

On the right, each year is one column with the five regions stacked inside it. **The totals are now
the heights**, and 180,682 against 143,512 is a comparison of two lengths from zero.

The parts are another matter. **Only the bottom segment sits on the baseline.** Southeast, at the
bottom, can be compared between the years. Every segment above it starts wherever the one below
ended, so comparing Northeast in 2024 with Northeast in 2025 means comparing two lengths with
different starting points, which is lesson 2's second rung at best and usually worse.

## Choosing

| the reader needs to compare | use |
|---|---|
| each part with the same part in another group | grouped bars |
| the totals of the groups | stacked bars, or plain bars of the totals |
| the totals **and** one part that matters most | stacked bars, with that part at the bottom |
| shares of each group (percentages) | 100% stacked bars, with the part that matters at the bottom |

The third row is a design trick worth keeping: **the segment on the baseline is the only one read
accurately, so give that place to the one the reader most needs.**

## When neither works

With more than three or four parts per group, grouped bars become a comb and stacked bars become a
striped column nobody can read. The answer is usually to **split the chart into several small
ones**, one per part, sharing an axis. Lesson 10 is about exactly that.

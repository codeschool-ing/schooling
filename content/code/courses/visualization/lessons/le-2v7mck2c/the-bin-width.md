without asking: **the width of the bins**.

- **2 minutes**: 52 bins and a ragged skyline. The small spikes and dips are not features of Horta's
  deliveries; with only 400 values, a bin two minutes wide holds a few deliveries, and a few more or
  fewer is chance. **Too narrow shows the noise.**
- **5 minutes**: 21 bins. One peak, a clear right tail, and nothing that looks like an accident.
- **15 minutes**: 7 bins. The tail has been swallowed: 50 to 105 minutes is spread over a few low
  bars, and the shape is a block. **Too wide hides the structure.**

There is no single correct width. The aim is the **widest bin that still shows every real feature,
and the narrowest that does not show noise**, and between those two there is usually a comfortable
range.

## Where the bins start

The width is not the only hidden choice. **Where the first bin begins** moves every edge, and with it
every bar.

Both charts use bins 10 minutes wide. On the left they start at 0: 30 to 40 holds 158 deliveries. On
the right they start at 5: 25 to 35 holds 161 and 35 to 45 holds 107. The peak seems to move by five
minutes and the right side looks steeper, though no delivery changed.

The practical rule: **try a few widths and a couple of starting points before believing a shape.**
A feature that survives all of them is in the data. A feature that appears in one and vanishes in the
next was made by the bins.

## Make the edges readable

Pick widths and starts that a reader can name: 5, 10, 15 minutes; R$ 10 or R$ 50 bands; edges at
round numbers. A bin from 23.7 to 28.6 minutes is a bin nobody can talk about.

A pie chart encodes each value twice, as the **angle** at the centre and as the **area** of the
slice, and both sit on the lower rungs of the ladder. With two or three slices that hardly matters.
With six it does. Here is Horta's revenue for 2025, by category, as a pie and as bars.

The numbers are close on purpose, because close numbers are what real categories look like:
Vegetables 21.0% of revenue, Fruit 19.7%, Dairy 17.9%, Bakery 15.2%, Drinks 14.0%, Pantry 12.2%.

Three questions show the difference.

- **Which is larger, Vegetables or Fruit?** In the pie the two slices start at different angles
  and point in different directions, and a gap of 1.3 points is invisible. In the bars both start
  at zero, and the ends are compared against the same axis.
- **Is Dairy more than Pantry by a third or by a half?** The real answer is 47%. In the bars you can
  nearly measure it with your eye. In the pie you cannot.
- **What is the order?** The bars are sorted, and a sorted bar chart *is* the order. A pie can be
  sorted too, but going round a circle has no natural start, and the reader has to work out where
  the largest slice begins.

## What people do to rescue a pie

They print the percentage on every slice. That works, and it shows the problem: **a pie that needs
its numbers written on it is a table drawn in a circle.** The chart has stopped doing a chart's
job, which is to let the eye compare without reading.

The other rescues make it worse. A **3D pie** tilts the circle so the front slices are drawn larger
than the back ones, and the same number looks bigger in front. An **exploded pie**, with slices
pulled away from the centre, removes the one thing the eye could use, which is slices sharing an
edge. Lesson 16 comes back to both as charts that mislead.

## And donuts?

A donut chart is a pie with the middle cut out. It removes the angle at the centre and leaves the
reader to judge arc length, which is no better. It makes room for a total in the middle, which is
its only real advantage, and the same total can sit above a bar chart.

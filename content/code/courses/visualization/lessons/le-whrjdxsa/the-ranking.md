Lesson 1 showed that the same five numbers read differently in different channels. This section
puts an order on that, and the order comes from measurement rather than from anybody's taste.

In 1984 William Cleveland and Robert McGill, two statisticians at Bell Labs, published *Graphical
Perception*. They showed people pairs of marks, one pair at a time, and asked them to say what
percentage the smaller was of the larger. Then they compared the answers with the truth. Each
channel got its own set of pairs: two dots on one scale, two bars, two angles, two circles, two
shades.

The **errors grew as they went down the ladder**. Judgements of position on a common scale were
the closest to the truth. Length came next, then angle, then area, with shading last. In 2010
Jeffrey Heer and Michael Bostock repeated the experiments with people recruited online and
got the same order.

## What "accurate" means here

It does not mean that a pie is unreadable. Everybody can see that the biggest slice is the biggest.
It means that **the size of a difference** is judged worse: a reader shown two slices of 20% and 18%
will often not know which is larger, and a reader shown two bars of 20 and 18 on one axis almost always
will.

That matters because the question a chart answers is almost always about a difference. Did this
region grow more than that one? Is this category bigger than that one, and by how much? A channel
that blurs differences blurs the answer.

## How to use the ranking

**Go down the ladder only as far as you have to.** Start with position on a common scale: dots or
bar ends against one axis. Use a lower rung when the higher ones are taken by something else. On a
map, position is used up by geography, so the number has to go into colour or area (lesson 8). In a
scatterplot, both positions carry the two main variables, so a third one has to go into size or
colour (lesson 6).

**The ranking is about quantities.** For categories, hue and shape do the work and the ladder does
not apply, because there is no amount to misjudge.

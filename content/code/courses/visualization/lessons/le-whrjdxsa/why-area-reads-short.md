In lesson 1 the circle for Southeast had 6.5 times the area of the circle for North, and most
readers judged it at three or four times. That is not carelessness. It is how perception of size
works, and it has a formula.

## Stevens's power law

In the 1950s the psychologist S. S. Stevens asked people to put numbers on sensations: how loud,
how bright, how long, how large. Across dozens of senses he found the same shape. **Perceived size
grows as the real size raised to a power**:

```localised
perceived ratio = (real ratio) ^ exponent
```

The exponent depends on what is being judged. For the **length** of a line it is close to 1, so a
line twice as long looks twice as long. For **area** it is lower, around 0.7 in most studies,
though the measured values range from about 0.6 to 0.8. For **volume** it is lower still.

Put the circles from lesson 1 into the formula and the readers' answer comes out:

```
ana@vm:~/viz$ .venv/bin/python -c "print(round(6.5 ** 0.7, 2), round(10 ** 0.7, 2), round(2 ** 0.7, 2))"
3.71 5.01 1.62
```

A ratio of **6.5 looks like 3.7**. Ten times the area looks like five times, and even twice the area
looks like only 1.6 times.

## What it means for a chart

**Every chart that puts a number into area understates the big values.** A bubble chart, a
proportional symbol map, a treemap, a pie: the largest marks always look smaller, relative to the
rest, than the data says.

Two responses are reasonable, and one is not.

- **Prefer length or position** when the comparison matters. This is the ranking from the first
  section, now with a reason.
- **When area is the right channel** (lesson 6 has bubbles, lesson 8 has symbols on a map), size
  the marks honestly by area and **label the values that matter**, so the reader does not have to
  judge them.
- **Do not "correct" the areas** by inflating the big ones to compensate. Some cartographers did,
  after James Flannery proposed it in 1971. It fixes the average reader and misleads the reader who
  measures, and the exponent varies too much from person to person to correct for anybody in
  particular.

Some charts survive being made narrow and some do not. The difference is almost always the text.

## Columns become bars

A column chart puts its labels beneath the columns, side by side, so each label gets the width of one
column. On a phone that is about 60 pixels, and "Centre-West" does not fit. The tool's usual answer is
to turn the labels sideways, which the reader then reads with their head tilted.

**On a narrow screen, width is scarce and height is free**, because the page scrolls. Horizontal bars
spend the free dimension on the labels: each name gets a whole line, level, with the value at the end
of its bar.

## Fewer ticks, and the value on the line

A time axis that held five dates across a laptop has room for two or three on a phone. Keep the ones
that frame the period, the first and the last, and let the line show the shape between them.

If the reader needs a value, write it on the line where it matters, usually the last point. That is
lesson 15's direct label, and on a phone it replaces both the gridlines and the hover tooltip.

## Other things that break

- **Legends.** A legend beside a chart has no room beside it on a phone. Direct labels need none.
- **Long titles.** A claim title that fits one line on a laptop may wrap to three. Shorten it, or
  accept two lines and check the chart below still starts where the eye expects.
- **Small multiples.** Twelve small panels in a grid become twelve panels in a column, which may be
  too long. Two columns of tiny panels can work if each one only needs to show a shape.
- **Maps.** A choropleth of Brazil keeps its shape at any width, but its smallest states become
  untappable. A sorted bar chart beside or instead of it is often the better phone version.

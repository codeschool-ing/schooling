Most chart titles are **labels**: they name what the chart contains. "Monthly orders by region,
2024–2025." A label is accurate, and it hands the whole job of interpretation to the reader, who may
find the point, a different point, or nothing.

A **claim title** states what the chart shows, as a sentence. "Northeast overtook South in October
2024 and kept pulling away." The chart below it becomes the evidence.

## Why claim titles work

- **They are read first.** The title is where the eye starts, so whatever it says frames everything
  after. A claim tells the reader what to check; a label tells them what to look at.
- **They can be checked.** A reader can hold "Northeast overtook South in October 2024" against the
  lines and agree or object. That is honest in a way an unexplained chart is not: the claim is out in
  the open.
- **They survive being forwarded.** A chart pasted into a chat or a slide without the paragraph that
  explained it still says what it was for.

## How to write one

- **One sentence, with a verb**: grew, fell, overtook, doubled, stayed flat.
- **The finding, not the method**: a title such as "Late deliveries rise when it rains", not
  "Analysis of delivery times by weather".
- **Numbers when they are the point**: "North grew 60.5%, four times Southeast's rate".
- **Keep the label as a subtitle**, in a quieter style beneath the claim: what is measured, where,
  when, in what unit.

## When not to

A claim title must be **true of the chart as drawn**. It is the place where lesson 6's warning about
causes bites hardest: "Rain makes deliveries slower" claims a cause; "Deliveries in rain took
longer on average" claims what the data shows. Write the second unless the analysis supports
the first.

And some charts are made for the reader to explore, such as a dashboard's filters or a reference
table of every state. There a label title is right, because there is no single finding to state.

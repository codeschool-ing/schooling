An **effect size** describes how large a difference or association is, independently of the sample size. Several are already familiar from this course.

## In the original units

The most useful effect size is usually the plainest: the difference in the units the decision is made in. **0.32 minutes** per delivery for the bags. **6.34 minutes** for rain, in lesson 19's model. **2.1 percentage points** of conversion for the checkout page of lesson 16, from 11.0% to 13.1%. Reported with its confidence interval, a difference in natural units needs no further translation.

## Relative to the spread: Cohen's d

When outcomes are measured in different units, or the units mean little to the reader, the difference can be expressed in standard deviations. **Cohen's d** is the difference of two means divided by their pooled standard deviation:

**d = (mean₁ − mean₂) ÷ pooled standard deviation**

For the bags, d = −0.32 ÷ 10.48 = **−0.03**. For the training course of lesson 16, where the same couriers were measured twice, the standardised change is the mean change divided by the standard deviation of the changes: −1.21 ÷ 1.65 = **−0.73**.

The psychologist Jacob Cohen suggested rough labels: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. He meant them as a last resort, for fields with no better yardstick, and warned against applying them mechanically. A d of 0.03 is negligible on any scale; whether a d of 0.2 matters depends entirely on what is being measured.

## Relative to the baseline

Rates are often reported as a relative change: the checkout page raised conversion by 2.1 points, which is **19%** more than the old page's 11.0%. Relative changes make small effects sound large, and absolute changes make them sound small. A report should give both, and the baseline.

## The share explained

For associations, lesson 17's r² and lesson 19's R² are effect sizes: the number of items explained **4%** of the variation in delivery times, with p = 0.032. That correlation was significant and small, the same pattern as the bags.

Lesson 14 promised an example of a tiny difference with a p-value smaller than any in that lesson. Here it is.

## The bags

For a year, Horta's couriers used two kinds of insulated bag: 100,000 deliveries in the old bags, 100,000 in a new design. Delivery times:

| | deliveries | mean | standard deviation |
|---|---|---|---|
| old bags | 100,000 | 40.01 minutes | 10.51 |
| new bags | 100,000 | 39.69 minutes | 10.45 |

The new bags were faster by **0.32 minutes**, about nineteen seconds. Welch's test gives t = −6.82 and **p = 9 × 10⁻¹²**: if the bags made no difference, a gap this large would turn up about once in a hundred billion trials. The 95% confidence interval for the difference runs from −0.41 to −0.23 minutes. There is no reasonable doubt that the new bags are faster.

And yet the two distributions are almost indistinguishable. A delivery in a new bag is faster than the median old-bag delivery 51.1% of the time, against the 50% it would be if they were identical.

## Why the p-value is so small

The p-value measures the difference against its standard error, and the standard error shrinks as the sample grows. Fix the true difference at 0.3 minutes, with a spread of 10.5 minutes, and see what happens to the expected test statistic as the sample grows:

With 50 deliveries per group, a 0.3-minute difference is invisible: the expected statistic is 0.14 and p is about 0.89. Around 9,400 per group it crosses the line where p = 0.05. With 100,000 per group, p is around 10⁻¹⁰. The effect never changed. Only the amount of data did.

**With enough data, any difference that is not exactly zero becomes significant.** In very large data sets, from web traffic to national surveys, almost everything is significant, and the p-value stops being informative about anything but the sample size.

The opposite of the bags' mistake is the more common one in small studies: treating "not significant" as "no effect".

## The same effect, fifty deliveries

Suppose Horta had tested the new bags on 50 deliveries each instead of 100,000. One such trial, simulated with the same true difference, gave a difference of −1.44 minutes, a 95% interval from **−5.18 to +2.29**, and p = 0.45. Not significant.

That trial says nothing about whether the bags work. Its interval is consistent with the bags saving five minutes, costing two, or doing nothing at all. **Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence**, as lesson 14 put it. A non-significant result from a study that could not have detected the effect is not a finding.

## Why small studies mislead in both directions

Lesson 15 showed the other half. A small study that does reach significance tends to **exaggerate** the effect, because only the samples that happened to show a large difference cross the line. So small studies produce two kinds of misleading result: nothing where there is something, and something too big where there is something small. Both come from the same cause: too little data for the size of the effect.

## What to report instead

For a non-significant result, report the interval and say what it rules out and what it does not:

- **"The trial could not rule out an improvement of up to 3.4 minutes, nor a slowdown of 1.2."** Honest, and it tells a reader that a larger trial might be worth running.
- **"The new system had no effect."** Wrong, and the kind of sentence that buries good ideas.

When the interval is narrow and sits inside the band of what does not matter, as with the new app, the conclusion is stronger: there is good evidence that any effect is too small to matter.

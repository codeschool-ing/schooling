**Rain**, from lesson 19: 4.7 to 8.0 minutes. Away from zero, and entirely beyond the band: significant and important. Plans for rainy days should allow several extra minutes.

**New bags**: −0.41 to −0.23 minutes. Away from zero, deep inside the band: significant, and too small to matter for any one delivery.

**Training course**, from lesson 16: −2.4 to −0.03 minutes. Away from zero, so the training probably helps. But the interval runs from almost nothing to just past the threshold. The data cannot say whether the effect is worth the course. A larger study would.

**Routing system**, from lesson 13: −3.4 to +1.2 minutes. It covers zero, so the test was not significant. It also reaches well past −2, so an improvement worth the cost is entirely consistent with the data. **Not significant, and not evidence of no effect**: the trial was too small to tell.

**New app**: −0.14 to +0.68 minutes, from a trial of 5,000 deliveries each way. It covers zero, p = 0.20, not significant. But unlike the routing trial, the whole interval lies inside the band. The app may change delivery times slightly, but by less than any amount that would matter. **Not significant, and good evidence that there is nothing worth worrying about.**

The last two have the same verdict from a test, "not significant", and opposite meanings. Only the intervals show the difference.

## Testing for no important effect

The new app's conclusion can be made formal with an **equivalence test**. Instead of testing whether the effect is zero, it tests whether the effect is inside the band, by running two one-sided tests: is it above −2, and is it below +2? Both must reject. With the band set at ±1 minute instead, the new app still passes, with p = 0.0003. Equivalence tests are standard in medicine, where a cheaper drug must be shown to work no worse than an existing one, and are a useful tool whenever the claim to support is "this change does no harm".

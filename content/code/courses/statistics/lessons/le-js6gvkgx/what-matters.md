title: Deciding what matters
version: 1
---

Statistics can say how large an effect probably is. Whether that size **matters** is a judgement about the world, and it should be made before the data arrives.

## The smallest effect worth acting on

Lesson 15 sized the routing trial around a business calculation: the system costs R$ 1,500 a month, and every minute off the average delivery is worth R$ 1,000, so an improvement smaller than two minutes would not pay for itself. **Two minutes is the smallest effect worth acting on.** Effects inside ±2 minutes are, for that decision, too small to matter, however significant.

Fields name this threshold differently: the **minimal important difference** in medicine, the **smallest effect size of interest** in psychology, a **minimum detectable effect** in online experiments. The idea is the same, and so is the rule: **choose it in advance**, from costs, benefits or what users can notice. A threshold chosen after seeing the result drifts towards the result.

## The same effect can matter or not

Whether an effect matters depends on what it is multiplied by. The bags save about nineteen seconds per delivery. No customer will notice that. But across 100,000 deliveries a year, it adds up to about **530 courier-hours**. If the new bags cost the same as the old ones, that is worth having; if they cost much more, it may not be. The size of the effect per delivery does not change. What it is worth depends on the question.

## Significant and important are independent

A result can be any of four things:

| | important | not important |
|---|---|---|
| **significant** | a real effect worth acting on | a real effect too small to matter |
| **not significant** | a possibly important effect the study could not pin down | no evidence of an effect, and possibly evidence of none |

The bags are top right. The bottom row is the subject of the section after next.

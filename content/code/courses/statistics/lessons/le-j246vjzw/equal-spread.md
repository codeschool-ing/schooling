The assumption of **equal variance**, also called **homoscedasticity**, asks that the residuals spread about as much for large predictions as for small ones. Its failure, **heteroscedasticity**, has a characteristic shape.

## What goes wrong

The slope is still a fair estimate. But the standard errors assume one common spread, so they are too small where the spread is large and too large where it is small, and the p-values and intervals inherit the error. A prediction interval of the same width for a 2-item order and a 20-item order is too wide for one and much too narrow for the other.

## Fixing it

**Transform the response.** When the spread grows in proportion to the size of the response, the logarithm of the response often evens it out. On the right of the figure, the logarithm of the basket regressed on the logarithm of the items gives residuals with a typical size of **0.29** for small orders and **0.32** for large ones: one band, the same width throughout.

The slope of that log-log line is **0.99**:

```localised
=SLOPE(LN(G2:G201), LN(F2:F201))    0.994544987258608
```

On logarithms, a slope close to 1 means the response grows in proportion to the predictor: doubling the items about doubles the basket. Current spreadsheets accept `LN` of a whole range inside `SLOPE`; older ones need the logarithms in their own columns first.

**Use standard errors that allow for unequal spread.** Statistical software offers versions, often called robust or heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors, that do not assume one common spread. They leave the coefficients alone and correct the uncertainty around them.

The most serious failure is a model of the wrong shape. Every prediction is then off in a systematic way, and no amount of data fixes it.

## The discount data

Lesson 17's discounts and weekly orders climbed fast and then flattened. A straight line through them has R² = 0.71, which sounds respectable. Its residuals tell a different story:

At no discount, the line overestimates orders by 95. In the middle it underestimates them by up to 46. At 20% it overestimates them again by 47. The residuals form an **arch**: negative, then positive, then negative. The line is too high at the ends and too low in the middle because the data curves and the line cannot.

## Fixing it

**Transform a variable.** Replacing the discount with the logarithm of one plus the discount turns a curve that flattens into something closer to a straight line. The fit improves to R² = **0.97**, and the residuals shrink to between −23 and +18. But they still arch: the logarithm flattens a little differently from these orders. A transformation that leaves a pattern is better, not finished.

**Add a curved term.** A model with discount and discount squared, a **quadratic**, can bend once. Here it reaches R² = 0.96, and its residuals wave instead of arching, a sign that the parabola fits the middle and misses at the ends, where it starts to turn back down.

**Use a model built for the shape.** Orders that rise towards a ceiling are better described by a curve with a ceiling in it. Fitting such curves is beyond this course, but recognising that the data needs one is not.

## Why it matters

A model of the wrong shape gives slopes that describe no part of the data well, predictions that are biased in a predictable direction, and p-values that answer a question nobody asked. A high R² does not protect against it: the straight line had R² = 0.71 and was wrong almost everywhere.

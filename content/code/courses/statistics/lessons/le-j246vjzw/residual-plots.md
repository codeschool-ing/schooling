The points form a band around zero, about as wide on the left as on the right, with no curve and no point far from the rest. That is what a well-specified model leaves behind: whatever structure the data had, the model has used, and what remains looks like noise.

## Why plot residuals instead of the data

With one predictor, a curve or a funnel can often be seen in the scatter plot itself. With several predictors there is no single scatter plot to look at, and the residual plot still works: one axis is the prediction, combining all the predictors, and the other is what the prediction missed. It also magnifies problems. A curve that bends the data by three minutes is hard to see when the data spans forty; in a residual plot, three minutes fills the picture.

## Residuals against other variables

A residual plot can also use a variable that is **not** in the model, to see whether it should be. In the distance-only model, the 19 rainy deliveries have an average residual of **+4.99 minutes** and the dry ones **−0.94**: the model consistently underestimates rainy deliveries. That is the signal that rain belongs in the model, as lesson 19 found.

## The four assumptions

The least-squares line itself needs no assumptions: it is always the line that minimises squared residuals. The **standard errors, p-values and intervals** do. They rest on four conditions, often remembered as **LINE**:

- **L**inearity: the average response really changes in a straight line with each predictor.
- **I**ndependence: the residuals of different observations are unrelated.
- **N**ormality: the residuals roughly follow a normal distribution.
- **E**qual variance: the residuals spread about as much at every predicted value.

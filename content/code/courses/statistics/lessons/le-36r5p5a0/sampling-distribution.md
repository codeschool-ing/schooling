Lesson 10 drew 1,000 random samples of 40 baskets and found that their means spread from R$ 61.53 to R$ 116.79. Each sample gave one mean. Together, the 1,000 means have a distribution of their own: a centre, a spread and a shape.

That distribution has a name: the **sampling distribution** of the mean. It is the distribution of a **statistic**, computed from many samples, as opposed to the distribution of the **data**, which describes the individual values.

## Three distributions, not to be confused

Keeping them apart is half the battle in this lesson and the next.

| | what it describes | for the baskets |
|---|---|---|
| the population | every value in the population | 400 baskets, mean R$ 82.78, skewed right |
| one sample | the values in one sample | 40 baskets, one histogram, one mean |
| the sampling distribution | a statistic over every possible sample | the means of all possible samples of 40 |

You see only the middle row in practice: one sample. The population is what you want to learn about. The sampling distribution is the bridge between them, because it says how far one sample's mean is likely to be from the population's.

## Why it matters

If you knew the sampling distribution, you could say: "the mean of a random sample of 40 lands within about R$ 18 of the true mean 95% of the time". Then one sample's mean would come with a statement of how much to trust it.

The trouble is that the sampling distribution comes from **every possible sample**, and you have one. The simulation above worked only because the 400 baskets were treated as a known population, which in real life you never have.

What rescues the situation is a theorem: for the mean, the shape and spread of the sampling distribution can be **predicted** from a single sample, without drawing thousands. The next section states it.

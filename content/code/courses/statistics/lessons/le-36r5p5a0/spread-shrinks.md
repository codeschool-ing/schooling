title: The spread shrinks with √n
version: 1

Of the theorem's three promises, the one with the most practical weight is the spread: the standard deviation of the sample means is **σ ÷ √*n***, where σ is the population's standard deviation and *n* the sample size.

## Checked against the simulation

For the baskets, σ = R$ 58.82. The formula predicts, and the 2,000 simulated samples found:

| *n* | σ ÷ √*n* | simulated |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 58.82 | 58.07 |
| 2 | 41.59 | 41.75 |
| 5 | 26.30 | 26.56 |
| 10 | 18.60 | 19.22 |
| 30 | 10.74 | 10.67 |
| 100 | 5.88 | 5.78 |

The simulated values wobble around the formula, as any simulation does, and follow it closely.

## The square root is the hard part

The spread falls with the **square root** of the sample size, not with the size itself. That has a consequence lesson 10 already met: **four times the data halves the spread**, and a hundred times the data cuts it to a tenth.

- From 1 basket to 4, the spread falls from R$ 58.82 to R$ 29.41: a big gain for three extra baskets.
- From 100 baskets to 400, the spread falls from R$ 5.88 to R$ 2.94: the same halving, for three hundred extra baskets.

Precision gets expensive. Each halving of the uncertainty costs four times the data, which is why surveys of a few hundred or a few thousand people are common and surveys of a million are not: the millionth respondent adds almost nothing.

## Why averaging helps at all

A single basket can be anywhere from R$ 14 to R$ 422. An average of 100 baskets is much harder to push around, because a big basket pulled into the sample is outweighed by 99 others. The more values share the average, the less any one of them can move it, and the √*n* says exactly how much less.

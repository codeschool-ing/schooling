A confounder can do more than create a correlation. It can **reverse** one. This is **Simpson's paradox**, named after Edward Simpson, who described it in 1951.

## Motorbikes and bicycles

Horta delivers by motorbike and by bicycle. Over one month, here is how often each was on time:

| | near | far | all deliveries |
|---|---|---|---|
| motorbike | 88 of 94, **93.6%** | 201 of 276, **72.8%** | 289 of 370, **78.1%** |
| bicycle | 241 of 280, **86.1%** | 52 of 76, **68.4%** | 293 of 356, **82.3%** |

Motorbikes are on time more often for near deliveries, and more often for far ones. Yet across all deliveries, bicycles are on time more often. Both statements are true, and they come from the same 726 deliveries.

## What is going on

Far deliveries are late much more often, for every vehicle. And motorbikes do most of the far deliveries: 276 of their 370, against 76 of the bicycles' 356. The motorbike's overall rate is mostly a far-delivery rate; the bicycle's is mostly a near-delivery rate. Comparing the two totals compares mostly-hard jobs with mostly-easy ones.

Distance is the confounder here, as with the coupons. It affects both which vehicle is used and whether the delivery is on time.

## Which answer is right?

It depends on the causal story, not on the arithmetic. Here distance influences the choice of vehicle, so the fair comparison holds distance fixed: **motorbikes are more punctual**, and the overall figure is misleading.

But there are cases where the subgroups are the misleading view: when the variable used to split the data is itself an effect of the thing being studied. Splitting a medical trial by blood pressure measured after the treatment, when the treatment works by lowering blood pressure, would hide the treatment's effect. **No rule about tables decides this; only knowing how the variables affect each other does.** That is why lesson 19's regressions need thought about which variables to include.

---
title: Which way does the arrow point?
version: 1
---

Even when one variable really does affect the other, a correlation does not say which affects which.

## Examples

**Couriers and lateness.** Horta's dispatcher notices that neighbourhoods with more couriers on shift have more late deliveries. Do extra couriers get in each other's way? More likely the dispatcher sends extra couriers to the neighbourhoods that are running late. Lateness causes couriers, not the reverse.

**Ratings and repeat orders.** Customers who order often give higher ratings. Perhaps a good experience brings people back; or perhaps people who already like Horta, for reasons of their own, both order more and rate higher. The correlation fits both.

**Exercise and health.** People who exercise are healthier. Part of that is exercise improving health, and part is that people who are ill exercise less. The arrow runs both ways.

## Time order helps, and is not enough

A cause has to come before its effect, so the order of events is evidence. If the extra couriers arrived **after** the lateness started, the reverse explanation is strongly suggested. When both are measured at the same moment, as in a single survey, the data alone cannot tell the directions apart.

But "first A, then B" does not prove that A caused B either. The rooster crows before sunrise. The fallacy has a Latin name, *post hoc ergo propter hoc*, "after this, therefore because of this", which shows how long people have been making it.

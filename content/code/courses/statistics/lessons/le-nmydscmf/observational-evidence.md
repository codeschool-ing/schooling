Most data is observational: it records what happened without anybody assigning anything. Causal conclusions from it are possible, but they need more than a correlation.

## Adjusting for confounders

The coupon example did this by comparing within neighbourhoods. Regression, in lessons 19 and 20, does it with several variables at once. Both work only for the confounders that were **measured**. An adjusted result is a claim that nothing important was left out, and that claim should be stated and argued, not assumed.

## Natural experiments

Sometimes the world randomises for you. A rule that changes on a particular date, a boundary that puts similar streets on either side of a policy, a lottery that decides who gets a school place: each creates groups that differ in one thing for reasons unrelated to the outcome. If Campinas closed one bridge for repairs, comparing deliveries just before and after, on routes that used it and routes that did not, would estimate the bridge's effect almost as well as an experiment.

## A checklist for a causal claim

In 1965 the epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill listed questions that, together, made the case that smoking causes lung cancer, when no experiment was possible. A shortened version:

- **Strength.** Is the association large? Smokers had about ten times the lung cancer rate of non-smokers; a confounder would have had to be enormous to explain that.
- **Consistency.** Has it been found repeatedly, by different people, in different places?
- **Time order.** Does the cause come before the effect?
- **Dose and response.** Does more of the cause go with more of the effect? Heavier smokers had higher rates.
- **Mechanism.** Is there a plausible way for the cause to work?
- **Experiment.** Does removing the cause reduce the effect? People who quit had falling rates.

No single item is proof, and the absence of one does not rule out a cause. Together, they turn a correlation into a case.

## How to word a finding

When the data cannot support a causal claim, the wording should not make one. "Customers who used coupons had longer deliveries" is a correct description. "Coupons lengthen deliveries" is a causal claim the data does not support. Watch for verbs that smuggle in a cause: **drives**, **leads to**, **boosts**, **affects**, **because of**.

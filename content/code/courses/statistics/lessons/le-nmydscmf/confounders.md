A **confounder** is a variable that influences both of the things being compared. It creates a correlation between them even when neither affects the other.

## The coupons

Horta sent free-delivery coupons to some customers. Of the 120 deliveries, 29 used one. Those took **46.1 minutes** on average; the other 91 took **36.3**. The difference, 9.8 minutes, is far beyond chance: Welch's test gives p = 0.0003.

But coupons were not handed out at random. The marketing team sent them where orders were scarce, which meant mostly to Barão Geraldo: 17 of the 29 coupons went there.

Lesson 19 does the same adjustment with a regression, holding distance fixed exactly rather than by neighbourhood: the coupon's coefficient drops from 9.8 minutes to **0.8**.

## Confounders are everywhere

- Towns with more ice-cream sales have more drownings. Hot weather drives both.
- Children with bigger shoes read better. Age drives both.
- Hospitals with more doctors per patient have higher death rates. The sickest patients are sent to the best-staffed hospitals.

In each case the correlation is real and the obvious causal story is wrong. The question to ask of any correlation is: **what else differs between the groups?**

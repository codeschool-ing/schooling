The point of a regression line is often to predict. That works well inside the data and badly outside it.

## A prediction

A customer six kilometres away: 25.03 + 2.48 × 6 = **39.9 minutes**.

```localised
=FORECAST.LINEAR(6, D2:D121, A2:A121)    39.9262752869543
```

That is the **average** delivery time the line expects at six kilometres. A single delivery will usually differ from it by about the residual standard deviation, four minutes either way, and sometimes by more. A promise built on 39.9 minutes would be broken about half the time. A promise of 39.9 + 2 × 4.28, about **48.5 minutes**, would be broken by about one delivery in forty, if the residuals behave roughly normally, as lesson 20 checks.

## Extrapolation

The 120 deliveries run from 1.0 to 13.9 km. Asked about 30 km, the line answers without hesitation: 25.03 + 2.48 × 30 = **99.5 minutes**.

Nothing in the data supports that number. Beyond 14 km, the courier might take a motorway and go faster per kilometre, or cross into a town with worse traffic and go slower, or leave the area Horta can serve at all. Predicting outside the range of the data is called **extrapolation**, and the line has no way of warning you that it is doing it.

The same applies at the other end. The intercept, 25 minutes at zero kilometres, is itself a small extrapolation, since no delivery was shorter than one kilometre.

**Use a regression line to predict inside the range of the data that built it.** Outside that range, any prediction is an assumption that the pattern carries on, and it should be labelled as one.

## Prediction is not intervention

The line predicts the time of a delivery that happens to be six kilometres away. It does not say what would happen if Horta moved its warehouse six kilometres closer to a customer. Usually the two agree; when there are confounders, as lesson 18 showed, they do not.

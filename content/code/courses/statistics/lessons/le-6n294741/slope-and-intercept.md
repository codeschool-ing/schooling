## The slope

The slope is **the change in the predicted response for one more unit of the predictor**. For the deliveries, 2.48 minutes per kilometre. Four kilometres more means about 4 × 2.48 = 9.9 minutes more, whatever the starting distance: that is what a straight line assumes.

The slope carries the units of both variables, minutes per kilometre, and changes with them: measured in metres, it would be 0.00248 minutes per metre. Unlike *r*, it is not a pure number. That is its strength. *r* = 0.91 says the relation is close; *b* = 2.48 says what each kilometre **costs**.

## Is the slope more than chance?

The slope estimated from 120 deliveries is a statistic, and like a mean it has a standard error. For the deliveries it is **0.101** minutes per kilometre. The test of whether the true slope is zero uses t = 2.48 ÷ 0.101 = **24.5**, with n − 2 = 118 degrees of freedom, the same t as the test of *r* in lesson 17. A 95% interval for the slope is roughly 2.48 ± 2 × 0.101, from **2.28 to 2.68** minutes per kilometre.

## The intercept

The intercept is the predicted response when the predictor is **zero**. For the deliveries, 25.0 minutes at zero kilometres. Whether that means anything depends on whether zero is a sensible value. Here it roughly does: about 25 minutes go on picking, packing and handing over, whatever the distance.

Often it does not. A regression of weight on height has an intercept at a height of zero, which describes nobody. For the twelve orders, the intercept is **−R$ 8.88**, the predicted basket for an order of no items, which is not a price anybody paid. The intercept is still needed to place the line; it just should not be read on its own when zero is outside the data.

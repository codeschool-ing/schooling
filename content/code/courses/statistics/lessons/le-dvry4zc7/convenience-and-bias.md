A **convenience sample** is made of whoever was easiest to reach. The customers who opened the app this week. The deliveries a manager happened to ride along on. Whoever answered the survey link.

Its problem is not size. It is that the easiest people to reach are rarely typical, and the sample **leans** towards them in a way the data cannot reveal.

## Bias does not shrink

Suppose Horta's dispatcher, short of time, measures delivery times only on the routes nearest the warehouse: Centro and Cambuí. The 60 deliveries there average **31.28 minutes**, while the true mean across all four neighbourhoods is **38.65**.

Take samples of 20 from those near routes, a thousand times, and their means centre on 31.29. Take all 60 and the answer is still 31.28. A larger convenience sample gives the same wrong answer more precisely. That is the defining difference between the two kinds of error:

- **Sampling error** is the wobble of a random sample. It is as likely to be too high as too low, and it **shrinks as the sample grows**.
- **Bias** is a lean in one direction, built into how the sample was chosen. It does **not shrink** with more data.

## The common shapes of bias

**Selection bias**: the way members are chosen favours some of them. A sampling frame that misses people, such as a phone survey of households without a phone, is the classic case.

**Voluntary response bias**: people choose themselves. An in-app rating prompt is answered most by the delighted and the furious, and least by the merely satisfied, which is most of them.

**Non-response bias**: the chosen people do not all answer, and those who do not answer differ from those who do. A survey of 400 randomly chosen customers that 90 answer is no longer a random sample of 400; it is a sample of the 90 who had time and cared.

**Survivorship bias**: only the ones that lasted are seen. Surveying current customers about why they like Horta says nothing about the people who left, and they are exactly the ones whose reasons matter.

## The 1936 poll

In 1936 the American magazine *The Literary Digest* mailed around ten million ballots to predict the presidential election, using lists of its own readers, telephone owners and car owners. About 2.4 million came back, and they predicted a clear win for Alf Landon. Franklin Roosevelt won in a landslide. In the middle of the Depression, owning a phone or a car was a mark of a better-off household, and the people who bothered to return a magazine's ballot were not typical either.

The same year, George Gallup predicted Roosevelt's win with a sample of about 50,000, chosen to resemble the electorate. The lesson has been retold ever since because it is so stark: **2.4 million biased answers lost to 50,000 well-chosen ones.**

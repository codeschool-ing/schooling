---
title: How big a sample?
version: 1
---

The question everybody asks first — how big? — is the one that matters least until the method is right. A well-chosen sample of 400 beats a badly chosen sample of 2.4 million, as the 1936 poll showed. With a sound method, though, size decides how precise the answer is.

## Precision grows slowly

Larger random samples wobble less, but not in proportion. Lesson 11 shows that the wobble of a sample mean shrinks with the **square root** of the sample size: four times the sample halves the wobble, and a hundred times the sample cuts it to a tenth.

That has a practical consequence. Going from 100 to 400 respondents is a big improvement; going from 4,000 to 4,300 hardly shows. Every survey reaches a point where more answers cost more than the precision they buy, and lesson 12 gives the arithmetic for finding it.

## The population's size barely matters

A surprising fact: for a random sample, **the size of the population hardly affects the precision**, as long as the sample is a small fraction of it. A random sample of 1,000 voters describes a city of a million about as precisely as a country of two hundred million.

The reason is that precision depends on how much the individual answers vary and how many there are, not on how many people were left out. Tasting a spoonful tells you about the soup whether the pot holds one litre or a hundred, provided the soup was stirred. **Stirring is random selection.** Without it, no spoonful is big enough.

## What size cannot fix

- **Bias.** A biased method gives a precise wrong answer.
- **Non-response.** 400 invitations that produce 90 answers are a sample of 90, chosen by the respondents.
- **The wrong population.** A perfect random sample of current customers says nothing about the ones who left.

So the order of decisions is fixed: **define the population, choose a method that gives everyone a known chance, then choose the size**. Most bad surveys got the first two wrong and spent their effort on the third.

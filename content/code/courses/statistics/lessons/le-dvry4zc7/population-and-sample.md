The **population** is the whole group a question is about. The **sample** is the part of it you actually observe.

Horta wants to know how satisfied its customers are with deliveries. The population is every active customer, around 9,600 households. Asking all of them is slow and expensive, and many would not answer anyway, so Horta asks 400. Those 400 are the sample, and the hope is that what they say describes the 9,600.

That hope is the subject of the next seven lessons. Going from a sample to a population is called **statistical inference**, and almost everything in statistics beyond description is inference.

## Parameters and statistics

A number that describes the population is a **parameter**. A number computed from a sample is a **statistic**. The distinction matters because the parameter is what you want and the statistic is what you have.

| | population parameter | sample statistic |
|---|---|---|
| mean | μ (mu) | *x̄* (x-bar) |
| standard deviation | σ (sigma) | *s* |
| proportion | *p* | *p̂* (p-hat) |

The Greek letters for the population and the Roman ones for the sample are a convention worth learning, because every formula from here on uses them. Lesson 3 promised this: *x̄* is a sample's mean, and μ is the mean of everything the sample came from.

A parameter is a fixed number, even if nobody knows it: the true mean satisfaction of all 9,600 households exists. A statistic changes from sample to sample: ask a different 400 households and you get a different *x̄*.

## A population is defined by the question

The population is not always a set of people, and it is not always finite.

- "How long do Horta's deliveries take?" — the population is **every delivery Horta makes**, including ones that have not happened yet.
- "What does a bag from this filling machine weigh?" — every bag the machine fills, under its current settings.
- "What do Horta's 9 staff earn?" — the 9 staff. Here the data is the whole population, there is nothing to infer, and lesson 5's division by *n* rather than *n* − 1 applies.

Writing the population down, in one sentence, is the first step of any study. It decides who could be in the sample, and therefore whom the conclusion can be about.

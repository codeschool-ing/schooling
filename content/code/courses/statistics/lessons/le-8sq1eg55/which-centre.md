title: Choosing between the three
version: 1
---

The three summaries answer three different questions, and choosing one is choosing the question.

| summary | the question it answers | needs at least |
|---|---|---|
| mean | if everyone got the same share of the total, how much each? | interval |
| median | what does the middle observation look like? | ordinal |
| mode | what happens most often? | nominal |

## Ask what the number will be used for

**Planning totals favours the mean.** Horta budgeting courier hours for next month wants the mean delivery time, because mean × number of deliveries is the total, and the total is what gets paid.

**Describing a typical case favours the median.** Horta telling a new customer how long delivery usually takes wants the median: half the deliveries come faster than 37.25 minutes. The mean of 38.96 is pulled up by two slow deliveries that most customers never experience.

**Stocking and choosing favour the mode.** Horta deciding which bag size to buy in bulk wants the most common order size.

## When they agree, and when they do not

On some data the three land close together. When a distribution is symmetric, with one peak in the middle and the values thinning out evenly on both sides, the mean, the median and the mode sit at the same place, and it hardly matters which you report.

On Horta's baskets they do not agree: the mean is R$ 79.17 and the median R$ 68.20. The gap is a message about the data's shape. A few large baskets — R$ 154.75 and R$ 212.60 — pull the mean up while the median stays with the bulk of the orders. Lesson 4 reads that message properly, and lesson 7 gives the shape a name.

## Report more than one

Nothing forbids reporting two. "Median delivery 37 minutes; mean 39" tells a reader both what a typical customer sees and what the fleet spends, and the gap between them tells them something about the slow deliveries. **A single number is a choice about what to hide**, and two numbers hide less.

## The common outcomes

- **A number, with roughly normal noise**: minutes, reais, weight. **Linear regression**, lessons 19 and 20.
- **Yes or no**: complained or not, late or not, bought or not. **Logistic regression**, this lesson.
- **A count**: complaints per week, items per order. Counts are whole numbers, cannot be negative, and their spread tends to grow with their mean, as with the Poisson distribution of lesson 8. **Poisson regression** models the logarithm of the expected count as a line.
- **One of several unordered categories**: payment by pix, card or cash. **Multinomial logistic regression** extends the logistic model to more than two outcomes.
- **Time until an event**: days until a customer's next order, or until they leave. Some customers have not reordered yet when the data is collected, so their times are incomplete. **Survival analysis** handles that.

Linear, logistic and Poisson regression are all special cases of one framework, the **generalised linear model**: a straight line in the predictors, linked to the outcome through a scale suited to it.

## Explaining and predicting

The models in this course are used for two different purposes, and the difference shapes what matters.

**Explaining** asks what a coefficient is: how much does an extra kilometre cost, how much more likely is a new customer to complain? It needs interpretable coefficients, attention to confounders, and the checks of lesson 20.

**Predicting** asks how well the model forecasts new cases. It cares less about why, and more about the error on data the model has not seen.

When prediction is the goal and there are many predictors with complicated interactions, methods such as decision trees, random forests and gradient boosting often predict better than a regression, at some cost in interpretability. Those are the subject of the **machine-learning** course, which builds on the ideas here: fitting by minimising a loss, the danger of overfitting, and the habit of judging a model on data it was not trained on.

A logistic model gives a probability. Often what is needed is a decision: Horta wants to call the customers most likely to complain, before they do, and apologise. That needs a **cut-off**: call every order whose predicted probability is above it.

## The confusion table

Each cut-off sorts the orders into four groups:

- **true positives**: predicted to complain, and did;
- **false negatives**: predicted not to complain, but did;
- **false positives**: predicted to complain, but did not;
- **true negatives**: predicted not to complain, and did not.

## Four rates

From the table, four rates describe how a classifier performs. For a cut-off of 0.5:

- **Accuracy**, the share of all orders classified correctly: (81 + 258) ÷ 400 = **84.8%**.
- **Sensitivity**, or recall, the share of real complaints caught: 81 ÷ 114 = **71.1%**.
- **Specificity**, the share of quiet orders correctly left alone: 258 ÷ 286 = **90.2%**.
- **Precision**, the share of calls that reach a real complaint: 81 ÷ 109 = **74.3%**.

## Moving the cut-off

Lowering the cut-off to 0.2 calls more customers. Sensitivity rises to **87.7%**: 100 of the 114 complaints are caught, against 81. Specificity falls to **76.9%**, and precision to **60.2%**: four calls in ten now reach somebody who was never going to complain. Accuracy falls to 80.0%.

Neither cut-off is right in general. The choice depends on the costs: if a missed complaint loses a customer worth hundreds of reais and an unnecessary call costs a minute of staff time, the lower cut-off is the better business decision even though it is less accurate. **Choose the cut-off from the costs of the two kinds of mistake**, not by default at 0.5.

## Accuracy can mislead

A "model" that predicts no complaint for every order is right for every order that did not complain: 286 of 400, an accuracy of **71.5%**. It catches no complaints at all. When one outcome is much more common than the other, accuracy rewards predicting the common one, and sensitivity and precision tell you more. This matters most for rare events, such as fraud or equipment failure, where a useless model can be 99% accurate.

## Test on new data

Lesson 19's warning applies here too. These rates were computed on the same 400 orders the model was fitted to, so they flatter it. A fair estimate tests the classifier on orders it has not seen.

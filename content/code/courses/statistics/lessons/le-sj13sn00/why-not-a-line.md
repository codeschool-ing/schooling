Horta recorded 400 orders: how long each delivery took, whether it was the customer's first order, and whether the customer complained. **114** of the 400 complained, 28.5%.

The outcome is **binary**: 1 for a complaint, 0 for none. Its average over a group of orders is the share that complained, which is the probability of a complaint for orders like those. Grouping by delivery time shows how that probability rises:

| delivery minutes | complaints | share |
|---|---|---|
| 20 to 30 | 0 of 82 | 0% |
| 30 to 40 | 4 of 78 | 5.1% |
| 40 to 50 | 13 of 82 | 15.9% |
| 50 to 60 | 28 of 65 | 43.1% |
| 60 to 70 | 69 of 93 | 74.2% |

## Fitting a line anyway

Least squares will fit a line to zeros and ones. It gives

**predicted probability = −0.594 + 0.0195 × minutes**

At 20 minutes it predicts a probability of **−0.21**, which is impossible. Past about 82 minutes it predicts more than 1, also impossible. And in between it gets the shape wrong: the real share barely moves from 20 to 40 minutes and then climbs steeply, while a line climbs at the same rate everywhere.

The residuals break lesson 20's assumptions too. A binary outcome's residuals can take only two values at each prediction, so they are not normal, and their spread is largest near a probability of 0.5 and smallest near 0 and 1, so the spread is not equal.

## What is needed

A curve that stays between 0 and 1, flattens near both ends, and is steepest in the middle. That is the shape of the **logistic curve**, and the method that fits it is **logistic regression**.

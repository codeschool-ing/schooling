A coefficient in logistic regression is a change in log-odds, which nobody thinks in. Turned back into odds, it becomes something people can read.

## Adding a second predictor

With whether it was the customer's first order as a second predictor:

| | coefficient | standard error | odds ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| intercept | −8.736 | 0.848 | |
| minutes | 0.1489 | 0.0150 | **1.161** per minute |
| first order | 0.886 | 0.337 | **2.43** |

## From coefficient to odds ratio

Raising *e* to the power of a coefficient gives an **odds ratio**: how many times the odds are multiplied by a one-unit increase in that predictor, with the others held fixed.

```localised
=EXP(0.8864)       2.42637894526683
=EXP(10*0.1489)    4.43266064129309
```

**First order: 2.43.** At the same delivery time, a first-time customer's odds of complaining are about two and a half times a returning customer's. At 45 minutes, the odds are 0.131 for a returning customer and 0.317 for a new one, and 0.317 ÷ 0.131 = 2.43. Its 95% interval runs from **1.25 to 4.70**, and the test of whether it is 1 gives z = 2.63, p = 0.009.

**Minutes: 1.161 per minute**, so each extra minute multiplies the odds by 1.161. Over ten minutes the factor compounds: e^(10 × 0.1489) = **4.43**. Ten minutes slower, more than four times the odds.

## Odds ratios are not risk ratios

It is tempting to read "2.43 times the odds" as "2.43 times as likely". At 45 minutes, the **probability** of a complaint is 0.116 for a returning customer and 0.241 for a new one, a ratio of 2.08, not 2.43. When probabilities are small, odds and probabilities are close and the two ratios nearly agree. When probabilities are large, they part: at 60 minutes, where complaints are common, the probabilities are 0.55 and 0.75, a ratio of only 1.36, for the same odds ratio of 2.43.

That is why the two curves are not simply one above the other by a fixed amount. On the log-odds scale a first order adds the same 0.886 everywhere. On the probability scale the gap is small for quick deliveries, where almost nobody complains, largest in the middle, and small again for very slow ones, where almost everybody does.

## The same cautions as before

Everything lessons 18 to 20 said still applies. An odds ratio adjusted for delivery time is not adjusted for anything that was not measured. A first order may go with complaints because new customers have different expectations, or because first orders go disproportionately to areas with worse service. The coefficient cannot say which.

Two things move a correlation without changing the relationship underneath: points far from the rest, and the range of the data.

The correlation jumps to **0.96**. A line through the eleven points runs from the cluster to the lone point, and every product of deviations is dominated by that one point. This is Anscombe's set IV again.

Spearman's coefficient for the eleven is **0.14**. To ranks, the lone point is just the highest value on each axis, and one point at the top of both rankings cannot outweigh ten that show nothing.

## A single point can destroy one

The opposite also happens. Horta's twelve orders have r = 0.996 between items and baskets. Add one order with a single item and a basket of R$ 400, a gift box, and the correlation falls to **0.22**. Spearman's falls to **0.60**, still showing that the other twelve agree.

In both directions the lesson is the same as lesson 9's: find out what the point is before deciding anything. An error should be corrected. A real but different case, a gift box among grocery orders, may belong in a separate analysis. A real case of the same kind stays, and the honest report gives the correlation with and without it.

The correlation falls to **0.37**, and for Centro alone, to 0.18. Distance has much the same effect inside the narrow window: each kilometre still adds a little over two minutes. But the window is only three kilometres wide, so distance moves a delivery by about seven minutes from one end to the other, while rain, traffic and order size move it by as much again at random. The noise swamps the signal.

This **restriction of range** is everywhere. A company that hires only applicants with high test scores will find that, among its employees, test scores barely correlate with performance, and may conclude that the test is useless. It has looked only at the top of the range.

**A correlation describes the data in the range it was measured, and only there.**

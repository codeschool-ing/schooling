**Spearman's rank correlation**, written ρ ("rho") or r with a small s, is Pearson's *r* computed on the **ranks** of the values instead of the values themselves. It is named after Charles Spearman, who introduced it in 1904.

## How it works

Rank each variable separately, from 1 for the smallest. Ties share the average of the ranks they cover, as in lesson 16. Then compute Pearson's *r* on the two columns of ranks.

For the twelve orders, the two orders with 3 items share ranks 3 and 4, so each gets **3.5**:

```localised
=RANK.AVG(A3, A$2:A$13, 1)    3.5
=CORREL(C2:C13, D2:D13)       0.998250217382143
```

with the ranks of items in column C and the ranks of baskets in column D. Spearman's coefficient for items and baskets is **0.998**, nearly the same as Pearson's 0.996, because the relationship is close to a straight line.

## What ranks measure

Ranks throw away how far apart the values are and keep only their order. So Spearman's coefficient measures whether the relationship is **monotonic**: whether y tends to go up whenever x goes up, in a straight line or not.

Horta's weekly orders at different discounts climb fast and then flatten: every extra point of discount brings more orders, but fewer each time. The ranks agree perfectly, so Spearman's coefficient is exactly **1**. The points are not on a straight line, so Pearson's is **0.85**. Both are right; they answer different questions.

## When to reach for it

- **The relationship curves but does not turn back.** Spearman measures it fully; Pearson understates it.
- **The data is ordinal.** A 1-to-5 rating has an order but no reliable distances, lesson 2's point. Ranks use only the order.
- **A few values are extreme.** A rank cannot be further than "highest", so one wild value has limited pull, as the next section shows.

What Spearman cannot do is see a relationship that turns back on itself. For y = x² on −5 to 5, ρ is 0 as well.

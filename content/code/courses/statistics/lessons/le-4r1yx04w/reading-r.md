## The size

At ±1 every point lies exactly on a line. At 0 there is no straight-line trend at all. In between, the clouds in the figure are the best guide, because words like "strong" and "weak" mean different things in different fields: a physicist calibrating an instrument would worry about 0.95, while a psychologist would be glad of 0.3.

Two points to notice in the gallery. A correlation of **0.30 looks like almost nothing**: the trend is real, but individual points tell you little. And **−0.90 is as strong as 0.90**; the sign is only the direction.

## r squared

Squaring *r* gives a number with a direct reading. For distance and minutes in the 120 deliveries, r = 0.914 and **r² = 0.836**: about **84% of the variation** in delivery times goes along with variation in distance, and 16% does not. Lesson 19 meets the same number as the R² of a regression line.

The square also shows how fast correlations lose meaning as they shrink. An *r* of 0.5 shares 25% of the variation; an *r* of 0.2, just 4%.

## Is it more than chance?

Even two unrelated variables give a sample correlation that is not exactly zero. To test whether a correlation could be chance, the null hypothesis is that the population correlation is zero, and the test statistic is

**t = r × √(n − 2) ÷ √(1 − r²)**, with n − 2 degrees of freedom.

For the 120 deliveries, the number of items and the minutes have **r = 0.20**. That gives t = 2.17 with 118 degrees of freedom and a two-sided **p = 0.032**:

```localised
=T.DIST.2T(0.1955*SQRT(118)/SQRT(1-0.1955^2), 118)    0.0323650256754248
```

So more items do go with slightly longer deliveries, and that is probably not chance. But r² is 0.04: the number of items accounts for about **4%** of the variation in times. A correlation can be clearly non-zero and still tell you almost nothing about any one delivery, which is lesson 22's subject in another form.

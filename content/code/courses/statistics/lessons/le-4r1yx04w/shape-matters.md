Pearson's *r* measures one thing: how well a **straight line** describes the points. It says nothing about whether a straight line is the right description.

## Anscombe's quartet

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small data sets, eleven points each, built to make this point.

All four have the same mean of x, 9; the same mean of y, 7.50; the same standard deviation of y, 2.03; the same correlation, **0.816**; and the same least-squares line, y = 3.00 + 0.500x. Any summary table shows them as identical.

The pictures are not.

- **I** is what the numbers suggest: a loose straight-line trend.
- **II** is a perfect curve. A straight line is the wrong model, and *r* rates it as quite good.
- **III** is a perfect straight line with one stray point. Without that point *r* would be 1.
- **IV** has no relationship at all among ten of its points, which share the same x. The eleventh point alone creates the correlation.

**Always draw the scatter plot before you trust r.** That is the whole lesson of the quartet, and Anscombe wrote it to persuade people who believed that numbers were precise and pictures were rough.

## A perfect relationship with r = 0

A correlation of zero does not mean no relationship. Take x from −5 to 5 and y = x². Every y is determined exactly by its x, and *r* is **0**: the left half falls as much as the right half rises, and the products of deviations cancel.

The same shape turns up in practice. Customer satisfaction against delivery temperature might peak in the middle, with food too cold or too hot both disliked. A correlation coefficient would report "no relationship" for something that matters a great deal.

So "uncorrelated" means "no straight-line trend", nothing more.

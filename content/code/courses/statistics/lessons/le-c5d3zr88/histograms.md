A **histogram** groups numerical values into intervals of equal width, called **bins**, and draws a bar for each bin whose height is the number of values in it. Unlike a bar chart of categories, the bars touch: the bins are consecutive stretches of one number line, and nothing lies between them.

It is the basic picture of a numerical variable's shape, and it has one setting that can change what it seems to say: **the width of the bins**.

## Three widths, three stories

All three panels show the same 400 baskets.

- **Bins of R$ 5** give 96 narrow bins, most holding a handful of baskets. The bars jump up and down from one bin to the next, and the eye reads peaks and gaps that are only chance.
- **Bins of R$ 20** give 24 bins. One clear peak between R$ 40 and R$ 80, and a long tail running right. This is the picture lesson 4 used.
- **Bins of R$ 80** give 6 bins. Nearly everything falls in the first two, and the tail and the peak have both disappeared into two tall bars.

None of the three is false, and only the middle one shows the shape. Too narrow, and the picture is noise; too wide, and it is a summary so coarse that it hides what it summarises.

## Choosing a width

There are rules of thumb. The oldest, Sturges's rule, suggests about 1 + log₂(*n*) bins, which for 400 values is about 10. Others take the spread of the data into account. Every spreadsheet and chart tool picks a width for you, using some such rule, and its choice is a starting point.

The practical advice is simpler: **try a few widths and keep the one that shows a shape that survives small changes**. If a peak appears at one width and vanishes at the next, it was probably never there. If it stays across three widths, it is a property of the data.

Two smaller rules make a histogram honest:

- **Use round bin edges** — R$ 0, 20, 40 — so a reader can say which bin a value falls in.
- **Start the vertical axis at zero.** The bar's height is a count, and a cut-off axis makes small differences between bars look large.

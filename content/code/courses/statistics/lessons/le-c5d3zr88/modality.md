## Two peaks are two groups

There is a peak at lunchtime, around 12:00, and a larger one at dinner, around 19:30. Between 15:00 and 17:00 there are two orders out of 500. A distribution with two peaks is **bimodal**, and it is almost always a sign of **two groups mixed together**: here, people ordering for lunch and people ordering for dinner.

## What the usual summaries say

The mean time of an order is about 16:06, and the median about 17:57. Both are honest arithmetic and both describe almost nobody: the mean falls in the empty afternoon, and the median falls where orders are only beginning to pick up again. A courier rota built around "orders peak at around four o'clock" would put drivers on the road when there is nothing to deliver.

The boxplot is no better. Its box runs from about 12:11 to 19:44, with the median line inside, and it looks like any unremarkable distribution. **A boxplot cannot show two peaks.** That is the limitation lesson 6 promised to show.

The excess kurtosis of these times is −1.69, strongly negative: two humps far from the mean leave no room for a sharp centre or long tails. A number like that is a hint to draw the histogram, and the histogram is what shows the two groups.

## What to do with two peaks

**Split the data.** Describe the lunch orders and the dinner orders separately: each has its own centre and spread, and each summary describes something real. A single mean of two groups is the average of a lunch and a dinner, which nobody eats.

**Look for the grouping variable.** Two peaks invite the question "what separates them?" Here it is the meal. In other data it might be two kinds of customer, two machines, two shifts or two versions of a product. Finding it is usually more valuable than any summary of the mixture.

And when the peaks are less obvious than these, try several bin widths, as the first section of this lesson advised: a real second peak survives the change, and an accidental one does not.

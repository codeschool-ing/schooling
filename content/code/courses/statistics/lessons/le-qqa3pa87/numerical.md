A **numerical variable** records a quantity: something measured or counted, where the number is the
amount. Horta's *basket* is one, in reais. So are *minutes*, the time from the order to the doorbell,
and *items*, the number of things in the bag.

The test is the opposite of the categorical one. The value answers *how much?* or *how many?*, and
**the arithmetic on it means something**. A basket of R$ 86.40 holds R$ 31.90 more than a basket of
R$ 54.50, and that difference is money a person paid.

## A numerical variable has a place on a line

Because the values are amounts, you can put them on a number line, and the gaps between them are
real distances.

That picture already says things no single number says. Most deliveries took between half an hour
and three quarters of an hour. Two took longer, at 52.5 and 61 minutes, and both went to Barão
Geraldo, the neighbourhood farthest from Horta's warehouse. A list of twelve numbers hides that; a
line shows it at a glance.

## What you can do with quantities

Everything you could do with a category, and a great deal more.

- **Add them.** The twelve baskets total R$ 950.05.
- **Average them.** Lesson 3 does this properly, and lesson 4 shows when an average misleads.
- **Measure how spread out they are.** Two couriers with the same average delivery time can be very
  different to depend on; lesson 5 measures that.
- **Compare them by subtraction and by ratio.** 61 minutes is 33.5 minutes more than 27.5, and a
  little over twice as long.

The last item hides a subtlety that lesson 2 opens up. For some quantities a ratio means something
and for others it does not. Twice as long is a fair thing to say about a delivery time. Twice as
hot is not a fair thing to say about 30 °C against 15 °C.

## Counts are numerical too

*items* is a count: 1, 2, 3, never 2.5. It is still a numerical variable. The values are amounts,
the gaps are real, and the mean number of items per order, 6.25, is a useful figure even though no
single order had 6.25 items in it. Lesson 2 calls this kind **discrete** and gives it its own name
because some methods treat it differently.

What makes a variable numerical is not that it is written in digits. The next section is about the
columns that are written in digits and are nothing of the kind.

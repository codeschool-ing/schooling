## The first question: what kind of outcome?

**Numerical**, such as a delivery time or a basket: the tests compare **means**, or, in their rank-based versions, the typical values.

**Categorical**, such as bought or did not buy, or paid by pix, card or cash: the data is a table of **counts**, and the test compares the counts with what no association would produce.

## The second question: how many groups, and how are they related?

- **One group against a fixed value**: the one-sample t test of lesson 13. Is the filling machine on its 1000 g target?
- **Two groups made of different units**: the two-sample t test, in Welch's version. Centro's deliveries against Cambuí's.
- **Two measurements on the same units**: the paired t test. The same ten couriers, before and after a training course.
- **Three or more groups**: the analysis of variance, ANOVA. The four neighbourhoods at once.

## And the rank-based alternatives

Each test of means has a cousin that uses **ranks** instead of the values: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired data, the Mann-Whitney test for two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test for several. They are less affected by outliers and skew, and they matter most when samples are small. The section on non-parametric tests explains when to reach for them.

## The table in words

| question | outcome | groups | test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is the machine on 1000 g? | numerical | one, against a value | one-sample t |
| Centro faster than Cambuí? | numerical | two separate | Welch's t |
| Couriers faster after training? | numerical | two, same couriers | paired t |
| Do the four neighbourhoods differ? | numerical | four | ANOVA |
| Does the new page sell more? | categorical | two | chi-square |

A popular alternative flags any value whose **z-score is beyond ±3**: more than three standard deviations from the mean. On normal data, that flags about 0.3% of values, roughly one in 370.

It is intuitive, it uses numbers everybody already computes, and it has a flaw that matters most exactly when the data needs checking.

## The outlier inflates its own yardstick

The z-score measures distance in standard deviations, and the standard deviation is computed from all the values, outliers included. An outlier makes the standard deviation larger, which makes every z-score, including its own, smaller.

Take Horta's twelve baskets with the R$ 2,126.00 typo. The mean becomes R$ 238.62 and the standard deviation R$ 595.85. The typo's z-score is (2126.00 − 238.62) ÷ 595.85 = **3.17**. Flagged, but only just.

There is a reason it is only just. In a sample of *n* values, **no z-score can exceed (*n* − 1) ÷ √*n***, however extreme the value. For 12 values that ceiling is 3.18, so the typo is almost as far out as any value in twelve could ever be, and still barely clears 3. For **10 values the ceiling is 2.85**: a z > 3 rule cannot flag anything in ten values, ever, whatever is in them.

## Masking: two errors hide each other

Now suppose a second typo: R$ 154.75 entered as R$ 1,547.50. With both errors in the twelve baskets, the mean is R$ 354.68 and the standard deviation R$ 703.88.

| value | z-score |
|---|---|
| R$ 2,126.00 | 2.52 |
| R$ 1,547.50 | 1.69 |

**Neither is flagged.** Each error inflates the standard deviation enough to hide the other. This is called **masking**, and it gets worse the more outliers there are: a z-score rule finds a lone outlier, and misses a cluster of them.

## When the z-score rule is fine

On large samples from roughly normal data with at most a stray outlier or two, the z-score rule works well, and it is perfectly reasonable for something like the weights of bags from a filling machine. The trouble comes with small samples, skewed data and multiple outliers, which between them describe most of the data where outliers matter. For those, the rule needs a robust centre and a robust spread, and that is the next section.

Here is the most common misreading in all of statistics. Horta's interval runs from R$ 57.64 to R$ 107.13, and somebody says: "there is a 95% chance that the true mean is between R$ 57.64 and R$ 107.13".

It sounds right and, in the standard framework this course uses, it is not. The true mean is a fixed number, R$ 82.78. It is either in that interval or it is not; for this interval, it is. Nothing about it is random.

What is random is the **interval**. A different sample gives a different mean, a different standard error and a different interval.

Nineteen of the twenty intervals contain the true mean, and one misses it. Over 1,000 samples, the intervals contained the true mean **94.3%** of the time: close to 95%, and a little short of it, because for data as skewed as the baskets a sample of 40 is not quite large enough for the normal approximation to be exact.

## The correct reading

The 95% describes the **method**, not any one interval:

> If you repeated the sampling many times and built an interval the same way each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true mean.

For the one interval you have, the honest phrasing is "we are 95% confident that the true mean is between R$ 57.64 and R$ 107.13", where "confident" means exactly the sentence above. It is a statement about how often the procedure works, applied to one result of it.

## Why the distinction is not pedantry

The misreading leads to wrong conclusions in practice. Two intervals that overlap are sometimes read as "the means are probably the same", and two that do not overlap as "definitely different". Neither follows. The interval is a range of values the data cannot rule out at a given confidence, nothing more, and lessons 13 and 14 make that precise with hypothesis tests and p-values.

A practical reading that is safe: **the interval lists the values of the true mean that are consistent with the data**. Values inside are plausible; values well outside are hard to reconcile with what was observed.

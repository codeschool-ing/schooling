The bars pile up between R$ 40 and R$ 80 and then thin out slowly to the right. The smallest basket is R$ 14.18 and the largest R$ 421.78. Nothing can be below zero, and a household doing a big monthly shop can go well above R$ 200. Eighteen baskets did.

## Where the two centres land

The **median basket is R$ 66.74**. The **mean is R$ 82.78**, about R$ 16 higher.

No single basket is responsible. The mean has been pulled right by the whole tail: dozens of large baskets, each adding a little. As a result, 259 of the 400 baskets, 64.75%, are below the mean. A customer told "the average basket is R$ 83" would be told a number that roughly two out of three orders never reach.

## Reading the gap

The order of the mean and the median is a quick diagnosis of shape.

- **Mean well above the median**: a tail of large values on the right. Money, waiting times, sizes.
- **Mean well below the median**: a tail of small values on the left. Exam scores where most people did well and a few did badly; the age at which people retire.
- **Mean close to the median**: no strong tail either way, often a roughly symmetric pile.

Lesson 7 gives these shapes their names, right-skewed and left-skewed, and a number that measures them. For now the rule of thumb is enough: when the mean and the median disagree by a lot, the data has a tail, and the mean is describing the tail as much as the bulk.

## And the median is not the hero of every story

The median told the truth about a typical basket. It says nothing about the tail at all. The 40 largest baskets, a tenth of the orders, brought in 26% of the month's money. A manager who only ever looked at the median would never see that the largest tenth of the orders brings in a quarter of the revenue. Lesson 6 gives a way to see the whole spread in one picture.

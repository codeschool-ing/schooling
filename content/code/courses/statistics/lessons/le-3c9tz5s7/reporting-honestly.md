The word "average" does not say which centre it means. In everyday speech and in plenty of reports it means the mean, but not always, and the reader cannot tell. So the first rule of reporting a centre is to **name the summary you used**.

## Four habits

**Say which one.** "Median delivery time 37 minutes" cannot be misread. "Average delivery time 37 minutes" can.

**Report both when they differ.** When the mean and the median disagree by much, give both. "Median basket R$ 66.74, mean R$ 82.78" tells a reader that there is a tail of large baskets, without a word about skew.

**Give the count.** A mean of twelve values and a mean of 400 values deserve different confidence. Lesson 12 makes that difference precise; until then, the count lets a reader judge for themselves.

**Show the shape when it matters.** A histogram like the one of the 400 baskets answers questions the reader has not thought of yet. A single number answers only the question you chose.

## Spotting the choice in someone else's report

When you read a report, ask which centre a sentence would have used if its author wanted the number to look as large as possible, and which if they wanted it small.

A company describing its pay to recruits has a reason to quote the mean, which includes the founder. A union describing the same pay has a reason to quote the median. Both numbers are correct. **When a report quotes only one, ask what the other would have said**, and whether the choice of summary was made before or after the numbers were seen.

## The lesson in one line

The mean is the right answer to questions about totals and the wrong answer to questions about typical cases, and on data with a long tail the two answers are far apart. Knowing which question is being asked is most of the skill; the arithmetic is the easy part.

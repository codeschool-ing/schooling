title: When the mean is the number you need
version: 1
---

After three sections of the mean misbehaving, here is the case for it. **Whenever the question is about a total, the mean is the only summary that answers it.**

## The payroll, again

Horta's finance person is not asking what a typical employee earns. They are asking how much money must be in the account on payday. The mean answers that exactly: R$ 5,338.89 × 9 = R$ 48,050, the whole payroll.

The median cannot. R$ 2,400 × 9 = R$ 21,600, less than half of what is needed. A budget built on the median salary would bounce on the first payday. The founder's salary, the value the median so sensibly ignored, is R$ 28,000 that has to be paid.

## Revenue, costs, capacity

The same holds for every question that ends in a sum.

- **Expected revenue**: next month's orders times the mean basket. With 400 orders at a mean of R$ 82.78, about R$ 33,100. The median basket would predict R$ 26,700 and miss the money in the tail.
- **Courier hours**: deliveries times the mean delivery time, because the slow deliveries take real time.
- **Stock**: the mean daily demand times the days until the next delivery.

In each case the extreme values are not noise. They are part of the total, and leaving them out produces a number that is smaller than reality in the direction that hurts.

## Two questions, two numbers

This is why the same data can honestly produce two different "averages" in two different meetings.

| the question | the summary |
|---|---|
| What does a typical employee earn? | median, R$ 2,400 |
| What does the payroll cost? | mean × 9, R$ 48,050 |
| What does a typical order look like? | median, R$ 66.74 |
| What will 400 orders bring in? | mean × 400, R$ 33,111 |

Neither number is the true average. Each is the honest answer to its own question, and the mistake is answering one question with the other's number.

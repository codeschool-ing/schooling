The **normal distribution** is the bell curve: symmetric, with one peak at its mean, and tails that thin out quickly on both sides. It is described completely by two numbers, its **mean** μ (the Greek letter mu) and its **standard deviation** σ (sigma).

It fits data produced by **many small, independent influences adding up**. The weight of a bag of rice from a filling machine is the target plus a little too much here, a little too little there: grain size, a vibration, the moment the valve closed. Measurement errors, heights within one population and the sum of many dice behave the same way. Lesson 11 explains why the normal shape turns up whenever many small effects are added, and why that makes it central to everything that follows.

## The 68–95–99.7 rule

Every normal curve has the same proportions, measured in standard deviations from the mean.

- About **68%** of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean.
- About **95%** lie within 2.
- About **99.7%** lie within 3.

For bags with a mean of 1003 g and a standard deviation of 6 g: 68% weigh between 997 and 1009 g, 95% between 991 and 1015 g, and almost all between 985 and 1021 g. A bag outside that last band would be a 3-in-1000 event, and worth looking into.

The exact figures are 68.27%, 95.45% and 99.73%. Lesson 12 uses a close relative: 95% exactly lies within **1.96** standard deviations, which is where that number in every confidence interval comes from.

## Real data is never exactly normal

The 200 bags from Horta's supplier have a sample mean of 1002.61 g and a standard deviation of 6.09 g, and 71% of them lie within one standard deviation of the mean, against the model's 68%. Close, as lesson 7's skewness of −0.05 and kurtosis of 0.48 suggested.

No real data is exactly normal: weights cannot be negative, and the normal curve's tails go on forever. The question is never "is it normal?" but "**is it close enough for the question I am asking?**". For the middle of the distribution the answer is usually yes when the histogram is roughly symmetric with one peak. For the far tails — the one-in-a-million event — the normal model is often too optimistic, as lesson 7's heavy tails showed.

## What is not normal

Baskets, waiting times, incomes and anything else with a long tail are not normal, and treating them as normal gives nonsense: a normal model of Horta's baskets, with mean R$ 82.78 and standard deviation R$ 58.89, would predict that 8% of baskets cost less than nothing. A model that predicts impossible values is telling you it does not fit.

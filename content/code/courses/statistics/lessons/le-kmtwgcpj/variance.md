To use every value, start from each value's distance from the mean, its **deviation**.

For Davi, whose mean is 35, the deviations are 22 − 35 = −13, then −9, −4, 0, 3, 5, 9 and 9. The obvious next step is to average them, and it fails: **the deviations from the mean always add up to zero**, as lesson 3 showed. The fast deliveries cancel the slow ones exactly.

Something has to stop the signs cancelling. The standard choice is to **square** each deviation, which makes every one of them positive.

## Davi's variance by hand

| time | deviation | squared |
|---|---|---|
| 22 | −13 | 169 |
| 26 | −9 | 81 |
| 31 | −4 | 16 |
| 35 | 0 | 0 |
| 38 | 3 | 9 |
| 40 | 5 | 25 |
| 44 | 9 | 81 |
| 44 | 9 | 81 |
| | **sum** | **462** |

The sum of the squared deviations, 462, is called the **sum of squares**. The variance is that sum divided by the number of values minus one:

```localised
s² = 462 ÷ (8 − 1) = 66
```

Davi's variance is **66**. The same table for Lia gives a sum of squares of 8, and a variance of 8 ÷ 7 = **1.14**.

The division by 7 rather than 8 is not a misprint. It is the **sample variance**, the version used when the data is a sample of something larger — here, eight of a courier's many deliveries. The section after next explains why.

## Why squares, and not just distances?

Ignoring the signs and averaging the plain distances also works, and it has a name, the **mean absolute deviation**. For Davi it is 6.5 minutes. It is easier to explain and less sensitive to extreme values.

Squares won for mathematical reasons: they make the algebra work in later results, the central limit theorem of lesson 11 and the regression of lesson 19 among them. Squaring has a side effect worth knowing. **A far value counts disproportionately**: Davi's −13 contributes 169 to the sum, more than his −4, 3 and 5 put together.

## The trouble with the units

Davi's times are in minutes, so his squared deviations are in **minutes squared**, and so is his variance. "A variance of 66 square minutes" describes nothing anybody can picture. The variance is the right quantity for the mathematics and the wrong one for a report. The next section fixes that with one square root.

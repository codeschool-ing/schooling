The **p-value** is the probability, **if the null hypothesis were true**, of getting a result **at least as extreme** as the one observed.

Each piece of that sentence matters, so here they are one at a time.

- **If the null hypothesis were true**: the p-value is computed inside the world the null describes. It does not ask whether that world is the real one.
- **At least as extreme**: not the probability of exactly this result, which is tiny for any result, but of this one or anything further from what the null predicts.
- **As the one observed**: the data you have, summarised in the test statistic.

It is **0.165**. If the routing system changed nothing, about **one sample in six** would look at least this favourable to the supplier. That is not surprising, and the test of lesson 13 did not reject the null.

## The filling machine

The 15 bags gave t = 4.21 with 14 degrees of freedom, and the test was two-sided, so "at least as extreme" means t of 4.21 or more **or** −4.21 or less. The p-value is the area in both tails beyond 4.21: **0.0009**. If the machine were truly centred on 1000 g, fewer than one sample in a thousand would sit this far from target. That is very surprising, and the null was rejected.

## In a spreadsheet

With the t statistic in cell D1:

```localised
=T.DIST(D1, 24, TRUE)     0.164699734920054
=T.DIST.2T(ABS(D1), 24)   0.329399469840108
```

`T.DIST` with `TRUE` gives the area to the left of the statistic, which is the one-sided p-value when the alternative points left. `T.DIST.2T` gives the area in both tails beyond the statistic's size: the two-sided p-value, here 0.329 for the routing test. A two-sided p-value is twice the one-sided one whenever the distribution is symmetric, as t is.

A mobile phone network hands a call from one tower to the next: the network measures, the network
decides, the phone obeys. **Wi-Fi does not work that way. The client decides when to leave an access
point and which one to join next**, and the access points can at most make suggestions. As in lessons 6
to 8, there is no radio in this lab, so what follows is drawn from the standards and from arithmetic, not
captured.

Each client runs its own roaming algorithm, written by whoever wrote its operating system or its driver.
Most follow the same outline: stay on the current AP until its signal falls below a threshold, then scan
for a better one, then move if the difference is worth it. The threshold is not in the standard. **Many
clients sit somewhere around −70 to −75 dBm, some much lower**, and the same laptop can behave
differently after a driver update.

## The sticky client

A client that waits too long is called **sticky**. Somebody opens a laptop at a desk beside AP A, walks to
a meeting room beside AP B, and the laptop is still on A:

From the crossover at 20 metres onwards, B is the better choice, and the laptop does not know or care.
It stays on A until A falls below its own threshold, drawn here at −80 dBm. In that band it is talking to a
distant AP at a low data rate, with more retransmissions, and the user sees a slow network while
standing under an access point.

**It is not only that user's problem.** A slow client needs more airtime to move the same data, and a
channel carries one transmission at a time, so every other client on AP A waits longer. The section on
capacity puts a number on that.

## What the network can do

The network cannot move a client, but it can shape the cells so that the client's own rule gives a good
answer:

- Overlap the cells so that, wherever one AP falls to the threshold, another is already strong. Design
  guides commonly ask for the next AP to be heard at a usable level at every cell edge, with the cells
  overlapping by something like 15 to 20 per cent. That is a rule of thumb, checked by the survey of lesson
  10, not a figure from the standard.
- Switch off the lowest data rates. A client is only associated where it can still read the AP, and at
  2.4 GHz the 1, 2, 5.5 and 11 Mbit/s rates reach a long way. Removing them shrinks the cell's edge, so the
  client's threshold arrives sooner. Very old devices that only speak those rates lose the network.
- A minimum signal level at which the AP drops a client. Some vendors offer it. It is a blunt tool: it
  also disconnects clients that had no better AP to go to.

**And make the move itself cheap**, because a client that knows roaming is slow has a reason to put it
off. That is the next section.

Three access points can be managed by logging in to each. Three hundred cannot, and the difference is
not only effort: fast roaming needs APs that share keys, and a channel plan (lesson 7) needs somebody who
sees every AP at once. **The architectures differ in where those shared decisions are made**, and so in
what stops when that place is unreachable.

## Autonomous

An autonomous AP, also called standalone, holds its own configuration and makes its own decisions:
channel, power, who may join. It works with nothing else on the network, which is its strength. **Each
change is made once per AP**, the channel plan is only as coherent as whoever set it by hand, and keys are
not shared, so fast roaming between two autonomous APs is limited to whatever their vendor added.

## Controller-based

A controller, a WLAN controller or WLC, holds the configuration for every AP, runs the radio resource
management that picks channels and power for all of them, and holds the keys for roaming. The APs are
sometimes called lightweight: they boot, find their controller and ask it what to be.

The standard protocol between them is **CAPWAP** (RFC 5415). It has two channels, both over UDP:

| channel | port | carries | protected by |
|---|---|---|---|
| control | 5246 | configuration, statistics, client events | DTLS, required |
| data | 5247 | users' traffic, when it is tunnelled | DTLS, optional |

The split of work is called **split MAC**. What has to happen within microseconds, sending beacons,
acknowledging frames, retransmitting, stays in the AP; what can wait a few milliseconds, association,
authentication, the channel plan, goes to the controller.

Users' traffic takes one of two paths. **Tunnelled**, it travels inside CAPWAP to the controller and joins
the wired network there, so the VLANs and the firewall rules live in one place. The cost is the detour and
a tunnel's overhead, which brings back the MTU arithmetic of lesson 1. **Switched locally**, it leaves the
AP straight onto the access switch, and only management goes to the controller.

**A controller is a single point of failure until it is paired**, and lesson 14 is about exactly that
shape. When it is unreachable, tunnelled traffic stops. APs switching locally keep forwarding for the
clients they already have in some vendors' designs, and stop in others; the vendor's documentation says
which, and a test says it better.

## Cloud-managed

The controller's management role moves to the vendor's cloud, reached over HTTPS from each AP, and the
users' data stays on site. There is no box to buy, run or pair, and a site with two APs is managed from the
same screen as a site with two hundred.

The trade is a dependency on the internet link and on the vendor. **When the link is down, the APs
generally keep serving clients with their last configuration, and nothing can be changed.** Anything
that lives in the cloud, a hosted captive portal or an authentication proxy, stops with it, and the
licence is a subscription: when it lapses, so does the management.

| | autonomous | controller-based | cloud-managed |
|---|---|---|---|
| configuration lives | in each AP | in the controller | in the vendor's cloud |
| channels and power set by | a person, per AP | the controller | the cloud service |
| roaming keys shared | no | yes | yes, between the APs |
| when the manager is lost | nothing, there is none | tunnelled traffic stops | no changes; cloud features stop |

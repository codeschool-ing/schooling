---
title: A packet inside a packet
version: 1
---

Most people picture a VPN as a pipe: a private cable laid through the internet, which nobody else can
see into. **There is no pipe.** Every packet of a tunnel crosses the same routers as everybody else's,
one at a time, and each router reads its header and forwards it like any other.

What makes it a tunnel is **encapsulation**. The packet that cannot be sent as it is gets wrapped whole,
headers and all, as the payload of a new packet that can. The new packet carries its own IP header with
addresses the internet knows how to reach. At the far end a router takes that outer header off and
forwards what was inside, as if it had arrived on a local cable.

Two things follow from the picture, and the rest of this lesson measures both.

**The inner packet is not changed.** Its source is still `192.168.10.20` and its destination is still
`192.168.20.30`, addresses that mean nothing on the internet. Nothing in the network between the two
offices ever acts on them, because nothing in the network ever looks past the outer header.

**The wrapping costs bytes.** An outer IPv4 header is 20 bytes, and GRE adds 4 more, or 8 with a key. A
link that carries 1500-byte packets can therefore carry inner packets of only 1480 or 1472 bytes, and
the section on MTU shows what happens when nobody tells the sender.

This is also the whole of what a tunnel is. IP-in-IP and GRE, the two in this lesson, add nothing else.
The VPNs of lessons 2 to 4 wrap the same way and add encryption and authentication around the payload,
so the header they put in front grows, and the idea stays exactly the one drawn above.

title: Reading a heat map
version: 1
---

A heat map is a floor plan coloured by one value: at every spot, the signal a survey measured or a model
predicted, with the gaps between measured spots filled in by interpolation. It is the picture every survey
report leads with. The one below is computed rather than surveyed, from a simple indoor model, so the shape
of its problems is realistic and its numbers are not measurements:

## What the colour does not say

The belief to replace: **a map that is green everywhere means the Wi-Fi is good.** The default view shows
the **strongest AP at each spot**, and several things that decide how the network behaves are invisible in
it.

| view | what it shows | what goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| primary signal | the strongest AP at each spot | this is the map everybody looks at |
| secondary signal | the second-strongest AP | a voice call has nowhere to roam to |
| SNR | signal over noise | a green spot beside a microwave oven |
| channel overlap | how many APs on the same channel are heard | green everywhere, from APs that share one channel |
| data rate, throughput | what an active survey measured | a strong signal with nothing getting through |

The fourth row is the one that surprises people. Ten APs at full power make a map that is green from wall to
wall, and because they can hear each other on the few channels available, they take turns on the air, the
co-channel contention of lesson 7. **A heat map of too much signal looks exactly like a heat map of good
coverage.** The channel-overlap view is what tells them apart.

## Where the colour came from

Interpolation fills the space between measured points with a guess, and a smooth colour looks equally
certain everywhere. **A heat map should come with the path that was walked**, drawn on the same plan, so a
reader can tell a measured corner from one the software painted in. A room nobody entered is not covered,
whatever colour it has.

And a map is a moment. The one in the report was walked on a particular day, in a particular state of the
building: empty or full, shelves stocked or bare, doors open or shut. People absorb signal, 3 to 5 dB each
in the way, and a warehouse's racks do not hold the same stock in March as in December.

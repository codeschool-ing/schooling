A signal weakens on its way from the AP to the client for two reasons: it spreads out, and things in the
way absorb it. The first has a formula, **free-space path loss**. It is short enough to run:

Two patterns in the table are worth keeping. **Every doubling of distance adds 6.0 dB of loss**: 60.2 at
10 m, 66.2 at 20 m, 72.2 at 40 m on the 2.4 GHz channel. And **5 GHz starts 7.1 dB behind 2.4 GHz** at
every distance, 47.3 dB against 40.2 at one metre.

The belief to replace is that 5 GHz loses more because the air absorbs it more. Over tens of metres the air
absorbs almost nothing at these frequencies. The frequency term in the formula comes from the **receiving
antenna**: an antenna sized for a shorter wavelength collects energy over a smaller area, so it catches
less of the same wave. The walls, on the other hand, do absorb higher frequencies more, and indoors that
is the part that decides.

## Indoors, the walls decide

Free space is the best case. Indoor measurements are commonly fitted with the same shape and a steeper
slope: loss grows by 10 × *n* dB for every tenfold increase in distance, where *n* is 2 in free space and
**typically 3 to 4 inside an office**, so 9 to 12 dB per doubling instead of 6. Walls come on top, and
the losses below are the kind of values survey tools and vendors' design guides carry. They are typical,
not exact: the same word covers very different walls, and a single measurement of one of yours beats the
whole table.

| obstacle, typical loss in dB | 2.4 GHz | 5 GHz |
|---|---|---|
| plasterboard partition | 3 to 4 | 4 to 5 |
| wooden door | 3 to 4 | 4 to 6 |
| clear glass window | 2 to 3 | 3 to 5 |
| coated, low-emissivity glass | 10 to 30 or more | 10 to 30 or more |
| brick wall | 6 to 10 | 10 to 15 |
| concrete wall | 10 to 15 | 15 to 25 |
| lift shaft, metal cabinet | 20 to 30 or more | 25 to 30 or more |
| a person in the way | 3 to 5 | 3 to 6 |

**A 3 dB loss halves the power and a 10 dB loss divides it by ten**, the dBm arithmetic of lesson 7. A
concrete wall at 20 dB leaves one hundredth of the signal.

## A link budget

The last lines of `fspl.py` do what a designer does for one spot: start from what the AP radiates,
subtract the path and the walls, and compare with the target. **15 m and two plasterboard walls leave
−58.8 dBm, 8.2 dB above −67**, with an SNR of 33.2 dB over the assumed noise floor. The 20 dBm and the
−92 dBm are assumptions picked for the example. On a real site the survey replaces the free-space term with
a measurement, and a result that close to the target is a reason to measure rather than to trust the sum.

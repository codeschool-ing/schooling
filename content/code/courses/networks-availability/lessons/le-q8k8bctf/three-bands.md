title: Three bands, reach against room

A common belief is that **5 GHz is faster than 2.4 GHz**. The band is not faster. An 802.11ax link 20
MHz wide, with one stream and the same modulation, runs at exactly the same 143.4 Mbit/s in either
band, because nothing in the rate arithmetic depends on the frequency. What differs is how far the
signal goes and how much room there is to use, and the two pull in opposite directions.

**A lower frequency reaches further; a higher one has far more room.** At the same transmit power, a
5 GHz signal arrives weaker than a 2.4 GHz one over the same distance, by about 6 dB in open air, and
walls and bodies take more from it again. Lesson 10 puts numbers on the loss. In exchange, the higher
bands are wide enough to hold many channels, and wide ones. Drawn to one scale, the difference is hard
to miss:

| | 2.4 GHz | 5 GHz | 6 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| spectrum | 2400 to 2483.5 MHz | about 500 MHz between 5150 and 5850, in pieces | 5925 to 7125 MHz where all of it is open |
| 20 MHz channels that do not overlap | 3 | up to 25, by country | 59 |
| who else is there | every Wi-Fi device since 1999, Bluetooth, microwave ovens | older Wi-Fi from 802.11a on, and weather and military radar in part of it | only Wi-Fi 6E and 7 |
| reach through walls | the best of the three | less | the least |

**How much of each band may be used is the regulator's decision, country by country.** The United States
opened all 1200 MHz of 6 GHz; the European Union opened the lower 480 MHz, 5945 to 6425. In Brazil the
rules are Anatel's. An access point is told which country it is in, and that setting, not the hardware,
decides which channels it will offer; lesson 7 goes through them.

2.4 GHz survives because of its reach and because everything speaks it. A thermostat, a printer and a
ten-year-old phone all have a 2.4 GHz radio, and many have nothing else. The price is crowding: three
usable channels, shared with every neighbour's network and with devices that are not Wi-Fi at all.

## Why 6 GHz is clean

Four things make the 6 GHz band quieter than the other two, and only the last of them will wear off
with time.

- Nothing old can join it. Only Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 devices have a 6 GHz radio, so there is no
  802.11b client to fall back for and no slow frame from 2003 occupying the air.
- It has room for wide channels: seven of 160 MHz, or three of 320 MHz.
- Indoors there is no radar to give way to. Low-power indoor access points need no radar detection
  there, unlike a large part of 5 GHz.
- It is new, so fewer neighbours use it today, and the shorter reach keeps next door's signal weaker
  through the wall.

**The limit is the first reason turned round.** A phone without a 6E radio does not see a 6 GHz network
at all, so a 6 GHz network is added beside the others and never replaces them.

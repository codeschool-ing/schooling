The last column the rate table multiplies by is the number of **spatial streams**. With several
antennas at each end, a transmitter can send different data from each antenna at the same time, on
the same channel. A receiver with as many antennas pulls the streams apart again, because each one
reaches it by slightly different paths. That is **MIMO**, multiple input and multiple output, and
it arrived with 802.11n. Two streams carry twice the data of one, in the same spectrum.

Radios are described as `4x4:4`: four transmit chains, four receive chains, four streams. The belief
to drop here is that **a four-stream router makes a two-stream phone twice as fast**. A link carries as
many streams as the smaller end supports, and phones and laptops are built with two, which is typical
of the devices sold today rather than a limit in the standard. A `4x4` access point talking to a `2x2`
phone runs two streams; the other two are idle for that client, or busy with somebody else.

## Several clients at once

Up to Wi-Fi 5, an access point sends to one client at a time. **MU-MIMO** (multi-user MIMO) lets it aim
different streams at different clients in the same transmission, shaping each so that it adds up at
one client and cancels at the others. 802.11ac allowed it downstream only, to up to four clients;
802.11ax allows it in both directions and to up to eight. It works when the clients stand apart and
stay still, because the access point has to measure the path to each one first, and a phone in a
walking hand changes its path before the measurement is used.

**OFDMA** solves a different problem, and it is the change that 802.11ax is best known for. Before it,
every transmission occupied the whole channel, even when it carried a 100-byte voice packet to one
phone, and each one paid the whole cost of winning the air and announcing itself. 802.11ax divides the
channel's subcarriers into **resource units** and gives different units to different clients in the
same transmission: up to nine clients in a 20 MHz channel, thirty-seven in 80 MHz.

**OFDMA raises no client's top speed.** One client alone gets the whole channel either way. What it
changes is the cost of many small things: voice, the acknowledgements TCP sends back, a sensor
reporting a temperature. A crowded room of phones is made of exactly those, which is why a Wi-Fi 6
network feels better in a lecture hall long before anybody measures a faster download.

Both MU-MIMO and OFDMA need the client to support them, as well as the access point. A Wi-Fi 5 laptop
on a Wi-Fi 6 network is served the old way, one at a time, whatever the access point can do.

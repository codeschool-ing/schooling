title: Thirteen numbers, three channels

An access point's menu offers channels 1 to 13 on 2.4 GHz, and it is natural to read that as thirteen
separate channels, so that ten networks in a building could each have one of their own. **There are
three.** The numbers are 5 MHz apart, and each signal is 20 MHz wide, 22 for the 802.11b of 1999. A
network on channel 3 spreads over channels 1 to 5, and one on channel 6 over 4 to 8.

A **channel number is only the address of a centre frequency**, worked out by a fixed formula in each
band. The program below does the arithmetic for the three bands and asks which channels a network on
channel 6 overlaps. It was run with `python3`, and its output is under it:

Every channel from 2 to 10 overlaps channel 6. **Channels 1, 6 and 11 are 25 MHz apart**, which is
more than the 22 each needs, and that is why they are the plan every guide gives: the only three that
fit side by side in the band, drawn here to scale.

Channel 13 exists in Brazil and Europe and not in the United States, where the band stops at 11, and
channel 14 was only ever for 802.11b in Japan. Some networks in Europe use four channels, 1, 5, 9 and
13, spaced exactly 20 MHz apart. That works only if no 802.11b device is left, and the four sit edge to
edge with nothing between them; **1, 6 and 11 remain the plan that works everywhere**, and the one a
visiting laptop from anywhere will find.

The plan decides what every access point in a building does, not only yours. Picking channel 3
because 1 and 6 look busy is the mistake this section exists for: the network on 3 now overlaps both,
and the next section shows why that is worse than sharing channel 1 outright. And the lab cannot
demonstrate any of it, because it has no radio: the frequencies above are arithmetic, not a
measurement.

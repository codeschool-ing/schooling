A KPI is not finished when somebody has named it. "Delivered on time" sounds like a definition and
is a heading over several possible ones, and each gives a different number. **The card is where the
choice is written down, before anybody computes anything**, so that the number on Monday means the
same thing in March as it did in January and the same thing to Caio as to Helena.

The card has eleven fields, and they fall into three groups. The **definition** is the name, the
objective it serves, the numerator, the denominator, what is excluded, and the unit. The
**plumbing** is the source: which system, which table, and when it is read. The **obligations**
are the owner, the frequency, the target and the action threshold, and they are what turn a
well-defined metric into somebody's job.

## Owner and frequency

**The owner is one person, never a department.** "Operations" cannot be asked on Monday why the
number fell; Caio can. The owner does not compute the number (Lívia does) and does not have to
cause every movement in it. The owner is the person who explains it and decides what to do.

**The frequency follows the decision, not the data.** The delivery records refresh every hour, but
the decisions the KPI feeds, such as changing a carrier or adding a van to a route, are made weekly,
so the card says weekly. A KPI read more often than anybody can act on it produces anxiety rather
than action, and the minute-by-minute view belongs to the people dispatching the vans, which is the
subject of lesson 14.

## The worked example: three honest answers in one week

Caio asked Lívia for "the on-time delivery rate". Below is one week of home deliveries from the
Contagem warehouse, 2 to 8 February 2026: fourteen orders, small parcels promised in two days and
furniture in seven or ten. *Took* is the number of days from order to delivery; the two cancelled
orders have none. Type it into a new sheet from A1:

| | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Order | Kind | Promised | Took | Status |
| 2 | 5101 | parcel | 2 | 2 | delivered |
| 3 | 5102 | furniture | 7 | 6 | delivered |
| 4 | 5103 | parcel | 2 | 1 | delivered |
| 5 | 5104 | parcel | 2 | 3 | delivered |
| 6 | 5105 | furniture | 10 | 8 | delivered |
| 7 | 5106 | parcel | 2 | 2 | delivered |
| 8 | 5107 | furniture | 7 | | cancelled on day 1 |
| 9 | 5108 | parcel | 2 | 2 | delivered |
| 10 | 5109 | furniture | 7 | 9 | delivered |
| 11 | 5110 | parcel | 2 | 1 | delivered |
| 12 | 5111 | furniture | 10 | | cancelled on day 12 |
| 13 | 5112 | furniture | 7 | 7 | delivered |
| 14 | 5113 | parcel | 2 | 2 | delivered |
| 15 | 5114 | furniture | 10 | 9 | delivered |

Two helper columns mark each order. In F1 type `On promise` and in G1 `In 2 days`, then in F2 and
G2:

```localised
=IF(D2="",0,IF(D2<=C2,1,0))      1
=IF(D2="",0,IF(D2<=2,1,0))       1
```

Copy both down to row 15. A cancelled order has no delivery, so both columns give it a 0. Now the
counts, in any empty cells:

```localised
=COUNT(C2:C15)      14
=COUNT(D2:D15)      12
=SUM(F2:F15)        10
=SUM(G2:G15)        6
```

Fourteen orders, twelve delivered, ten of them by the promised day, six within two days. Three
reasonable people now write three formulas.

**Caio's warehouse team** counts deliveries that kept their promise, out of deliveries made:

```localised
=ROUND(SUM(F2:F15)/COUNT(D2:D15)*100,1)      83.3
```

**Renata**, whose site says "fast delivery", counts deliveries made within two days:

```localised
=ROUND(SUM(G2:G15)/COUNT(D2:D15)*100,1)      50
```

**Otávio**, who sees refunds, counts on-promise deliveries out of every order placed:

```localised
=ROUND(SUM(F2:F15)/COUNT(C2:C15)*100,1)      71.4
```

**83.3%, 50% and 71.4%, from the same fourteen rows.** None of the formulas is wrong. Renata's
punishes furniture for a promise nobody made: no seven-day sofa can arrive in two days, so her
number mostly measures the share of parcels in the week. Caio's leaves out both cancelled orders,
including 5111, which was cancelled on day 12 of a ten-day promise: the customer waited past the
date and gave up. Otávio's counts 5107 as a failure, though that customer changed their mind the
day after ordering, before anything was late.

## What the card decides

The card for Caio's KPI takes a position on each of those, and writes it down. The numerator is
orders delivered by the promised day. **The denominator is the orders delivered plus the orders
cancelled after their promised day had passed**, because a customer who gives up waiting is the
worst late delivery there is. Orders cancelled before the promised day are excluded, since nothing
had gone wrong yet. This week that means twelve deliveries plus order 5111:

```localised
=ROUND(SUM(F2:F15)/(COUNT(D2:D15)+1)*100,1)      76.9
```

The `+1` is order 5111, typed by hand because this sheet has fourteen rows; in a real report it
would be a column of its own. **76.9% is the number Caio's KPI reports for the week**, and it is
the first number on the card that anybody could recompute from the records and get the same.

Renata's two-day figure does not disappear. It is a fair metric for the parcels, and she may want it
on her own page, labelled as what it is. What the card prevents is the scene from lesson 1: two
people walking into the same meeting with 83.3 and 50 under the same name.

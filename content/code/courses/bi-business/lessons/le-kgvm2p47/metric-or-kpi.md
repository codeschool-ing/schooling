The usual picture is that every number on a dashboard is a KPI, and that a company with more of
them is better managed. Varanda's systems can produce several hundred numbers without anybody
writing a line of code: items per receipt, page views per product, returns by colour, minutes on
hold at the call centre. **Every one of them is a metric. Very few of them are key performance
indicators**, and telling the two apart is the first thing this lesson does.

## Two definitions

A **metric** is any quantity measured with a written definition: what is counted, over what period,
from which records. "Items per receipt in February" is a metric. So is "page views of the hose-reel
product page". A metric is useful whenever somebody wants to understand something, and most of the
numbers in a diagnosis, like the October drop of lesson 7, are metrics nobody looks at the rest of
the year.

A **KPI** is a metric that somebody chose because it tracks an objective the company has set, and
that a named person is expected to act on when it moves. **The word that matters is not
"performance" but "key"**: key to an objective, and key to somebody's decisions. Take either away
and the number goes back to being a metric, which is no insult. It simply stops claiming a place on
the page people read every week.

So every KPI is a metric and few metrics are KPIs. At Varanda in early 2026 the list Helena Prado
agreed with her directors has four, one for each of the areas lesson 5 described:

| KPI | the objective it tracks | who acts on it |
|---|---|---|
| sales against plan, by month | reach the 2026 sales plan | Helena Prado, CEO |
| delivered on promise, by week | keep the date we give the customer | Caio Barreto, operations |
| online conversion, by week | make the online shop pay for its traffic | Renata Sá, marketing |
| staff turnover, by month | keep trained people in the stores | Sônia Freitas, HR |

## Three questions that sort them

Put any number through three questions. **Which objective does it serve? Who acts when it moves?
What would they do?** A KPI has an answer to all three, and the third is the one that weeds out most
candidates. "Page views went up 30%" passes the first question loosely (the online shop should grow)
and fails the other two: nobody at Varanda changes anything because page views moved, and lesson 12
has a name for numbers like that.

The questions also show that **"key" is relative to a person**. Online conversion is Renata's KPI:
she reviews it every Monday and changes campaigns, prices on the site and the product pages because
of it. To Helena it is a metric. She does not act on it directly; she opens it when sales against
plan disappoint and she wants to know whether the online shop is the reason. One number, a KPI on
one page and a metric one click below on another.

## Why the distinction pays

A list where everything is "key" has no key. When the Monday report carries forty numbers in the
same font, the four that should start a conversation are as easy to miss as the thirty-six that
should not, and lesson 11 is about keeping the list short and defending it.

The other reason is the rest of this lesson. **A KPI carries obligations a metric does not**: a
definition precise enough that two people compute the same value, an owner, a frequency, a target
and the point at which somebody must act. Writing those down costs an afternoon per KPI, which is
affordable for four and not for four hundred. The next section writes them down for Caio's, and
finds that "delivered on time" can honestly be three different numbers in the same week. When
several teams have to share one definition across a whole company, the tooling for it is the
subject of `analytics-bi` lesson 2; here it is one card and one spreadsheet.

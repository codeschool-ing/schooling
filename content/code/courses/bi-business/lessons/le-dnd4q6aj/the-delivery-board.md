When Marcos asked for "a dashboard of deliveries", Lívia's first draft had three charts: the on-time
rate by week, deliveries per day for the month, and a pie of deliveries by region. Marcos opened it
once. **Every chart on it was correct, and every one answered Caio's question rather than his.**
Caio, the operations director, wants to know whether deliveries are getting better this quarter.
Marcos wants to know which truck to ring at 11:00. The second draft started from that question and
looks like this.

## Five decisions behind the board

**The header carries the time of the data.** "Data from 11:00 · 2 min old" is the time the numbers
describe, not the time the page was opened. A screen opened at 11:05 that shows the 09:20 picture
looks exactly as current as one that is right, and the last section of this lesson is about the
morning that happened.

**Four numbers, and they are counts.** 286 orders for today, 91 delivered, 4 late, 57 at risk. Marcos
acts on orders and customers, so the tiles count them. A percentage on time would turn four customers
waiting at home into a decimal close to 100, which reads as good news. The two tiles that need action
are outlined in red, and they also say in words what they are, because a colour on its own is lost on
anybody who does not see it (`visualization` lesson 14).

**The exceptions come first, sorted by how far behind.** Three routes are 30 or more minutes behind
the plan, and each row says how many stops are left, how many windows are already missed and what to
do next. The top row is outlined because it is the one with customers already waiting.

**Everything else is shrunk, not removed.** The other eleven routes are small chips with their minutes
behind. They need no action, so they take little room; but they are all there, so Marcos can see that
the board knows about fourteen routes. A route whose driver's phone stopped sending would otherwise
vanish from the list of exceptions and look exactly like a route with no problem.

**There is no chart of months.** The trend of the on-time rate is a fine chart, and it belongs to the
monthly review in lesson 15. On this screen it would take a third of the space and change nothing
Marcos does before lunch.

## The definitions underneath

Each number on the board rests on a definition that Lívia wrote down with Marcos, on the card lesson 10
described, because "late" said out loud means three different things to three people.

| on the board | the definition |
|---|---|
| minutes behind | the time the route's last completed stop was delivered, minus the time the plan gave that stop |
| late | an order whose promised delivery window has closed without a delivery |
| at risk | an order not yet delivered on a route 30 or more minutes behind |
| left | stops on the route not yet delivered |

The 57 at risk are the stops still to make on the three routes in the list: 18 on route 6, 17 on
route 10 and 22 on route 11. They are not a forecast of how many will be late. They are the orders
whose fate depends on what Marcos does in the next hour, which is the only reason to show them.

## Where it lives, and who it is for

The board runs on a television on the warehouse wall and on Marcos's phone. That decides the design
as much as the data does: large type, few colours, nothing that needs a mouse to hover over, and a
list short enough to read from the loading bay. **A board for the floor is designed for a glance, not
for study.** If Marcos has to read it for more than a few seconds, it has already failed him, because
he is doing something else.

Any BI tool can draw a board like this — Power BI, Tableau, Looker Studio, Metabase — and so can a
spreadsheet with conditional formatting, which `excel-analytics` lesson 9 teaches, as long as
something refreshes it. The design questions behind it, from hierarchy to the path the eye takes,
are `visualization` lesson 18; the reader who has thirty seconds is `data-storytelling` lesson 7.
This lesson's point is narrower: **the question decided the layout**, and the question was Marcos's.

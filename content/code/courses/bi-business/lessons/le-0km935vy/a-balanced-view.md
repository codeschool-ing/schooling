Every number on Varanda's board page is money: sales, growth, sales per square metre, online share,
sales per employee. **A pack that is all money shows the results of past decisions and none of their
causes.** By the time sales per square metre stops growing, whatever made it stop happened a year or two
earlier, in the stores, the warehouse and the people, where the pack does not look.

## The balanced scorecard

Robert Kaplan and David Norton proposed the balanced scorecard in the early 1990s for exactly this
complaint: companies steered by financial measures alone were looking in the mirror. Their answer was
to look at the company from four perspectives, each with a few measures, and to read them as a chain in
which the lower ones drive the higher ones.

| perspective | its question | at Varanda, for example |
|---|---|---|
| financial | how do we look to the owners? | sales in real terms, sales per m², operating margin |
| customer | how do customers see us? | share of customers who buy again within a year; deliveries on the promised date (lesson 10) |
| internal process | what must we be excellent at? | stock-outs on the best-selling lines; days of stock; time from order to delivery |
| learning and growth | can we keep improving? | staff turnover in the stores; hours of training a year; share of stores using the new stock system |

Read from the bottom up, it is a hypothesis about how Varanda makes money. Trained, stable staff keep
the shelves stocked and the deliveries on time; customers who find what they want and get it when
promised come back; returning customers raise sales per square metre without new floor. **Each lower row
is a leading indicator of the one above it**, in the sense lesson 10 gave the words, and the financial row
is the lagging result of all three.

That is what the board page lacked. Its five rows say that sales per square metre stalled; the rows
below would say whether stock-outs rose, whether customers stopped coming back or whether store
turnover jumped, which is to say where to look and what to do. A board that sees only the top row can
only demand better results. A board that sees the chain can ask for a specific change.

## Using it without turning it into forty numbers

The risk is obvious: four perspectives with five measures each is a twenty-row page, and lesson 11 already
said what happens to a page of twenty numbers. Varanda's board added **one measure per perspective**, three
rows to its five, and each was chosen because a question on the board's table depends on it. Repeat
customers belongs there because the online decision turns on whether online customers come back.
Stock-outs belongs there because the warehouse decision turns on them.

The scorecard is also a hypothesis, and a hypothesis can be wrong. If training hours rise for two years and
turnover, stock-outs and repeat customers do not move, the chain that justified the training does not
hold at Varanda, and the board should know that rather than keep the row out of loyalty. Whether one row
really drives the next is a question for the methods of `statistics` lesson 18, and it cannot be settled
by drawing an arrow.

## The north star, and its limit

A newer idea, common in technology companies, is the **north star metric**: one number that captures the
value the company delivers to its customers, chosen so that if it grows, the business grows. For a music
service it might be hours listened; for Varanda's online shop, perhaps orders delivered complete and on
the promised date each month.

The idea is useful for what it forces: a company that has to choose one number has to say what it is
for. Its limit is the one lesson 11 called Goodhart's law. **A single number that everybody is pushed to
raise is the number most likely to be raised in ways that do not help**: orders delivered on time can rise
because the promised date was moved further out. A north star works as a focus for a team, paired with
the counter-metrics lesson 11 described. It does not replace a board page, and a board that steered by one
number would have the all-money problem in a smaller size.

## What the three levels add up to

Lesson 14's board was a list of exceptions for the next hour; lesson 15's review was a month against a plan,
with actions; this lesson's page is five years against a plan, with the world outside. **The same company,
three questions, three screens**, and none of them is a smaller or larger copy of another. That was the
promise of lesson 1, which said a dashboard is one way of delivering an answer and the question decides
the form. Lesson 17 takes the course's habits into an industry of its own, starting with a method for
reading any industry at all.

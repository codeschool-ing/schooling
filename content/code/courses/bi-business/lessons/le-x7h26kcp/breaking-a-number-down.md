A diagnosis starts where the descriptive report of lesson 6 stopped: October 2025 sold R$ 7,960
thousand, 0.7% below October 2024, and it was the only month of the year below its 2024 value.
Everybody at Varanda had an explanation ready. **The first job is not to choose one of them. It is to
find where in the number the change actually sits**, and that is done by breaking the total into
parts that add or multiply back to it.

## A total is made of parts

The wrong instinct is to go looking for causes straight away: the weather, a competitor, the
economy. Each of those would act on some part of the business and not on others, so before testing
any of them it pays to know which part moved. A total can be split in three ways that every retailer
has in its data:

- by channel: the nine stores, and the online shop;
- by place or product: each store, each category (furniture, garden, kitchen, lighting,
  decoration);
- into a count and an average: sales = number of orders × average ticket, where the average
  ticket is sales divided by orders.

The first two are additions. Varanda's October is the nine stores plus the online shop, and each
store is its five categories added up. The third is a multiplication, and it answers a different
question: whether fewer people bought, or the same people spent less. **Fewer orders points at
traffic, a campaign or stock; a lower ticket points at prices, discounts or the mix of products.**
The two lists of causes barely overlap, which is why the split is worth making before anything else.

Read it from the top down. The total fell 0.7%. One branch, the stores, grew 3.5%; the other, the
online shop, fell 21.2%. Inside the online shop, the average ticket stayed at R$ 500 and the number
of orders fell from 2,740 to 2,160. **So the question changed shape.** "Why did October fall?" is
too wide to test. "Why were 580 fewer online orders paid in October?" can be tested against
a short list of things.

## How far down to go

A tree can be split until every leaf is one product in one store on one day, and at that level
every leaf moves, so nothing stands out. **Stop splitting when one branch holds the change and its
siblings do not.** Here that happened at the second level: all nine stores grew, so splitting them
by category would spend an afternoon describing growth that needs no explanation. The online branch
was split once more, into orders and ticket, because that split chooses between two different lists
of causes.

The order of the splits matters less than people fear. Splitting by orders and ticket first, and by
channel second, would have reached the same leaf: online orders. What matters is that every split
adds back up to its parent, so that nothing falls through a gap between the branches. The next
section does that in a sheet.

The views of the last section did not come from Lívia's imagination. They came from a conversation
with each person, and the conversation has a shape. **The wrong way is to ask "what do you want on
the dashboard?"**, which gets a list of charts the person has seen somewhere else. The right way
asks about their work and leaves the dashboard for later.

## The interview

Half an hour per stakeholder, in the order that gets the most out of it:

1. **What decisions do you make about deliveries, and how often?** This is the question from lesson 4,
   pointed at one person.
2. **Tell me about the last time you made one.** A real recent case shows what information they
   actually used, and what they wished they had had.
3. **What would make you act differently?** This finds the threshold: the number at which Caio
   calls a carrier, or at which Marcos reroutes a van.
4. **How do you know today, without the dashboard?** There is always an answer, often a spreadsheet
   or a phone call, and it is what the dashboard has to beat.
5. **Who else should I talk to?** This is how the missing names of the first section turn up.

Write the answers down during the conversation, in the person's own words. "I need to know by ten
which vans won't make it" is a requirement; "real-time delivery monitoring" is a slogan.

## The mock before the build

Before any data is connected, Lívia draws each view on paper or in a slide, with invented numbers
labelled as invented, and shows it to the person it is for. **A mock takes an hour, and it surfaces
in ten minutes the misunderstandings that a finished dashboard would take a month to reveal.**
Marcos looked at his mock and said the list was no use sorted by order number; he needed it by route,
because he calls one driver at a time. Changing a drawing cost nothing.

## Confirm it in writing

After each conversation, a short note goes back to the person: the decision the view serves, the
definition it uses, the detail and the frequency, and what is not included. **The note turns a
conversation each side remembers differently into a document both can point at.** It is also where
the card of lesson 10 starts to exist for real.

## When two stakeholders want different definitions

It happens on almost every project. Renata wanted the dashboard's headline to be deliveries within
two days, because that is what her site promises to parcel customers. Caio wanted delivered on
promise. Both are reasonable, and lesson 10 showed they give very different numbers from the same
week: 50% and 83.3%, before the card's choice about cancellations.

**The analyst's job is not to pick the winner, and not to average the two.** It is to make the choice
visible and send it to the person who owns the objective. Lívia wrote one page: both definitions,
what each counts, what each gave in the same weeks, and what each would reward. Caio owns the
delivery objective, and he chose delivered on promise as the KPI. Renata's two-day figure went onto
her own page as a labelled metric for parcels. When the two owners cannot agree, the question goes
one level up, to Helena, with the same page.

Whatever is decided, **write down who decided and when**, because the person who lost will ask again
in six months, and the answer should be a document rather than a memory. Keeping those relationships
in good order over the life of a project, beyond the first conversation, is `data-storytelling`
lesson 13.

---
title: What it is not
version: 1
---

Five wrong pictures of BI are common enough that each one costs companies money. Naming them is the
quickest way to the right one.

## It is not a tool

Power BI, Tableau, Looker Studio, Metabase and Qlik are tools for building reports and dashboards.
**A company can own a licence for every one of them and have no business intelligence at all**, and a
company with a spreadsheet and a disciplined analyst can have a great deal of it. The tool draws the
answer; it does not decide which question is worth asking or what the number means. This course
names tools as examples and teaches none of them: `analytics-bi` lesson 4 covers Power BI, and the
rest of the `bi` track adds the others where they matter.

## It is not a dashboard

A dashboard is one way of delivering an answer, and often not the best one. The Monday sales figure
at Varanda reaches Helena Prado, the CEO, as two lines in an email, because she reads it on her
phone before the meeting. **The question decides the form.** A store manager looking for the orders
that are late today needs a list; a board looking at five years needs a few lines on a chart; a
director asking whether a campaign worked needs a paragraph with one number in it. Lessons 14 to 16
take those three cases one at a time.

## It is not a report factory

The failure here is quieter. Requests arrive — "can you pull sales by colour?" — and the analyst
produces them, one after another, without asking what each is for. After a year there are two
hundred reports, nobody knows which ones are read, and the analyst is too busy maintaining them to
answer a new question. **An analyst who asks "what will you decide with this?" before building
anything is doing BI; one who never asks is running a print shop.** Lesson 4 turns that question
into a routine.

## It is not data science, and it is not AI

BI mostly answers questions the company asks again and again — what sold, where, against what
target — and answers them the same way each time. Building a model that predicts which customer will
stop buying, or which machine will fail, is a different job with different methods, usually a data
scientist's. The two meet, and lesson 3 says where. A chatbot that answers questions about sales is a
new way of asking; it still needs a written definition of a sale behind it, or it will give two
directors two totals with more confidence than any spreadsheet did.

## It is not the truth, by default

A number on a screen looks finished. It is as good as its definition and its data, and both can be
wrong without anything on the screen saying so. Online orders counted twice because a pipeline ran
twice; a store missing because its system did not send the file; a "customer" who is really a
company buying for forty employees. **Checking a number before publishing it is part of the job,
not a courtesy**, and lesson 12 is about the numbers that are correct and still mislead.

## What is left

Take the five away and what remains is the definition from the last section: records turned into
answers to decisions, the same way every time. Everything else — the tools, the dashboards, the
models — is in service of that, and is worth exactly as much as the decisions it improves.

title: What business intelligence is
version: 1
---

Most people meet the words *business intelligence* on a software vendor's website, so they arrive
thinking it is a kind of program. It is a kind of work. **Business intelligence is turning the
records a company already keeps into answers to the decisions it has to make, the same way every
time.** Each part of that sentence carries weight, and this course is built around them.

## The records a company already keeps

A company writes down what it does because it has to. A till prints a receipt and the system behind
it stores one line per item sold. The warehouse records every pallet that arrives and every box that
leaves. Payroll records who was paid and for how many hours. None of this was written for analysis:
it exists to run the business, collect the money and satisfy the tax authority.

**BI starts from those records and not from a survey or an experiment.** That is its strength,
because the data is complete and already paid for, and its weakness, because it records what
happened and not why. A receipt says a customer bought a garden hose; it does not say they came in
for a barbecue and left without one because the shelf was empty.

## Answers to decisions

A table of a million receipt lines answers nothing. An answer is small: *sales in 2025 were R$ 98.0
million, 5.7% above 2024*. It has a definition (which sales, counted when), a period and a
comparison. Without the comparison, the number does not say whether the year was good.

**And an answer exists for a decision.** "How did last week go?" is asked on Monday morning because
somebody will move stock, call a store manager or change a campaign depending on the reply. A number
that changes nothing anybody does is a curiosity, however well it is drawn.

## The same way every time

This is the part that separates BI from a one-off analysis. Varanda Casa & Jardim, the home-and-garden
retailer this course follows, has nine stores in Minas Gerais and an online shop. Before it hired
Lívia Moraes as its first BI analyst in January 2026, the Monday sales figure was assembled by hand:
somebody exported a file from the stores' system, another from the online shop, pasted both into a
spreadsheet and typed a total into an email. It arrived after lunch, and **twice in 2025 two
directors walked into the same meeting with different totals for the same week**. One had counted
online orders on the day they were placed, the other on the day they were paid.

Neither was wrong. They had two definitions, and nobody had written either down. BI's first job at
Varanda was not a dashboard. It was one written definition of a sale, one place where the number is
computed from it, and the same answer for everybody by nine on Monday.

## Where the name comes from

The phrase is older than any of the software sold under it. Hans Peter Luhn, a researcher at IBM,
used it in 1958 for a system that would deliver the right information to the right person in an
organisation. The idea in that title — information arranged around the decisions of the people
who need it — is still the useful definition. The tools came later and keep changing; the work they
serve has not.

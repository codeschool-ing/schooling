A short page is easy to build and hard to keep short. Within a month of Caio's page going out with
its five indicators, three people asked for a number to be added: Otávio wanted delivery cost per
order, Renata wanted the number of online orders shipped the same day, and a store manager wanted
his store's footfall. **Each request is reasonable on its own, and saying yes to all of them takes
the page back to forty in a year.** Saying no without a reason makes enemies. What works is a
routine that everybody knows before the first request arrives.

## A written reason for every indicator

Each indicator on the page carries, on its card, the sentence that put it there: the question from
the objective that it answers, and the score it got. The reason does two jobs. It is the answer to
"why is that number there and mine is not?", which is the question every request really asks. And
it makes retiring an indicator possible, because a reason can stop being true, while "it has always
been there" never does.

## The parking lot

**A request that does not make the page goes on a list, not in the bin.** The list holds the
candidate, who asked for it, the question it would answer, its score, and the date it will be looked
at again. Otávio's delivery cost per order went there with 38 points, one short of the cut, and the
note that it answers a question about money rather than about the date, which is Otávio's objective
rather than Caio's. It also went onto Otávio's own monthly page, where it is a KPI.

The parking lot changes what a "no" means. The person asking hears "not this quarter, and here is
when we look again", their number keeps being produced as a metric, and they can see what it would
have to beat. Most requests are satisfied by that, because what they wanted was to know the number
exists.

## The quarterly review

Once a quarter, the owner and the analyst take thirty minutes over the page with three questions:

1. Did anybody act on each indicator in the last three months? An indicator nobody acted on in two
   reviews running is a candidate to retire.
2. Has the objective changed? If Varanda's objective moves from keeping the date to cutting delivery
   cost, the page follows, and the scoring sheet is redone with the new questions.
3. Does anything in the parking lot now beat something on the page? **One in, one out**: the page
   stays at its size, and the indicator that leaves goes back to being a metric, not into the bin.

## When a director insists

Sometimes the person asking outranks the owner of the page and does not want to wait a quarter. The
routine still helps, because it turns a contest of seniority into a question about the page. Lívia's
reply to a director is not "no"; it is the scoring row for their indicator, the five it would have to
displace, and the question: **which of these would you take off to make room?** Often the answer is
that the director wants the number on their own page, which is easy. Sometimes the answer is that
one of the five really should go, and then the page changes for a reason somebody wrote down.

When two directors want contradictory definitions of the same indicator, the argument belongs to
the card rather than to the page, and lesson 13 takes it up with the people involved. Keeping a
promise like this across many teams, with one shared list of definitions, is the work of
`analytics-bi` lesson 2 and of the data catalogue in `warehouse-modeling` lesson 12.

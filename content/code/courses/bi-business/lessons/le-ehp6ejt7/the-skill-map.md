Ask somebody what a BI analyst needs to know and the answer is a list of software: Excel, SQL, Power
BI. **The software is a quarter of it.** An analyst who knows every tool and cannot say how the
company makes money builds accurate dashboards about the wrong things, and one who cannot write a
clear paragraph sees good work ignored. The skills fall into four groups, and the job needs all four
at a working level more than any one of them at an expert level.

## Tools

**A spreadsheet, SQL, one BI tool, and a little modelling and code.** The spreadsheet is where most
questions are first answered and where most of the business already lives. SQL is how an analyst
asks the database directly instead of waiting for an export. A BI tool — Power BI, Tableau, Looker
Studio or Metabase, whichever the company owns — is how answers reach people on a schedule. Modelling
means knowing how the tables are arranged and why, enough to work with the engineer rather than
around them. A little code, usually Python, helps when a task is too large or too repetitive for the
spreadsheet.

None of this is taught here; the next courses of the `bi` track take it one tool at a time, starting
with `excel-analytics`.

## Analysis

**Descriptive statistics is the core**: averages and medians, spread, percentages and their
denominators, comparisons over time. Most BI questions are answered with nothing more, provided the
analyst knows when an average misleads and when a difference is too small to mean anything. Cleaning
data belongs here too, because a large part of every analysis is finding out what is wrong with the
input. Time series, cohorts and tests come after, in `bi-techniques`.

## Business

**How the company makes money, what each part of it decides, and the rules it has to follow about
data.** This is the group new analysts most often skip, and the one that separates a useful answer
from a correct one. An analyst who knows that Varanda's December carries 12.5% of the
year's sales, that a furniture sale on twelve instalments brings the money in over a year, and that
a store manager's bonus depends on the store's margin will ask better questions of every table.
Lesson 5 is a tour of the four functions of a company, and `data-governance` covers the rules.

## Communication

**Writing, choosing a chart, and running a meeting.** Most of what an analyst produces is read by
somebody in a hurry: a two-line email, one chart, a ten-minute slot. The analysis can be right and
still change nothing if the finding is in the fourth paragraph or the chart needs explaining.
`visualization` teaches the charts and `data-storytelling` the rest.

## What "a working level" means

A BI analyst does not need to be the best SQL writer in the company, or a statistician. **The test
for each skill is whether its absence would stop a piece of work**: an analyst who cannot write a
join waits for somebody else; one who does not know what a gross margin is cannot check a finance
report. Lívia arrived strong in spreadsheets and SQL, weaker in the business, and spent part of her
first month with Otávio learning how Varanda's monthly close works. The next section shows where her
hours went.

Requests arrive as solutions: "I need a sales dashboard", "can you pull last year by colour?". The
new analyst's instinct is to build exactly what was asked, quickly, as a way of being useful. **The
request is the person's guess at what would help, and the analyst's first job is to find the decision
behind it.** Five questions do that, and they take about fifteen minutes.

## The five questions

| question | what it finds out |
|---|---|
| What will you decide with this? | whether there is a decision at all, and which one |
| Who decides? | who the answer is for, which sets its form and its level of detail |
| By when? | the deadline after which the answer is no use |
| What would change your mind? | which result would make the person act differently — the part worth measuring |
| How is the number defined? | what is counted, over what period, compared with what |

The first three come from lesson 2: a decision has options, a deadline and a cost of being wrong.
**The fourth is the one that turns a vague request into a measurable one**, because it names the
result that matters before anybody has seen the data, which also protects the analysis from being
read afterwards to fit what the person already wanted. The fifth is where most of lesson 10 starts.

## Renata's request, before

In her second week Lívia received this from Renata Sá, the marketing director:

> I need a sales dashboard for the online shop. Everything: by day, by category, by campaign, by
> region. Can you have it by Friday?

Built as asked, it would have been a dozen charts covering every way to slice a sale, published on
Friday and opened twice. Nothing in the request says what Renata would do differently after looking
at it.

## The conversation

Lívia booked fifteen minutes and asked the five questions.

**What will you decide with this?** Renata thought about it and said the real question was whether
to keep spending on the paid-search campaign that had started in November, or move the money to
email. The contract with the agency renews on 31 March.

**Who decides?** Renata, with Otávio approving any change above R$ 50,000 a month.

**By when?** The agency needs notice by 15 March, so the answer is needed by 10 March.

**What would change your mind?** If paid search brings orders at a cost per order clearly above
email's, she would cut it. "Clearly" turned out to mean more than R$ 10 an order of difference, once
she thought about it.

**How is the number defined?** Here they found the disagreement. The agency reports orders from
paid search by the last click before purchase; Renata's team counts an order for the first channel
that brought the customer. Lívia wrote both down and they agreed to show both, side by side.

## Renata's request, after

What Lívia wrote back the same afternoon:

> Decision: keep or cut paid search when the agency contract renews on 31 March.
>
> Decides: Renata; Otávio approves above R$ 50,000 a month. Needed by: 10 March.
>
> Measure: cost per order, paid search against email, November 2025 to February 2026, by month.
> Orders counted two ways — last click and first channel — shown side by side.
>
> Would change the decision: a gap of more than R$ 10 per order either way.

That is one table and one chart, not a dashboard, and it was delivered on 6 March. **The written
version is the deliverable of the conversation**, and sending it back for the person to confirm is
what stops the work from drifting while it is being done. If Renata later wants a monthly view of
the online shop, that is a second request with its own five answers.

## When the person cannot answer

Sometimes the answer to "what will you decide with this?" is "nothing, I just want to keep an eye on
it". That is not always a refusal to engage. Some people genuinely need to watch a number to notice
when it moves; that is what operational and tactical reports are for, and lessons 14 and 15 are
about building them well. **The question then becomes "what would make you act?"**, and the answer
is a threshold, which is just as useful a requirement as a decision.

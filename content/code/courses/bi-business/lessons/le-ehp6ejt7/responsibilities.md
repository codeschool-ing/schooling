---
title: What the analyst is responsible for
version: 1
---

Skills are what an analyst can do. Responsibilities are what the company can count on them to do
when nobody is checking. **A BI analyst's numbers are read by people who cannot verify them**, and
that makes six things the analyst's job even though no request will ever ask for them.

## Owning the definitions

The analyst keeps the written definition of every number they publish: what is counted, what is
excluded, over what period, from which source. The owner of the number decides what it means, as lesson 3 put it.
**The analyst makes sure that decision is written down, findable, and the same in
every report that shows the number.** When Lívia published the Monday sales email, the definition of
a sale went with it, as a link at the bottom: paid orders and till receipts, counted on the day of
payment, returns subtracted in the week they happen.

## Checking before publishing

Every number is checked before it leaves, against something independent: last week's figure, the
sum of the parts, the finance system's total. **The check that matters is the one that would have
caught a mistake you can name**, not a ritual. Lívia's Monday check takes ten minutes: the total
against the finance system's daily takings, each store against its previous four weeks, and the
count of stores that sent a file. In her third week it caught a store whose file had arrived empty,
and the email went out with a line saying Betim's figure was missing instead of a total about
R$ 170,000 short, which is what an ordinary week at Betim sells.

## Documenting

Not only definitions: where each report's data comes from, what was assumed, what was left out and
why. **The test is whether somebody else could rerun the work next year and get the same answer
without asking you.** It feels like overhead in February. Next December, when the board asks how the
2025 repeat rate was computed and the person who did it is on holiday, it is the only thing that
helps.

## Saying "we don't know yet"

Sometimes the data cannot answer the question, or not by the deadline, or not with enough
confidence to act on. The analyst says so, plainly, with what would be needed to find out. **A
confident answer to a question the data cannot settle is worse than no answer**, because it will be
acted on. Lesson 2 listed the places where the data runs out; this is the habit of admitting it
out loud.

## Keeping confidential things confidential

A BI analyst sees more of the company than almost anybody: every salary in a headcount report,
every customer's address and purchase history, the sales of a store before its manager does. **Access
to data is for the work it was given for**, not for curiosity, and a report shows what its reader
needs and no more. Salaries appear in HR's reports by band, not by name; customer lists leave the
database only when there is a reason and a basis for it. Personal data has rules of its own, and
`data-governance` lessons 6 and 7 cover them.

## Not changing a number to please

Sooner or later somebody senior will dislike a number and ask for it to be "looked at again". Looking
again is fine: numbers are wrong sometimes, and a second check costs little. **Changing the
definition, the period or the comparison until the number looks better is not**, and it destroys the
one thing a BI function has to offer, which is that its numbers mean the same thing whoever they are
inconvenient for. When Renata asked whether the paid-search figures could leave out the two weeks the
campaign was paused, Lívia showed both versions, labelled, and said which one answered the question
they had agreed. That is the whole technique: never hide a version, and always say which one is the
answer.

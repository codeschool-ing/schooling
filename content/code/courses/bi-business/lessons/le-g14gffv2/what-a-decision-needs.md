title: What a decision needs from data
version: 1
---

A new analyst is tempted to treat every question as deserving an analysis, because analysis is the
job and saying no feels like laziness. **Data is worth what it can change.** If no answer the data
could give would change what somebody does, the analysis is a cost with nothing on the other side,
however interesting the result. This section gives the test Lívia uses before she opens a
spreadsheet.

## Three things every decision has

**Options.** At least two things somebody could do, and doing nothing counts as one. "Enlarge
Contagem" is not a decision on its own; "enlarge Contagem, enlarge another store, or enlarge none this
year" is.

**A deadline.** The date after which the answer is no use. Varanda's building budget for 2026 is
approved by the board in April, so an analysis of which store to enlarge that arrives in May changes
nothing, however good it is.

**A cost of being wrong.** What it costs to pick the worse option. Enlarging a store is a project of
about R$ 4 million and a year of building work, and if it goes to the wrong store the money is spent
and the better store waits another budget cycle. A wrong reorder of garden cushions costs a few
thousand reais and is fixed next month.

If Lívia cannot name the options, the deadline and the cost, **she is not looking at a decision yet,
and the first job is to find out what it is** — lesson 4 turns that into five questions she asks every
time.

## The test: could an answer change the choice?

Before analysing, ask what result would make the person choose differently. Two of Lívia's requests
from her first month show the two outcomes.

Helena's store question passes. If Contagem turns out to sell less per square metre than other
stores, the case for spending R$ 4 million on more of its floor weakens, and another store becomes a
candidate. A plausible answer changes the choice, the cost of being wrong is large, and the
analysis takes an afternoon. **Afternoon against R$ 4 million is the easiest trade she will see this
year.**

The second request failed it. Sônia Freitas, the HR manager, asked for a breakdown of 2025
sick-leave days by day of the week, "to see". When Lívia asked what she would do with it, the answer
was nothing in particular: the leave policy is set by law and by the union agreement, and no pattern
would move it. The breakdown would have taken two days. Lívia wrote back saying so and asked whether
there was a decision behind it; there was not, and nobody missed the report.

## Worth less than it looks, worth more than it looks

A rough rule follows, and it is enough at this stage. **The value of an answer grows with the cost
of being wrong and with the chance that the answer changes the choice, and shrinks with the cost of
getting it.**

That rule explains two things that surprise new analysts. Some impressive analyses are worth
nothing: a beautifully drawn report on a decision that was already taken. And some ugly ones are
worth a great deal: a count done by hand in ten minutes that stops a store being enlarged by mistake.
Formal methods put a number on the value of information, and they belong to decision analysis rather
than to this course; what you need is the habit of asking the question before the work starts.

## When the answer is "we already know"

Sometimes the data would only confirm what everybody believes, with no realistic chance of a
surprise. Then the analysis is worth little, and saying so is part of the job. The judgement is
whether the belief is as safe as it feels — which is exactly what the store ranking, in the next
section, shows it was not.

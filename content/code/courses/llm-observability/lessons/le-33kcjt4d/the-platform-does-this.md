title: The platform you are reading does this

Lessons 1 to 7 observed the assistant: what it did, how long it took, what it cost, how people reacted.
From here the course **evaluates** it: decides, reply by reply, whether the reply was any good. The first
kind of evaluation is the one that needs no judgement at all, only a rule a program can apply.

You have been on the receiving end of one all along. Every exercise in this course is marked by a
grader on the platform's server, and the way it works is a summary of this lesson:

- **There is an answer key, written down in advance.** A quiz names its correct choice; a cloze lists
  the answers it accepts. Nothing is decided at marking time that was not decided when the question
  was written.
- **Normalisation is declared, not assumed.** A cloze says whether case and accents matter. In a
  question about a SQL keyword they do not; in a question about naming conventions the difference is
  the point. The grader does what the question says, and a question that says nothing is marked
  strictly.
- **The verdict is binary.** Right or wrong, never partly right, because partial credit is a decision
  about assessment that would have to be made once for every type.
- **An answer the grader does not understand is an error, not a pass.** A typo in a question's type
  does not become a question everybody gets right.
- **The key is checked against itself.** Before a question is published, its own answer is fed to the
  grader and must pass, because a question whose key is wrong is the one failure a system with no
  human reviewer cannot absorb.

Every one of those carries over to an assistant. The evaluation set is the answer key. The comparison
declares how loose it is. A reply passes or fails each check. A check that cannot decide says so. And
the checks themselves are tested, because a rule that passes everything measures nothing.

What does **not** carry over is the part that makes marking an exercise easy: an exercise is asked in
a form that has one right answer. A customer's question does not, and the rest of this lesson is about
how far a program can get anyway.

title: Three reasons a value goes missing
version: 1

**The question that decides everything about a blank is what made it blank.** In 1976 the
statistician Donald Rubin sorted the answers into three kinds, and the names stuck even though they
are famously confusing. They describe the relationship between whether a value is missing and the
values themselves.

**Missing completely at random (MCAR).** Whether the value is missing has nothing to do with
anything — not with the value, not with any other column. A sensor that drops one reading in a
hundred by a fault in the cable is the textbook case. The rows that kept their value are a fair
sample of all the rows, so an average over them is an honest average, only with fewer rows behind
it.

**Missing at random (MAR).** Whether the value is missing depends on **other columns you can see**,
and on nothing else. Rapidex never reports delivery times, so every Rapidex delivery is blank; the
blank depends on the courier, and the courier is in the file. Within a courier, the blanks are
random. The name is the confusing part: the blanks are not random overall, only once you account
for the columns that explain them.

**Missing not at random (MNAR).** Whether the value is missing depends **on the value itself**, or on
something you did not record. A timer that stops at two hours leaves every delivery of two hours or
more blank. The values that are missing are, by construction, different from the values that are
present, and nothing in the remaining rows tells you how different.

## Why the kind matters

The three are not a taxonomy for its own sake; each one licenses a different response.

| | the rows with a value are | an average over them is | what lesson 4 can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| MCAR | a fair sample of all rows | unbiased, with less precision | drop the rows, or fill from the rest |
| MAR | fair within each group the explaining columns form | biased overall, fixable by group | fill or weight by group |
| MNAR | systematically different from the missing ones | biased, and the data cannot say by how much | flag, and get the information elsewhere |

**MNAR is the dangerous one** because it is the only one that cannot be detected from the data
alone. MCAR and MAR leave traces: compare the rows with and without a value, and either they look
alike (MCAR) or they differ in some column you have (MAR). MNAR can look exactly like MAR — the
missing rows differ on some visible column — while the real cause is the hidden value. The timer
section of this lesson is that case, and the clue there comes from outside the blanks.

## Not applicable is none of the three

One more kind of blank does not fit Rubin's scheme, because nothing is missing at all: **a value
that cannot exist**. A pickup has no delivery time. It is not missing at random or otherwise; there
is no delivery to time. The next section deals with those first, because separating them out is
what makes the rest of the analysis possible — 5,717 pickup blanks mixed with 456 timer blanks would
hide the timer completely.

title: Where the copies are kept

A cache is a copy of an answer, kept so that the next person who asks the same question gets the
copy instead of making the original do the work again. The idea is simple. **What makes HTTP caching
hard is that the copies are kept in several places at once, by programs you do not control, under
rules you set only by what your responses say.**

From the browser to the database, a request for one book can be answered by four different copies:

| where | who it belongs to | what it may keep |
|---|---|---|
| the **browser's cache** | one person | anything, including pages only that person may see; it is a **private** cache |
| a **CDN** or a company's proxy | everybody who passes through it | only what is safe for everyone; it is a **shared** cache (lesson 7) |
| the **reverse proxy's cache**, Nginx here | everybody who reaches the site | the same rule: shared (this lesson's last sections) |
| an **application cache**, Redis or Memcached | the application | whatever the application decides, in its own format (lessons 8 to 11) |

The first three speak HTTP and obey the same headers: **one `Cache-Control` line written by the
origin governs every copy between it and the person**, including copies in places the origin's owner
will never see. The fourth is different in kind. It stores data rather than responses, and nothing in
HTTP reaches it; the application decides what goes in and when it comes out.

Every one of them makes the same trade. **A cached answer is faster and cheaper, and it may be
wrong**: the price changed a second after the copy was taken, and whoever gets the copy sees the old
price. The headers in this lesson are how a server says, for each response, how long that risk is
acceptable and who may take it. Lesson 6 is about the moment it stops being acceptable.

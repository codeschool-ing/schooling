**"Is the ticket shop good?" is a question nobody can answer, and "does it sell a ticket in under ten
seconds on a phone?" is one anybody can.** The way from the first to the second is to split quality
into characteristics small enough to check. The split most of the industry uses is the product quality
model of **ISO/IEC 25010**, an international standard first published in 2011 and revised in 2023.

The 2023 revision names nine characteristics. Each is a family of questions, and each family has a
tester's version:

| characteristic | the question it asks | at Cine Aurora |
|---|---|---|
| **functional suitability** | does it do the right things, correctly and completely? | the right price for every customer and session |
| **performance efficiency** | how fast, and with how much of the machine? | a seat map that opens before the customer gives up |
| **compatibility** | does it work beside, and with, other systems? | the box office and the website selling from one set of seats |
| **interaction capability** | can people use it, including people who use it differently? | a blind customer buying with a screen reader |
| **reliability** | does it keep working, and recover when it does not? | sales on the night a blockbuster opens |
| **security** | does it protect data and resist misuse? | card details, and nobody reading another customer's orders |
| **maintainability** | can it be changed safely? | a new price rule added without breaking the old ones |
| **flexibility** | can it move to new places and conditions? | the same shop running for a second cinema |
| **safety** | can it avoid putting people or things in danger? | a full room never sold past its capacity |

**Interaction capability** was called usability in 2011, **flexibility** was portability, and
**safety** is new. You will meet the older names in most books and job adverts; they mean nearly the
same thing.

## What the model is for

The model is a **checklist against forgetting**, and that is its whole value. A team building a ticket
shop thinks about functional suitability every day, because that is what the requirements describe.
Nobody writes a requirement about the night a blockbuster opens until the first time the shop falls
over on one. Reading down nine names before testing starts turns "we never thought of it" into "we
decided not to", which is a different and much better sentence to say afterwards.

It is not a requirement that every characteristic be tested equally. A ticket shop and a hospital's
dosage calculator both have all nine; safety is a footnote for the first and the whole point of the
second. Choosing which characteristics matter most for this product, this month, is prioritisation,
and lesson 20 is about how.

## Functional and non-functional

The first row is often called **functional** quality and the other eight **non-functional**. The split
is useful as a word and misleading as an idea. It suggests the eight are extras, and some teams treat
them that way: the functions get tested every sprint, the rest "before the big release", which in
practice means never.

A customer does not make the distinction. A seat map that takes forty seconds to open does not feel to
them like a non-functional issue; it feels like a shop that does not work. Testing the other eight is
mostly the subject of `non-functional-testing`, later in the track. What belongs here is the habit of
asking about them early, while asking still costs a sentence.

## Quality in use

The same family of standards has a second, smaller model, **quality in use**, which sat inside ISO/IEC 25010 in 2011 and has had its own document, ISO/IEC 25019, since 2023. It measures what happens when
real people use the product for real tasks: whether they succeed, how long it takes, how satisfied they
are, and what risk they run. It is fitness for use, from the previous section, turned into things that
can be counted. The product model is about the thing; the quality-in-use model is about the thing in
somebody's hands, and a product can score well on the first and badly on the second.

The model is a vocabulary rather than a procedure. It tells you what to ask about. It says nothing
about how the product came to be the way it is, and that is the other half of the word.

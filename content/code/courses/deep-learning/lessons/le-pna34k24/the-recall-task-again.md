Lesson 14 ended with a measurement. On the task of naming the first of a sequence of random symbols, the
LSTM was perfect at length 20 and reached 0.144 at length 50 after 3,000 steps, little above the 0.125 of
guessing. **This section gives a transformer exactly the same task and exactly the same budget**, and
changes nothing else. It uses the `seqtask.py` from lesson 14 unchanged, the same Adam at 0.003, the same
3,000 steps of 64 fresh sequences, the same seeds and the same 1,000 test sequences, on one thread as
before.

The model is small and built from this lesson's parts. It has an embedding of 32 for the symbols, a
learnt table of 50 positions, one block with 4 heads, a last layer norm, and a linear layer reading the
last position. There is no causal mask, because only the last position is read and nothing comes after
it. Save as `~/dl/recall_tf.py`:

**The transformer named the first symbol of all 1,000 test sequences at every length, already at the
first measurement, after 250 steps.** Lesson 14 did not measure at 250 steps, so the fair comparison is
the other three columns:

| length | RNN after 3,000 steps | LSTM after 3,000 steps | transformer after 3,000 steps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| 20 | 0.482 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| 50 | 0.118 | 0.144 | 1.000 |

It did that with 10,728 parameters, half of the LSTM model's 21,640. **Speed is not where it won.** On one
thread of the course's machine the three runs took 19, 29 and 62 seconds, 110 seconds together, where
lesson 14's six runs, half of them the cheaper RNN, took 2 minutes 2 seconds. The time grew with the length because attention compares every
position with every other one. Lesson 14's argument for the transformer was about
hardware that does many multiplications at once, and one thread is the opposite of that; this course has
no graphics card, so that part was not run.

Why the length stopped mattering is visible inside the trained model. The program below loads the model
of length 50 and asks the block which positions the last one looked at. Save as `~/dl/where.py`:

**All four heads look at position 1 from position 50.** Three of them put more than 0.99 of their weight
there and the fourth 0.844, and no other position received more than 0.021 on average. The LSTM had to
carry the first symbol through 49 rewrites of its state. Here it is one weighted sum away, whatever the
length, which is what the table above could not show from outside.

The positions are what made that possible, and taking them out shows it:

```
ana@vm:~/dl$ python recall_tf.py --no-positions
parameters: this model 9128  lesson 14's LSTM 21640
accuracy on 1,000 new sequences after 250, 1,000, 2,000 and 3,000 steps (chance is 0.125)
length 50   0.162  0.178  0.182  0.171     63 s
```

Without positions the same model reached 0.171 after 3,000 steps. That is above chance, because the first
symbol is always among the symbols present, and a model that sees which symbols occur can use that much.
**It cannot do better without knowing which symbol came first**, and nothing in it can tell.

The comparison has limits, and they matter. The task asks for one hop, and attention is built for exactly
that, so it is the easiest possible case for a transformer and the hardest for a recurrent network. It
says nothing about which model is better at language, and lesson 14 left open whether a different recipe
would take the LSTM to 50. What it does show, with the same budget and the same data, is the property the
transformer was adopted for: **the distance between two tokens is always one step.** Lesson 16 turns
to what those tokens are when the sequence is text.

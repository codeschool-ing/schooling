`filter.py` used a filter somebody wrote. The network's first layer has sixteen, and **nobody wrote
any of them**: they started as small random numbers and the gradient moved them, step by step, towards
whatever made the loss fall. `compare.py` saved the trained weights in `cnn.pt`. This program reads
them back and prints the first layer. Save as `~/dl/filters.py`:

read 9 inputs with weights between -1/3 and 1/3, which is the 0.33 on the first line; after training
the largest is 1.17. Drawn as grids, with one colour for weights above zero and another for weights

Several of them are edge detectors of the kind `filter.py` used. **Filter 2 is the nearest to the one
written by hand**: its largest weights, 0.85 and 0.92, are in its right-hand column, so it answers most
where ink sits to the right of a blank. Filter 8 is strongly positive along its top and left and
strongly negative in its bottom right corner, a diagonal edge nobody asked for, and filter 10 is a
weaker version of the same. Filter 15 is slightly negative along its top row and strongly positive
below it, so it answers for the top edge of a horizontal stroke, the bar of a 7 or the top of a 5.

**Read that as a guess, not a finding.** On 8 by 8 images a 3 by 3 filter covers a large piece of the
digit, and several of the sixteen, 13 and 14 among them, stayed small and look like nothing in
particular. The second layer combines all sixteen maps through 4,640 weights, and what the network
uses is that combination, not any one filter. On large photographs the first layer's filters are
much tidier: the network of Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton in 2012 drew its 96 first-layer filters,
and they are oriented edges and patches of colour, learnt from images alone.

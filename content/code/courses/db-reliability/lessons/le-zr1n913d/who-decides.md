---
title: Who decides which node is the primary
version: 1
---

Lesson 15 ended on a rule: an automatic failover is only safe when something other than the two
servers decides. Two servers that cannot reach each other can each conclude that the other is dead,
and nothing in either of them can break the tie.

A **cluster manager** breaks it by putting the decision somewhere both can see. **Patroni** is the
most used one for PostgreSQL. It runs beside each PostgreSQL server, as a separate process that
starts, stops, promotes and demotes it, and it keeps the cluster's state in a **distributed
configuration store**, here **etcd**: a small database built for exactly one job, agreeing on a value
among several machines, and refusing to answer rather than answering wrongly when they cannot agree.

The whole design rests on one key in etcd, the **leader key**:

- The node holding the leader key is the primary, and **only** that node. Every other node makes
  itself a replica of whichever node the key names.
- The key has a **time to live** (`ttl`, 30 seconds here). The leader renews it on every loop, every
  10 seconds. If it stops renewing, because its machine died or because it can no longer reach etcd,
  the key expires.
- When the key is gone, the replicas race to take it, and etcd lets exactly one of them win. The
  winner promotes itself; the others follow it. A replica too far behind
  (`maximum_lag_on_failover`) does not enter the race.
- A leader that **cannot renew** its key demotes itself, because by then somebody else may already
  hold it.

The last rule answers lesson 15's first question, "is the primary really gone?", differently from a
script: the primary does not have to be gone. **It only has to lose the key**, and a primary that
cannot reach etcd stops being a primary on its own, whether or not it is alive. The cost of that rule
is the subject of this lesson's last two sections.

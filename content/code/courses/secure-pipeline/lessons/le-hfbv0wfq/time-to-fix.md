Time to remediate is the number of days between the day a check raised a finding and the day it was
fixed. It is the closest thing a security programme has to a speed, and the usual way to report it
is the mean, "MTTR", the mean time to remediate. **The mean is the wrong average for this, and the
data says why in one table.**

### The measuring program

One program reads both files and prints any of six reports. Save it as `~/metrics/measure.py`:

### Mean, median and the 90th percentile

```
ana@vm:~/metrics$ python3 measure.py ttr
severity  closed  mean median  p90 in SLA
critical      21   6.7      4   11    71%
high         103  20.4     15   40    81%
medium       134  47.3   34.5  103    86%
low           80  75.2     57  142    94%
```

Look at medium. 134 medium findings were fixed; the mean is 47.3 days and the median 34.5. Half of
them took 34.5 days or less, so the mean sits well above the typical case, and that gap is the tail.
The 90th percentile, 103 days, says how long the tail is: one fix in ten took more than three months.

**A mean is moved by the tail, and the tail is a handful of findings.** One medium fix in this data
took 199 days. Had it taken 400, the median would not move at all and the mean would rise by about a
day and a half, so a single stubborn dependency would show up as the whole team getting slower. The
median answers "how long does a fix usually take here", which is the question a team can act on,
and the 90th percentile answers "how bad are the bad cases", which is the one somebody should be
looking into. Report the two together. A mean on its own, the number many dashboards label MTTR,
mixes both questions into one figure that answers neither.

The same holds across severities. Critical: mean 6.7, median 4. Low: mean 75.2, median 57. In every
row the mean is above the median, which is what a long right tail looks like.

### The SLA, per severity

A service level agreement for findings is a promise of a number of days per severity. This program
uses 7 for critical, 30 for high, 90 for medium and 180 for low. **Those four numbers are a common
shape, not a standard.** They are a decision, and the people who have to meet them should take part
in making it, because an SLA nobody can meet is a gate that fails on noise, which lesson 7 showed
gets switched off.

Two numbers come from an SLA, and the report above has only the first:

- **The share fixed within it**, the last column: 71% of critical fixes landed within 7 days, 94% of
  low fixes within 180. Critical is the row to worry about, because it is the shortest promise on the
  most serious findings, and it is missed most often in this data.
- **The open findings already past it**, which a report over fixed findings cannot contain. They are
  in the next section's debt report: 2 high, 9 medium and 7 low findings are open and late.

### What time to remediate cannot see

The report counts only findings with `resolution` equal to `fixed`. A finding that is still open has
no time to remediate yet, so it is not in the table. **The findings missing from the table are the
oldest ones.** A team that fixes the quick findings and leaves the hard ones open gets a better
median every month while its backlog grows. That is survivorship bias in its simplest form: the
measurement only sees the findings that survived to be fixed.

There are two ways to keep it honest. Report time to remediate beside the debt, so the open findings
are always on the same page. Or compute an age for open findings as of today, as `load()` already
does, and look at both distributions. What you should not do is pick one and leave the other out.

The other thing the table cannot see is how a finding was closed, because it has filtered on
`fixed` already. Counting every way off the list as a remediation is a one-word change, and the
section on Goodhart's law runs it to see what it does to these medians.

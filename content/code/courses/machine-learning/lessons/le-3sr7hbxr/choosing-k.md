title: How many clusters
version: 1
---

k-means finds as many groups as it is told to, and it will find twelve in data that has two. The number
has to come from somewhere else, and two measurements help.

- **Inertia** always falls as *k* rises: more centres are always closer to the rows. What matters is
  where it stops falling fast. That bend is the **elbow**.
- The **silhouette** asks each row how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the nearest other
  one, on a scale from −1 to 1, and averages. Near 1, the groups are tight and apart; near 0, the rows sit
  between groups.

Save this as `choose_k.py`:

```python
# choose_k.py
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

baskets = pd.read_csv("data/baskets.csv")
scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(baskets.drop(columns="customer_id"))
print(" k   inertia   silhouette")
for k in range(2, 9):
    kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(scaled)
    print(f"{k:2} {kmeans.inertia_:9,.0f} {silhouette_score(scaled, kmeans.labels_):12.3f}")
```

**Both say four, and that is unusually clear.** Inertia falls by 2,415 from two to three, by 1,632 from
three to four, and then by 119: past four, each new centre only splits a group that was already
together. The silhouette peaks at four, 0.589, and falls on both sides.

Real data is rarely this tidy. The elbow is often a gentle curve with no corner, and the silhouette may
peak at two, because splitting a population into two halves is always fairly clean. Then the number
comes from the use: the marketing team can write four newsletters, not eleven. **A k that the business
cannot act on is wrong whatever the silhouette says**, and between two values the measurements cannot
separate, the smaller one is easier to explain and to keep.

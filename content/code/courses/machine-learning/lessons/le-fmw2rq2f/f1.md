---
title: F1, and what a single number costs
version: 1
---

People want one number, and the usual way to fold precision and recall into one is the **F1 score**:
their harmonic mean, `2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)`. At the break-even cut it is
**0.317**.

The harmonic mean is chosen because it punishes imbalance. The arithmetic mean of a precision of 1.0
and a recall of 0.01 is about 0.5, which flatters a model that finds one leaver in a hundred. The
harmonic mean is 0.02, which does not. F1 is high only when both are.

It has a cost, and `metrics.py` shows it. **The cut with the highest F1 is 0.2, at 0.367; the cut with
the highest net value is also 0.2, at R$ 23,448.** Here they agree. Now look at 0.1: F1 barely moves,
0.359, and the net value collapses to R$ 1,848. F1 thinks a cut that sends credits to thousands of
stayers is nearly as good as the best one; the business thinks it is nearly worthless.

The reason is in the formula. **F1 weighs a missed leaver and a wasted credit as equally bad**, and here
they are not: a false positive costs R$ 40 and a missed leaver R$ 104. F1 also ignores the true
negatives completely, so it cannot tell sending 2,000 credits to stayers from sending 200.

There is a version with a dial, **F-beta**, which weighs recall beta times as much as precision; F2
counts a missed positive as worse than a false alarm. It is better than F1 when the two errors are known
to differ, and worse than the actual costs whenever those are known.

**The practical rule:** when the costs of the two errors are known, as at Feira em Casa, optimise the
money, and report precision and recall beside it so people can see how it is made. When they are not
known, F1 is a reasonable default for comparing models at their best cuts, and it should be said out
loud that it assumes the two mistakes cost the same.

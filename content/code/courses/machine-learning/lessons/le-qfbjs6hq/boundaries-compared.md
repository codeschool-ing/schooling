*yes* and a region where it says *no*. The line between them is its **decision boundary**, and its
shape is the clearest way to see what each model can and cannot do. Here are four, fitted to the

- **Logistic regression** draws a straight line, because that is all it can draw. It gets the
  general direction and misses both curls.
- **k-nearest neighbours**, with 15 neighbours, follows the data closely, curling where the
  crescents curl and wobbling where the points are sparse.
- **Gaussian naive Bayes**, the version for numeric columns, assumes each class is a bell-shaped
  cloud and draws the curve where the two clouds are equally likely. Two clouds of about the same
  spread meet along something close to a straight line, and that is what it draws here, scoring
  exactly what logistic regression scores.
- **The rbf support vector machine** draws a smooth curve that follows the crescents, calmer than the
  neighbours and more flexible than the line.

On these test points the neighbours score highest, 89.3% against the support vector machine's
88.0%, a difference of two points out of 150 that a different seed could reverse. No shape is right
in general. On data whose classes really are separated by a straight line, the
logistic boundary is the right one, and the curvy boundaries are fitting noise. **The data decides,
on validation rows.**

## When each one is worth reaching for

| model | reach for it when | avoid it when |
|---|---|---|
| **logistic regression** | always first; when the model must be read or explained | the signal lives in thresholds and interactions nobody wrote |
| **k-nearest neighbours** | few rows, few scaled columns, and "similar cases" is itself the answer someone wants | many rows (slow) or many columns (the curse) |
| **naive Bayes** | text, counts, and a fast baseline | the probabilities are used as probabilities without calibrating |
| **support vector machines** | few to medium rows, many columns, a curved boundary | hundreds of thousands of rows, or probabilities are needed directly |

On Feira em Casa's churn table, which has tens of thousands of rows, a mix of numbers and
categories, gaps, thresholds and interactions, none of these four wins. That is the usual result
for tabular business data, and the reason the next two lessons are about trees.

title: Support vector machines and the widest margin

A logistic regression draws a line between the classes that makes the data most likely. A **support
vector machine** draws the line that leaves **the widest possible margin**: the widest empty street
between the two classes, with the line down its middle. Only the rows on the edge of the street, or
on the wrong side of it, decide where it goes; they are the **support vectors**, and every other row
could be deleted without moving the line.

Real classes overlap, so the street cannot be completely empty. `C` sets the price of a row inside
the street or on the wrong side: a large `C` charges a lot and gets a narrow street that bends around
individual rows, a small `C` charges little and gets a wide, calm one. It is the same dial as
regularisation in lesson 5, pointed the other way.

The part that makes support vector machines famous is the **kernel**. A straight street cannot
separate two crescents curled into each other, but the same street drawn in a richer space, with
new axes computed from the old ones, can. The kernel computes the distances in that richer space
without ever building it. The common choice, `rbf`, measures similarity by distance and lets the
boundary curve as much as the data needs.

The crescents here come from `make_moons`, which draws them with a seed, so they are the same on
every computer. Save this as `svm.py`:

```python
# svm.py
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=600, noise=0.25, random_state=0)   # two interlocking crescents
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)

for kernel in ["linear", "rbf"]:
    svm = SVC(kernel=kernel, C=1).fit(X_train, y_train)
    print(f"{kernel:6}  right on {svm.score(X_test, y_test):.1%} of the test points, "
          f"{len(svm.support_)} of {len(X_train)} training points are support vectors")
```

```
ana@lab:~/ml$ python svm.py
linear  right on 85.3% of the test points, 142 of 450 training points are support vectors
rbf     right on 88.0% of the test points, 98 of 450 training points are support vectors
```

The straight street gets **85.3%** of the test points right, and the curved one **88.0%**. The rbf
kernel also needs fewer support vectors, 98 against 142: the curved boundary fits the crescents
with fewer rows sitting in its street.

## Why they are less used than they were

Support vector machines were the strongest general-purpose classifier of the 2000s. On tables like
Feira em Casa's they have since been overtaken by gradient boosting, and they have two practical
problems. **Fitting time grows faster than the number of rows**, roughly with its square or worse,
so 38,000 rows is slow and a million is impractical. And **they do not produce probabilities**: the
distance to the street is a score, and `probability=True` fits an extra model on top to turn it into
one. They still do well with few rows and many columns, such as text, where the street is drawn in a
space nobody could picture.

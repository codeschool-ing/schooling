Back to the shop. For one person at home, **Home** is enough: it runs the same programs at the same
speed, and nothing in the table's "no" column is something a household misses.

For the office, **Pro**, and the reasons are specific rather than general:

1. **It can join the organisation.** Lesson 2 said an office PC should be set up with a work account.
   On Home that option does not exist, so the PC stays a personal machine that happens to be in an
   office, managed by nobody.
2. **BitLocker with the key where the office wants it.** On Home the recovery key goes to whichever
   Microsoft account signed in first. That is lesson 4's Activation Lock problem again: a disk that
   only one person can open is that person's disk.
3. **Remote Desktop.** When Ana supports somebody from her own desk, or the accountant works from home
   on the office PC, the PC has to accept the connection.
4. **Policies.** Updates postponed until after month-end closing, USB sticks blocked on the reception
   PC: those are Group Policy settings, on Pro.

**The price difference is usually smaller than the first hour of work** that Home's limits cause, and
the upgrade later costs more than buying Pro now. The same judgement runs through the rest of this
course: for anything shared, pay for the thing that lets it be managed.

## The Enterprise question

Enterprise is not bought in a shop. It comes with the **Microsoft 365** enterprise plans, E3 and E5, as a
subscription per user, and a PC with Pro **steps up to Enterprise by itself** when a user with that
licence signs in. An eight-person office rarely needs it; a company where security teams want
Credential Guard and 36-month versions does.

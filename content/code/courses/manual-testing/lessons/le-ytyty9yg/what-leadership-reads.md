The report a tester is tempted to send up is the one that shows the work: how many cases were
written, how many ran, the pass rate, the defects found, a chart for each. **What the people who
decide whether to release actually read is much shorter: can we ship, and what is the risk if we
do.** Everything else is evidence for those two answers, and belongs below them or behind a link.

## The numbers that do not answer the question

A count of cases measures effort, and a count of defects measures a mixture of the product's
quality and the testing's thoroughness that nobody can separate. The **pass rate** is the one most
often put at the top of a report, and boxoffice's two runs in lesson 18 show what it hides:

On 1.0, nine of the fourteen cases that ran passed: 64%. On 1.1, ten of seventeen passed: 59%. A
reader who sees only the rates concludes that 1.1 is worse. In fact 1.1 fixed two
defects, the six-ticket limit and the member discount for big orders, and broke one, the student
discount. The rate fell because three new cases joined the run, each written to check a defect
already found, and a new case written from a defect fails on the day it is added.

The rate cannot say any of that, and it cannot say which failures matter. A run where the only
failure is a misspelt message and a run where the only failure is the price both show one
failure. Leadership needs to know which of the two it is looking at.

## Who reads what

The same run has several readers, and each wants a different slice of it:

| reader | wants to know | reads |
|---|---|---|
| the developer | what failed, exactly, and how to see it happen | the failure messages, the defect report |
| the test lead | how far through the run we are, and what is blocked | results by status and by area |
| the product owner, the manager | can we release, and what happens if we do | one page |

Section 02 of this lesson is the first row's file and section 03 is the second row's report. This
section and section 04 are about the third, which is the one testers write least well, because it
asks them to leave most of their work out.

## What goes on the page

**The answer first.** Yes, no, or yes with conditions, in the first line. A manager who reads one
line has the decision; one who reads on has the reasons.

**The answer is checked against the plan.** Lesson 1's plan wrote down its exit criteria before
any case ran: every case for risks A to C run and passed, no open critical or major defect,
acceptance signed off. Saying whether each one holds turns "can we ship" from the tester's opinion
into a comparison everybody agreed to in advance.

**Risk in consequences, not in case ids.** "TC-10 failed" means nothing to the theatre's manager.
"A student buying two tickets for Hamlet pays R$ 160,00 instead of R$ 80,00, or R$ 144,00 if she
is a member" is a sentence the manager can weigh against the cost of a delay. Each risk names who
is hurt, how, and how often it could happen.

**What would change the answer.** Which defects have to be fixed for "no" to become "yes", and
what has to be run again afterwards. A report that says only no leaves the reader with nothing to
do.

**What was not tested.** Scope left out by the plan, and gaps found on the way. A reader who
assumes everything was tested makes a different decision from one who knows that the confirmation
link has no case at all.

**Then the numbers, as evidence**, with what they count written beside them: run, passed, failed,
blocked, open defects by severity, and what changed since the last release.

## The decision is not the tester's

A one-page report informs a decision; it does not take it. The theatre's manager may ship 1.1 with
a known defect because the season opens on Friday and she has decided a misspelt message is a
price worth paying. That is her call. **The tester's job is that the call is made with the risks
in front of her, in words she can weigh**, rather than discovered at the box office on opening
night. Section 04 writes that page for boxoffice 1.1.

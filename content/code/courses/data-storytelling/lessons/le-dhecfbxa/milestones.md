A plan for an analysis does not need software. **It needs five or six milestones, each with a date and
something you could show somebody on that date.** A milestone with nothing to show is a hope.

| milestone | date | what exists on that date |
|---|---|---|
| brief agreed | 23 June | the table of lesson 13, confirmed by Paulo |
| first cut | 4 July | one paragraph and one table, labelled rough |
| data and definitions | 11 July | the joined table and the definitions, checked by Diego |
| checks and money | 25 July | the regional check and the arithmetic of lesson 11 |
| ghost deck | 28 July | the titles of lesson 5, read by Paulo |
| previews | 7 to 13 August | the conversations of lesson 13 |
| decision meeting | 15 August | the deck, the page, the appendix |

Notice that the first cut comes **before** the data milestone is finished: Marina joined a rough version of
the tables in week two and showed the result while Diego was still checking the definitions. Milestones can
overlap; what matters is that each produces something visible.

## Somebody watches the dates

In a project of one, the analyst is also the project manager, and the job is simple: **on each milestone
date, check whether the thing exists, and if not, say so that day** to whoever is waiting for it. A slip
reported on the day it happens is a scheduling problem. The same slip discovered the week of the meeting is
a credibility problem.

## The plan is a communication tool

The milestone table is also the skeleton of the weekly update from lesson 13: done this week is the
milestone just passed, next week is the next one. **A stakeholder who can see the plan does not need to ask
how it is going**, and a manager who can see it can protect the dates when a new request arrives, as the
request log of lesson 13 does for priorities.

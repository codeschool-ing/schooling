The four principles of this lesson come from Robin Williams's *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, which
has been teaching them to people with no training in design since the 1990s. The first is contrast,
and her rule for it is short: **if two elements are not the same, make them very different**.

## Contrast is hierarchy

A slide has a reading order whether you design one or not. The eye goes first to whatever is biggest,
heaviest or most saturated, then to the next, and so on. **Contrast is how you decide that order** instead
of leaving it to chance.

On the left, the title, the chart labels and the footnote are all much the same size and weight, and the
two bars are two shades of the same colour. Nothing tells the eye where to start, so it wanders. On the right, the
title is much larger and heavier than anything else, the bar that carries the message is the only one
in colour, and the source note is small and grey. **The eye reads it in the order the argument needs**:
claim, evidence, source.

## Weak contrast is worse than none

The commonest failure is not the absence of contrast but contrast that is too timid: a title in 24 points
over body text in 20, a dark grey against a slightly darker grey. The difference is visible on close
inspection and invisible at a glance, so it **creates a doubt instead of an order**: is this meant to be
different or not? Williams's advice is to commit. If the title should stand out, make it twice the size,
not a little bigger.

## Contrast for legibility

Contrast has a second meaning that is not about hierarchy: **the difference in lightness between text and
the background behind it**. Pale grey text on white, or red on dark blue, can be hard to read for anybody
and impossible for some people. The accessibility guidelines most organisations follow, WCAG, set a
minimum contrast ratio of 4.5 to 1 for ordinary text and 3 to 1 for large text, and every slide tool can
be checked against them with a free contrast checker.

A projected slide loses contrast that a screen has: a meeting-room projector washes out dark colours and
the room is rarely dark. **Design for the worst room you will present in**, which means darker text,
larger type and fewer subtle shades than look necessary on your laptop.

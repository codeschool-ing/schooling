The four principles say how to arrange what is on a slide. **How much should be there depends on whether
the slide is going to be presented or read**, and confusing the two is behind most of the decks people
complain about.

## Two different documents

A **presented** slide is seen while somebody talks. The audience reads it in a few seconds and then
listens, so it carries a title sentence, one piece of evidence and very little else. If it holds a
paragraph, the room reads the paragraph instead of listening to you, and reads faster than you speak, so
they finish and wait.

A **read** deck is sent instead of a meeting, or before it, and nobody is there to explain it. It needs
full sentences, the definitions, the caveats. Garr Reynolds, in *Presentation Zen*, called the hybrid
that tries to be both a **slideument**: too dense to present and too fragmented to read.

## The rule for presented slides

Practical limits for a slide you will speak to:

- **A title of up to two lines**, as lesson 5 required.
- **Text large enough for the back of the room.** Guy Kawasaki's well-known rule of thumb is nothing
  smaller than 30 points; the exact number matters less than testing it, by looking at the slide from
  across a room or at a quarter of its size on your screen.
- **No paragraph** that you would then read aloud. Reading your slides to a room is the fastest way to
  lose it.
- **Speaker notes** for what you want to say, so the slide does not have to hold it.

## When the document wins

Some organisations decided that meetings work better with documents than with slides. The best-known is
Amazon, where Jeff Bezos told senior executives in 2004 to replace slide presentations with narrative
memos of a few pages, read in silence at the start of the meeting. The reasoning was that full sentences
force the writer to connect ideas that bullet points let them leave unconnected.

That is a useful test even if you will present: **write the argument as a page of prose first**. If the
page does not hold together, no slide design will make it. And if the meeting is one where people will
decide something complicated, consider sending that page with the deck, or instead of it. Lesson 8 is the
one-page version of exactly that.

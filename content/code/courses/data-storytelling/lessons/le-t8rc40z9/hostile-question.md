---
title: When the question is an attack
version: 1
---

Sooner or later a question will arrive with an edge: *So you're saying my team is failing?* **The edge is
information**, not an insult to be returned. It tells you the finding has touched something the person
cares about, usually their reputation or their team's.

## Answer the concern, not the tone

The worst response is to defend the analysis: *the numbers are what they are*. It is true, and it hears
the question as a challenge to the data when it was a question about blame. The better response
answers the concern underneath:

> No. Deliveries are on time 94.5% of the time, which is your team's number, and it's a good one. What I
> found is a step on first orders that costs a day, and your team is the one I'm proposing should change
> it.

That answer does three things: it says the person is not being blamed, it uses their own number
respectfully, and it gives them a role in the fix. It is lesson 2's context and lesson 4's management
version, said in ten seconds under pressure.

## Separate the person from the problem

Roger Fisher and William Ury, in *Getting to Yes*, put this as a principle of negotiation: **separate the
people from the problem**. Talk about the step, the data and the decision, never about whose fault it is.
"The check uses a day" is a statement about a process; "your team is slow" is a statement about people,
and it will be defended rather than examined.

## When to take it outside the room

Some objections are not about the analysis at all. They are about budget, status or a history between two
departments that a meeting cannot resolve. **Recognise them, name them politely and offer to continue
afterwards**: *that's a bigger question than this slide, and I'd like to talk it through with you after the
meeting.* Then return to the recommendation, so the room can still decide what it can decide today.

The best defence against a hostile question, though, is to have no surprises. If Sandra saw the finding a
week before the meeting, alone, and helped shape the recommendation, she will not attack it in public.
That is the subject of lesson 13.

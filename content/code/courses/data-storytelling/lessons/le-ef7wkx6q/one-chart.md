Once the title says the message, **the chart's only job is to show the evidence for that sentence**. The
`visualization` course taught which chart types exist and when each one lies. This section is about the
step before that: letting the message pick the comparison, and the comparison pick the chart.

## The message names a comparison

Every assertion title compares something with something. Find the comparison and the chart follows:

| message | comparison | chart |
|---|---|---|
| Late first boxes more than double cancellations | two groups, one number each | two bars |
| The gap holds in the capital and in the interior | two groups inside two regions | grouped bars |
| First deliveries are a sliver of the total | part against whole | one stacked bar |
| The first-delivery rate has not improved all year | one number over time | a line |

A chart that answers a different comparison from the title's is the commonest mismatch, and it is hard
to see because the chart is correct. **It shows something true; it just does not show the sentence above
it.**

Marina's first deck had a line chart of the cancellation rate by month, one line for each region and
first-delivery status: four lines over six months. It is accurate and it took real work. Under the title
*Late first deliveries more than double cancellations*, it forces the reader to compare two pairs of
wiggly lines, mentally average each one, and divide. The two bars on the right show the same claim in one
glance, because they **draw the comparison the title makes and nothing else**.

The monthly lines are not useless. They answer a different question, *is the gap stable over time?*, and
the answer is yes: every month, both regions, the late line sits well above the on-time one. That is a
good appendix slide, ready for somebody who asks whether the gap is a one-month accident.

## Fewer marks, not fewer facts

A chart for a message has **as few marks as the comparison needs**. Two bars, not twenty-four. Two lines,
not four, unless the four are the point. Every extra mark is something the eye has to process and decide
to ignore, and the room's attention is the scarce thing from lesson 1. The data you leave out of the chart
is not hidden; it is one appendix slide away.

A chart can show the right comparison and still make the reader hunt for it. **Emphasis is the decision
about what the eye sees first**, and on a slide built around one message, the answer is the mark that
carries it.

## Grey first, then one colour

The most reliable technique is also the simplest: **draw everything in a neutral grey, then colour the one
thing the title is about**. In the regional chart, all four bars start grey; the two late bars get the
colour, because the title says late deliveries cancel more in both regions. The eye goes to the colour
before the title has finished being read.

Two rules keep it honest. Use the colour for one idea only, so it means the same thing on every slide of
the deck (lesson 6 calls this repetition). And **never let colour be the only thing that says which bar is
which**: a label on each bar, or a pattern, keeps the chart readable for somebody who does not see the
difference between your two colours, which is the subject of lesson 14 of the `visualization`
course.

## Label the data, drop the legend

A legend asks the reader to look away from the data, find a colour in a box, remember it and look back.
**Label the bars or the lines directly**, at their ends, and the legend disappears. Where there are only
two series, a label next to each is shorter than any legend, and it cannot be misread.

## Say the point on the chart

An annotation puts the message where the eye already is: a short line of text beside the two late bars
saying *more than double, in both regions*, or an arrow to the month where something changed. It
repeats the title, and that is fine here: **the title is read once and the chart is looked at for the
rest of the minute**, so the point should be in both places.

## What to take away

Everything that is not data and not the point competes with both: heavy gridlines, borders, shading,
three-dimensional effects, a logo in the corner of every slide. The test for each is the one from lesson
2 for evidence. If nobody would miss it, it goes.

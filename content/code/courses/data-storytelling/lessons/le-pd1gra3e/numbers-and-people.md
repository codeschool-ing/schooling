---
title: Why a correct number is not enough
version: 1
---

It is tempting to believe that a clear enough number will speak for itself, and that people who ignore
it are being irrational. **People do not decide on numbers alone, and that is not a defect you can
present your way around.** They decide on numbers they trust, that they understand, and that connect to
something they are responsible for.

## Trust comes before the number

A room weighs the messenger before the message. Marina was three months into the job and presenting a
finding about logistics to the head of logistics. Whatever her slides said, the first question in the
room was whether she understood how deliveries work. **A number from somebody the audience does not yet
trust is examined for errors, not for meaning.** Lesson 13 deals with building that trust before the
meeting rather than in it.

## A number needs a scale the listener already has

"1,058 late first deliveries in six months" is a precise number with no scale attached. Is that a lot?
Compared with what? The same fact becomes legible when it is placed against something the listener
already holds: *one new subscriber in six gets their first box late*, or *more than double the
cancellations*. Neither is less precise than the original; each gives the number a ruler.

## Cases and statistics

There is a well-known finding about how people respond to a single case and to a statistic. In a 2007
study, Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic asked people to donate to a charity. Those shown
the photograph and story of one girl gave more than those shown statistics about millions of people in
need, and **adding the statistics to the girl's story reduced what people gave** compared with the story
alone.

That study was about charity, not business meetings, and it should not be stretched into a rule that
stories beat data. What it supports is narrower and still useful: **a single concrete case gets
attention that an aggregate does not**. So the case belongs in a presentation as an illustration of the
number, not as a replacement for it. A customer whose first box arrived four days late, who wrote to
cancel two weeks later, makes the 41.5% real to a room. The 41.5% is what makes the customer more than an
anecdote.

The danger runs both ways. A vivid case that is not typical persuades the room of something false. Pick
the case after the number, and pick one that the number describes.

## Relevance: whose problem is it?

Finally, people attend to what they are accountable for. The cancellation rate was the marketing
director's number, and she was not in the meeting. The late deliveries belonged to logistics, whose own
measure said everything was fine. **A finding that crosses two departments has no natural owner in the
room**, and lesson 4 is about saying it in the terms of whoever has to act.

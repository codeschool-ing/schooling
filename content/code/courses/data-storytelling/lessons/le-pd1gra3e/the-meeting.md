Faro sells pet food by subscription in the state of São Paulo: a bag of food every month, delivered to
the door. Marina, its data analyst, was asked a fair question in March: **why do so many new subscribers
leave in their first three months?**

She spent three weeks on it. She joined the orders to the deliveries and the deliveries to the
cancellations, checked the joins, and found something real: customers whose first box arrived late
cancelled within ninety days far more often than customers whose first box arrived on time.

Then she presented it. The operations meeting had forty minutes and six items on the agenda, and her
item got eleven of them. She showed thirty-eight slides. Most were tables like the one on the left
below: every cohort, every region, every count, accurate to the unit.

The room was polite. The director of operations asked whether the numbers included the interior. The
head of logistics said that deliveries were on time 94% of the time, which everybody already knew. The
meeting moved on to the next item, and **nothing was decided**.

## What went wrong

Not the analysis. Every number in those tables was correct, and lesson 10 checks the most dangerous
objection to it and finds that it holds.

What went wrong is that **the slides handed the room raw material and asked it to do the analysis on the
spot**. To find the finding in the table on the left, a director has to compare the cancellation counts
against the subscriber counts, row by row, do the division in their head, and notice that the late rows
are consistently worse. Nobody does that in eleven minutes while thinking about the next item.

The slide on the right carries the same evidence and does that work in advance. Its title is the
finding, written as a sentence. Its chart shows the one comparison the finding rests on. A director who
reads only the title has the point; one who looks at the chart can see why.

## The three things a room is short of

A meeting is rarely short of data. It is short of three other things, and the rest of this course is
organised around them:

- **Attention.** Six items in forty minutes. Whatever needs decoding loses to whatever does not.
- **Meaning.** A number is not yet a reason to do anything. The audience needs to know what it implies
  for something they are responsible for.
- **A request.** Marina never said what she wanted the room to do, so the room did the default: nothing.

The next section gives those three a structure you can check a presentation against.

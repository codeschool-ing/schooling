The three questions of this lesson, with the ones the earlier lessons added, make a short checklist. **Run it
on every number before it goes on a slide**, your own first. It takes a few minutes, and most of its
questions have answers you already know.

| question | what it catches | Faro |
|---|---|---|
| **what exactly is counted?** | a definition that means something else | late by whose date? |
| **who produced it, and how?** | a measurement that does not measure the thing | the driver's scan, at the door or later |
| **out of how many?** | a count with no denominator | 1,058 of 6,113 |
| **compared with what?** | a number with no scale | 41.5% against 17.4% |
| **who is in the group, and who is not?** | a self-selected or survivor sample | 304 of 1,320 answered the survey |
| **who benefits if it says this?** | a measure moved without the reality | Ligeiro's bonus, the three-day promise |
| **what else could explain it?** | a confounder | region, checked in lesson 10 |
| **what would change my mind?** | your own bias | the gap vanishing inside each region |

## Using it without becoming the person nobody invites

A checklist applied aloud to every number a colleague presents makes enemies quickly. Use it in three ways
instead:

- **On your own work, always**, before anybody sees it.
- **On a colleague's work, privately**, as questions rather than objections: "how is late defined here?"
  helps them before the meeting; the same question in the meeting embarrasses them.
- **In the meeting, only for the one question that would change the decision.** If the answer to "out of
  how many?" would not change what the room decides, it can wait.

## Write yours

Run the checklist on the central number of your own analysis and write the answers in `my-analysis.txt`.
**Any row you cannot answer is either a gap to close before presenting or a caveat to state** when you do.

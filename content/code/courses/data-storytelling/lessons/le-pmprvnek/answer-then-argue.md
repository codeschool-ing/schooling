Lesson 2 said the recommendation comes last. This lesson says the conclusion comes first. **Both are true,
because they are about different things**: one is the order of the argument, the other is what the room
knows while the argument is being made.

## The shape that combines them

A presentation built this way has five moves, and only the first is new:

1. **The answer, in one sentence.** "Late first deliveries more than double early cancellations; I'll ask
   for an eight-week pilot to fix them."
2. **Context.** "Deliveries are on time 94.5% of the time."
3. **Conflict.** "But first deliveries, the ones that decide whether a customer stays, are on time only
   82.7%."
4. **Evidence.** The gap, the check by region, the size.
5. **Recommendation.** The pilot in full: what, who, how much, when. Then the decision.

The first sentence is a promise and the rest keeps it. The room listens to the context knowing where it
leads, judges each piece of evidence against a claim it already knows, and **is not surprised by the
request, because it was told about it in the first minute**. A request that arrives as a surprise is a
request people need time to think about, and time to think is how decisions get postponed to next month.

## The military version: BLUF

Military writing has a name for the same rule: **BLUF, bottom line up front**. A memo opens with the
conclusion and the action required, and the rest of it is the justification. It is taught in the US
armed forces for exactly the reason newspapers adopted the inverted pyramid: the reader is busy, may be
interrupted, and must be able to act on the first paragraph alone.

BLUF is useful as a check rather than as a style. **Read the first two sentences of anything you are about
to send or present, and ask whether someone who read nothing else could act on them.** If not, the bottom
line is not up front yet.

## When to hold the answer back

There is one honest exception. When the conclusion will be rejected on sight, because it contradicts what
the room believes strongly, stating it first can close the room before the evidence has a chance. Then
the order changes slightly: **state the question first** ("why do so many new customers leave?"), walk
through the context and conflict, and let the evidence bring the room to the answer.

Use this sparingly and knowingly. Faro's finding contradicts Sandra's sense that deliveries are fine, but
it does not contradict her number, which is why Marina can still lead with it. Most findings are like
that: uncomfortable, not unbelievable. Lesson 14 deals with the rooms where the number itself is the
threat.

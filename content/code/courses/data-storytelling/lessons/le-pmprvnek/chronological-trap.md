course, because the order of the work is the order you remember.**

Marina's first deck followed her three weeks exactly. Slide 2 described the order table, slide 3 the
delivery table and slide 4 the join. Slides 5 to 12 explored cancellations by month, by region, by pet
and by day of the week. Slide 14, two-thirds of the way through, showed for the first time that late
first boxes and cancellations went together, and the recommendation was a sentence on slide 37.

## Why it feels right

The chronological order has a real virtue: it shows that the work was careful. **The analyst is
answering a question nobody asked out loud, "can I trust you?"**, by walking the room through every step.
And it postpones the moment of being challenged, because a finding nobody has heard yet cannot be
argued with.

Both motives are understandable and both backfire. The room does not follow eight slides of exploration
waiting to learn why it is being shown them, and by the time the finding arrives, the people best placed
to challenge it are tired, distracted or gone. Trust comes from the evidence being sound and from your
answers to questions about it, both of which happen after the finding, not before.

## What to do with the journey

The journey is not worthless; it is in the wrong place. Three homes for it:

- **The appendix**, as slides nobody is shown unless they ask.
- **A methods note**, a page sent with the deck for the people who will check the work (lesson 4 calls
  them the technical audience).
- **The answer to "how did you get there?"**, given in a sentence when somebody asks, with the appendix
  slide ready.

One part of the journey does belong up front: **a dead end the room is likely to think of**. If everybody
will wonder whether region explains it, saying early that you checked, and that it does not, saves the
question. That is evidence, though, not chronology, and it goes where lesson 2 put it.

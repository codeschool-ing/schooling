The inverted pyramid matters most where it is used least: **in writing that is read by people deciding
whether to read it**. An email, a chat message to a manager, the summary at the top of a report. Most of
the analysis anybody in a company sees reaches them that way rather than in a meeting.

## The subject line is the top of the pyramid

Compare three subject lines for the same email:

| subject line | what the reader knows without opening it |
|---|---|
| First-delivery analysis | that an analysis exists |
| Interesting finding on churn | that you think it is interesting |
| Decision by Friday: 8-week pilot to cut late first deliveries | the finding, the request and the deadline |

The third is longer and it is the only one that **can be acted on from the inbox**. Paulo, scanning forty
emails, knows from it that something needs his decision this week and roughly what. A subject line has
room for about one clause, so give it the request and the deadline; the finding can wait for the first
sentence.

## The first sentence carries the rest

The first sentence of the email is the governing thought from the previous section, written for this
reader:

> Customers whose first box arrives late cancel within ninety days at 41.5%, against 17.4% for the rest,
> and I'd like your approval by Friday for an eight-week pilot in the interior to fix it.

The second sentence says what the approval involves (Sandra's team, no new cost beyond her team's time).
Then the evidence, in three short paragraphs, then a link to the deck. **Somebody who stops after two
sentences can still reply "approved"**, and that is the test.

## The headline test for anything you write

Before sending any analysis, cover everything but the title or subject and the first sentence, and ask
two questions. *Does a reader who sees only this know what I found?* *Do they know what I want from
them?* If either answer is no, rewrite the top before touching the rest. The same test applies to a slide
title, which lesson 5 turns into a rule.

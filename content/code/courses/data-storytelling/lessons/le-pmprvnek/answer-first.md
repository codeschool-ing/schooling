Newspapers learnt to write for readers who stop early. A news story opens with the most important fact,
follows it with the details that support it, and leaves the background for the end, where a reader who
has had enough can leave without missing anything that mattered. **The shape is called the inverted
pyramid**: widest at the top, where the weight of the news is.

It arose for practical reasons as well as editorial ones. An editor who needed to cut a story to fit the
page cut from the bottom, and a story written in this shape survived the cut. A reader scanning the page
read the first paragraph of everything and the whole of almost nothing.

## The meeting is a reader who stops early

A presentation has the same reader. Paulo has forty minutes and six items. His attention is highest when
your item starts and falls from there, and **he may be called out of the room, or the meeting may run
over and cut your last five minutes**. A finding that arrives at minute nine of eleven is a finding that
some of the people who matter never hear.

So the first thing said is the answer:

> Late first deliveries more than double early cancellations, and I'm going to ask for an eight-week
> pilot to fix them.

Everything after that sentence supports it. If the meeting ends a minute later, the room still has the
finding and the request.

## What goes at the top

The top of the pyramid is **the one sentence you would keep if you could keep only one**. For an
analysis, that is the finding together with what you want done about it. Not the topic ("today I'll talk
about first deliveries"), which tells the room what is coming without telling it anything, and not a
teaser ("we found something surprising"), which spends the room's best minute on suspense.

Below it come the reasons, most important first. At the bottom is what only some people will want: the
method, the caveats, the cuts that did not matter. In a deck, the bottom of the pyramid is the appendix
from lesson 2.

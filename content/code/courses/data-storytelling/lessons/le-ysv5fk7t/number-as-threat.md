---
title: When the number itself is the threat
version: 1
---

Some findings do more than report a problem: **they change what counts as success**. Faro's did. Before it,
the logistics team was judged by the overall on-time rate, and 94.5% was good. After it, the number that
matters is the first-delivery rate, and by that measure the same team was at 82.7%. Nothing about the work
changed; the ruler did.

## Why a new measure is harder than a new finding

A finding can be argued with and survived. **A new measure is permanent**: it will be on the dashboard every
Monday, and a team that was green becomes amber without doing anything differently. That is the strongest
kind of threat a number can carry, and lesson 3 warned that it is the one case where leading with the
conclusion can close the room.

## Three things that help

- **Keep the old number beside the new one, for a while.** For the first months, Faro's dashboard showed
  both: overall on-time 94.5%, first deliveries 82.7%. The old measure was not wrong; it was incomplete.
  Showing both says so, and lets the team see that their old achievement is still counted.
- **Let the team own the new measure.** Diego, Sandra's analyst, rebuilt the first-delivery rate from his own
  data and became its owner. **A number your own analyst computes is a number you defend rather than
  attack.**
- **Agree the target together.** The 95% target of lesson 7 was proposed by Sandra, as the level her team
  already reached for renewals. A target somebody chose is a goal; a target imposed from outside is a
  judgement.

## What not to do

Do not present the new measure as a revelation of failure: *"we discovered that logistics was only at 82.7%
all along"*. It is accurate, it will be remembered, and it guarantees that every future number from you is
received as an accusation. **The frame that works is the true one: a measure that could not see something,
and a new one that can**, which is exactly how lesson 2 framed the conflict.

A **stakeholder** is anybody who can affect your analysis or is affected by what it finds. The list is
always longer than the people in the meeting, and **the ones left off it are where the trouble comes
from**: the marketing director who owns the cancellation rate and was not in Marina's first meeting, the
carrier whose drivers make the scans.

## Mapping them

A simple and widely used way to sort stakeholders is the **power and interest grid**, associated with
the management researcher Aubrey Mendelow. Each person is placed by two questions: *how
much can they affect the outcome?* and *how much do they care about it?*

The four corners call for four different treatments:

- **High power, high interest: manage closely.** Paulo, who decides, and Sandra, whose team changes. Involve
  them early and often, and never let them hear something first in a meeting.
- **High power, low interest: keep satisfied.** The board, and Renata until the money is in question. Brief
  them on outcomes, in their unit, without asking for their time on details.
- **Low power, high interest: keep informed.** Diego, and the warehouse staff who will run the new process.
  They will not decide, and they will carry it out, so tell them what is happening and why.
- **Low power, low interest: monitor.** Most other departments. Check occasionally that it stays that way.

## The map moves

Positions change as the work goes on. Renata's interest rose sharply when the analysis turned into R$ 790
thousand a year. **Ligeiro moved from the bottom left to the middle when the pilot started needing its
help**. Redraw the map at each stage of the work, not once at the start.

## Draw yours

Place the people around your own analysis on the grid in `my-analysis.txt`, one line each: name, power,
interest, and what they need from you. **If one of the top-right names has not yet heard about the
finding, that is your next conversation.**

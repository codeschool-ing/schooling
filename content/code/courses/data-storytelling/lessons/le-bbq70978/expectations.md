---
title: Agree the question, the deliverable and the date
version: 1
---

Most disappointment with an analysis comes from **expectations nobody wrote down**. The person who asked
had one deliverable in mind and the analyst produced another, both reasonable, and the difference only
appears on the day of delivery.

## Marina's first brief

In March, Paulo asked: *why do so many new subscribers leave in their first three months?* Marina said yes
and started. Nobody said what the answer was for, who would read it, by when, or what "done" looked like.
Three weeks later she produced thirty-eight slides for a meeting Paulo had imagined as a ten-minute
update.

## A brief, written back

The fix costs fifteen minutes. **Write the request back to the person who made it, as a short brief, and
ask them to confirm it**:

| field | Faro, as Marina should have written it |
|---|---|
| **question** | why do new subscribers cancel within ninety days, and what can operations change? |
| **decision it informs** | whether to change anything in the first-delivery process this quarter |
| **audience** | Paulo first; then the operations meeting; possibly the board |
| **deliverable** | a one-page summary and a deck of about ten slides, with an appendix |
| **date** | first findings in two weeks; final for the operations meeting of 15 August |
| **done when** | Paulo can decide whether to run a pilot, and on what |
| **out of scope** | pricing and marketing campaigns; they belong to other teams |

The table looks bureaucratic and saves weeks. **It turns a question into an agreement**, and an agreement
can be renegotiated in the open when the work reveals something new, which it always does.

## When the question changes

Halfway through, Marina found that the answer pointed at a step inside the warehouse, which made Sandra a
stakeholder nobody had named. That is normal; analysis finds things. **The rule is to update the brief and
tell the requester the same week**, not to absorb the change silently and let the delivery surprise them.

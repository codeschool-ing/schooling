The single rule that does most for an analyst's relationships is **no surprises**. Nobody important should
learn something about your work for the first time in a meeting, from a forwarded email or from their own
boss.

## The weekly update

A short written update, sent on the same day each week to the people in the top half of the grid, keeps
everybody where they need to be. Four parts, a few lines each:

- **Done this week.** "Joined deliveries to cancellations; checked the join with Diego."
- **Early findings, marked as early.** "Late first boxes look strongly linked to early cancellations. Not
  checked by region yet; don't quote it."
- **Next week.** "Regional check; first draft of the money."
- **Needed from you.** "Sandra: twenty minutes to walk me through the address check."

The second line is the one that does the work. **An early finding shared as early lets people get used to
it** before it is final, and lets them correct it while correcting is cheap.

## Pre-wiring the meeting

Before Marina's second presentation, she met each key person alone:

1. **Sandra**, first and in private, because the finding was about her warehouse. Marina showed her the
   numbers, asked how the address check worked, and asked whether her team would want to own the pilot.
2. **Diego**, to check every number and every definition.
3. **Renata**, with the arithmetic of lesson 11, so the money was hers before it was on a slide.
4. **Paulo**, with the one-page summary and the request.

The practice has a Japanese name, **nemawashi**, from preparing a tree's roots before moving it: consensus
is built in small conversations so that the formal meeting confirms rather than debates. **None of it is
manipulation.** Every conversation was an honest preview, and two of them changed the recommendation: Sandra
asked for the pilot to run in the interior first, and Renata asked for the range.

## How often is enough

Too little communication produces surprises; too much produces unread email. **Match the frequency to the
grid**: weekly for the top right, at milestones for the top left, a note when something changes for the
bottom right. The test is whether any of them could be surprised by what is in your next presentation.

An analyst who is any good receives more requests than there is time for. **Every yes is a no to something
else**, and the question is whether that no is decided by somebody on purpose or by whichever request
shouted loudest this week.

## Value and effort

A rough way to sort requests is two questions, the same shape as the grid in the first section:

- **What decision does it inform, and how much is riding on it?** A request tied to a decision worth R$ 790
  thousand a year outranks one that will be read and filed.
- **How much work is it?** A two-hour answer to a valuable question is the first thing to do. A three-week
  answer to a question nobody will act on is the last.

Requests that are valuable and cheap go first. Requests that are neither go nowhere, politely. **The hard
ones are valuable and expensive**, and those are where the next rule matters.

## Who decides

The analyst should not be the one who decides that marketing's request waits for operations'. **The
person who sets priorities across teams, usually the analyst's manager, decides**, and the analyst's job is
to make the trade-off visible: "I can do the cohort analysis for marketing or finish the first-delivery
work by the 15th, not both. Which comes first?" That sentence turns a conflict between two departments into
a decision for the person whose job it is.

## Saying "not now"

Most refusals are delays, and they are easier to hear when they come with something:

- **a date**: "I can start it on the 18th";
- **a smaller version**: "I can give you the overall rate this week and the breakdown next month";
- **a way to do it yourself**: "the dashboard already has that, here is the filter".

## A request log

Keep a list, visible to whoever sets your priorities: who asked, what for, when they need it, how big it
is, and its status. **A visible list is the strongest argument an analyst has** when a new request
arrives, because it shows exactly what would move to make room.

Evidence is where most analysts are strongest and most presentations go wrong, because **the analyst
has far more evidence than the story needs**. The job is to choose.

## One claim, three supports

Start from the claim the conflict sets up. For Faro: *late first deliveries are costing us customers*.
Then ask what a sceptical person in the room would need to believe it, and supply exactly that.

1. **That the gap is real.** Customers with a late first box cancelled within ninety days at 41.5%,
   against 17.4% for those whose box was on time. One chart, two bars: the slide from lesson 1.
2. **That it is not something else.** The obvious alternative is region: the interior has more late
   deliveries and might churn more for other reasons. The gap holds inside each region (lesson 10 shows
   how), so the delay, not the address, is the better explanation.
3. **That it is big enough to matter.** 1,058 late first deliveries in six months, and lesson 11 turns the
   extra cancellations into the margin Faro loses each year.

Each piece answers one question a reasonable person would ask, and **anything that answers no question
somebody would ask is not evidence for this story**, however interesting it is. Marina's analysis also
showed that cancellations peak on Mondays and that cat owners churn slightly less than dog owners. Both
are true and neither is in the presentation.

## Where the rest goes

What you leave out is not wasted. It goes to an **appendix**: slides after the last one, not presented,
ready for the question that needs them. A good appendix lets you answer "did you look at the interior?"
with a slide instead of a promise. Lesson 10 is about preparing it.

## Strength over volume

Three strong pieces of evidence persuade more than ten mixed ones, because **a room judges the evidence by
its weakest piece**. If one of ten charts has a flaw, the discussion moves to that chart and stays there.
Leave out the evidence you would have to defend more than it defends you.

This is also why the evidence comes *after* the conflict and not before. Presented first, the same three
facts are a list. Presented after the room has heard that the on-time rate hides the first box, each one
answers a question they are already asking.

A story told with data has **four parts, and they come in this order: context, conflict, evidence,
recommendation**. The names vary between authors and the skeleton does not.

- **Context** is the situation the audience already accepts. Nothing in it should be news.
- **Conflict** is what disturbs that situation: a gap between what people expect and what is happening.
  It is the reason to listen.
- **Evidence** is the smallest set of facts that makes the conflict believable and its size clear.
- **Recommendation** is what should happen next, and who should do it. It resolves the conflict.

## Where the structure comes from

Two traditions arrive at the same shape. Fiction has long been described as setup, conflict and
resolution, and every scriptwriting manual teaches some version of it. Business writing has Barbara
Minto's **situation, complication, question, answer**, from her work at McKinsey in the 1960s and her
book *The Pyramid Principle*: state the situation the reader recognises, introduce the complication
that changes it, let the reader's question form, and answer it.

This course uses context, conflict, evidence and recommendation because the four words say what a
presenter actually has to supply. Minto's *question* is usually left unspoken, and her *answer*
contains both the recommendation and the evidence behind it; here they are separated because they fail
separately.

## Why the order matters

Each part prepares the next. **Without context, the conflict has nothing to disturb**: "first deliveries
are 82.7% on time" means little until the room has been reminded that deliveries are supposed to be on
time. Without the conflict, the evidence is a list of facts nobody asked for. And a recommendation that
arrives before the evidence is a demand.

The proportions are uneven on purpose. In an eleven-minute slot, context takes one minute, the conflict
one or two, the evidence most of the rest, and the recommendation the last two, with time left for the
decision itself. Lesson 3 moves the recommendation to the front without breaking this order, which sounds
like a contradiction and is not.

## Testing a draft against it

Write one sentence for each part before you open a slide tool. If you cannot write the conflict in one
sentence, you do not yet know why anybody should listen; if you cannot write the recommendation, you
are about to present a report. Faro's four sentences are the subject of the next four sections.

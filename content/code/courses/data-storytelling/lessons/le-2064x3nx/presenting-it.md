A case study is presented twice: **read, by somebody who opens your portfolio, and told, by you, in an
interview**. The two use the same material and different shapes.

## The written version

The README with the summary on top, as the previous sections built it. Two checks before publishing it:

- **The headline test of lesson 3.** Cover everything except the title and the first two sentences. Does a
  reader know what you found and what you recommended?
- **The checklist of lesson 12**, run on your own central number. A reviewer who finds an undefined term or a
  missing denominator will wonder what else is missing.

## The spoken version: five minutes

In an interview, "tell me about a project" is an invitation to five minutes, not twenty. Use the structure
of lessons 2 and 3:

1. **The answer, in one sentence.** *I found that late first deliveries more than doubled early
   cancellations at a subscription company, and the pilot I recommended cut them by more than two-thirds in a region.*
2. **Context and conflict.** *Their on-time rate was 94.5%, so nobody was looking at first deliveries, which
   were 82.7%.*
3. **Evidence.** The gap, the regional check, the money, one sentence each.
4. **Recommendation and result.** The pilot, its criterion, what happened.
5. **What you learnt.** The failed first meeting, and what you changed.

Then stop, and let them ask. **The questions are the part of the interview where they learn most about you**,
and lesson 10's four moves work there as well as in a meeting.

## Rehearse it aloud

Say the five minutes aloud, timed, at least three times before the first interview. Written sentences that
read well are often too long to say, and the first time you hear yourself explain the regional check should
not be in front of a hiring manager. Record it if you can, and listen for the sentence where you lose your
own thread: **that sentence is the one the interviewer will remember**.

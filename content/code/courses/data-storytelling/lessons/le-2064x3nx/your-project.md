You have been carrying an analysis of your own since lesson 1. **This is where it becomes a case study**, or
where you decide it is not the right one and choose another while there is still time.

## Three tests

- **Is there a decision?** Somebody, real or plausible, who would act differently depending on the answer.
  An analysis with no decision behind it is an exploration, and explorations make weak case studies.
- **Can you show the data?** Public data, your own data, or work data your employer has cleared. If none,
  see the previous section.
- **Can you finish it in four to six weeks?** A finished small case study beats an unfinished ambitious one,
  which the `portfolio-project` course argues at length in its lesson 2.

## Your track

::: track bi
For the BI track, the strongest case studies pair a dashboard with a decision. Take the analysis you
started in lesson 1 and make sure it ends in a recommendation, not a dashboard tour: the dashboard is the
monitoring tool of lesson 7, and the case study is the argument for what to do with what it shows.
:::

::: track data-science
For the data-science track, the strongest case studies put the model in service of a decision. The
accuracy of your classifier is one supporting fact; the case study is about what a business would do with
its predictions, what that is worth, and how you would test it, as lessons 9 and 11 did for Faro.
:::

::: track *
Whatever your background, the strongest case studies answer a question somebody in a business would ask.
A public dataset from your city, your country's statistics office or an open-data portal, with a question
about it that someone could act on, is enough.
:::

## This case study and the next course's project

The `portfolio-project` course comes after this one in the BI and data-science tracks, and it asks for a finished project with a
repository, a README and a short presentation. **Build one project, not two.** The case study you write here
is the analytical heart of it; `portfolio-project` turns it into a finished public piece, with the README of
its lesson 16, the screenshots and demo of its lesson 17, and the two-minute presentation of its lesson 19.
What this lesson adds that the other cannot is the story: the question, the decision and the
recommendation that make an analysis worth a reviewer's five minutes.

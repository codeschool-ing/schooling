## The seven parts

1. **The question and the decision.** One paragraph: who asked, what they needed to decide, and by when.
2. **The data and its definitions.** What you had, how much, what each key field means, and what was
   missing. A table like lesson 4's.
3. **The approach.** The hypotheses you listed (lesson 15's tree) and how you tested them, including the
   ones you set aside.
4. **The finding, with its evidence.** The assertion and the two or three supports, with the charts that
   show them (lessons 2 and 5).
5. **The recommendation, and what happened.** The decision you asked for and its result, or, if you do not
   know the result, what would test it (lesson 9).
6. **The limits.** What the analysis cannot show, said plainly (lesson 12).
7. **What you would do next, and what you learnt.** One paragraph, including what you would do differently.

## The summary goes on top

Above the seven parts goes the one-page summary of lesson 8. **Most reviewers will read only that page**,
exactly as most of Marina's readers did, so it has to stand alone: finding, impact, next step. The seven
parts are for the reviewer who is interested enough to keep reading, and the link to the files is for the
one who wants to check your numbers.

## Length

Five to eight pages, with the charts, is plenty. A case study that runs to thirty pages has the same problem
as Marina's first deck: it hands the reader the analysis instead of the story of it. **Cut until every
paragraph answers a question the reviewer would ask.**

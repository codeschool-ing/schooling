The `visualization` course designs dashboards from the chart outwards, in its lessons 18 and 19. This
lesson designs one from the reader inwards, and the reader it has in mind is the busiest one: **somebody
who opens it between two meetings and gives it thirty seconds**.

## A dashboard is for monitoring

Stephen Few, whose *Information Dashboard Design* is still the standard reference, defined a dashboard as
a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives,
consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be **monitored at a glance**. Each part
of that definition is a constraint: the most important information, not all of it; one screen, not a
scrolling report; at a glance, not after study.

## The three questions

A reader at a glance asks three questions, in this order:

1. **Are we all right?** One or two numbers against their targets. If they are fine, the reader can stop
   here, and most weeks they should be able to.
2. **If not, where?** The breakdown that locates the problem: which region, which product, which step.
3. **What changed?** The trend: is this new, getting worse, or the same as it has been for months?

A dashboard designed around those questions puts the answer to the first one where the eye lands first,
the second beside or below it, and the third as small trend lines next to the numbers. **Anything that
answers none of the three is a candidate for removal**, however much work went into it.

## Faro's dashboard, before

Paulo's operations dashboard had fourteen tiles: orders today, orders this week, revenue, deliveries,
on-time rate, two gauges for warehouse capacity, a map of the state coloured by orders, a table of the
ten largest customers, and five more. **The overall on-time rate, 94.5%, sat in a gauge in the middle of
the second row**, coloured green, and the first-delivery rate was not on the dashboard at all.

In thirty seconds Paulo could learn that orders were up and deliveries were green. Both were true, and
the one problem that was costing Faro customers was invisible, because the dashboard had been built from
the data that was easy to get rather than from the questions he needed answered.

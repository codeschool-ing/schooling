A reader of a left-to-right language starts a screen near the top left and works across and down. That
is a habit, not a law, and it is strong enough to design for: **the top left of a dashboard is its most
valuable space, and it belongs to the answer to "are we all right?"**

## Faro's dashboard, after

The redesign has four zones, numbered in the order the eye reaches them:

1. **Top left, the two numbers that say whether things are fine**: first deliveries on time, and the
   ninety-day cancellation rate of the latest cohort, each against its target.
2. **Top right, where the problem is**: first-delivery on-time rate by region, as two bars, because the
   interior is where it is worst.
3. **Below, what changed**: the weekly first-delivery rate over six months, one line, with the target
   drawn across it.
4. **Bottom, everything else**: orders, revenue and the overall on-time rate, small, for the reader who
   wants them.

Fourteen tiles became seven elements, and **the number that was missing is now the first one read**.

## Overview first, details on demand

Ben Shneiderman, who studies how people use information displays, summarised good interactive design in
1996 in a phrase that is now quoted everywhere: **overview first, zoom and filter, then details on
demand**. On a dashboard, the overview is the top row, the zoom is a filter by region or week that the
reader can apply, and the details are one click away: a drill-down into the orders behind a number, never
on the first screen.

The mistake the phrase prevents is putting the details first, because they are what the analyst built.
A table of the ten largest customers is a detail. It belongs behind a click, if anywhere.

## Group what is read together

The principles of lesson 6 apply unchanged. **Numbers compared with each other sit next to each other**,
with the same scale and the same format. Related tiles share an edge and a margin; unrelated groups are
separated by space rather than by boxes. And a colour that means "below target" means it in every tile,
which is lesson 6's repetition at the scale of a screen.

An **observation** says what the data shows. A **recommendation** says what somebody should do about it.
Between them sits a step most analyses skip, and the gap is why a careful report so often ends with a
sentence nobody can act on.

## The three rungs

- **Observation.** *17.3% of first deliveries are late.* True, checkable, and inert: it does not say
  whether that matters or what to do.
- **Interpretation.** *Late first deliveries more than double early cancellations, and the delay comes
  from the manual address check.* Now the fact has a consequence and a cause, and the cause points at
  something that can be changed.
- **Recommendation.** *Remove the manual check for addresses the postcode lookup confirms, in the interior
  from 1 September, for eight weeks; Sandra's team owns it; success is late first deliveries at 8% or less;
  review on 27 October.* Now somebody can say yes.

Each rung needs the one below it. A recommendation without an interpretation is a guess, and an
interpretation without an observation is an opinion. **The failure this lesson is about is stopping on the
first rung**, and it has the same cause as stopping at *What?* in lesson 1: the first rung is the only one
that cannot be wrong.

## Recognising an observation in disguise

Some sentences look like recommendations and are observations with a verb attached:

- *"We should look into first deliveries."* Look into how, by whom, to decide what?
- *"Delivery performance needs to improve."* Whose, by how much, by when?
- *"Further analysis is recommended."* Lesson 2 already met this one.

The test is to imagine replying *approved*. If nobody would know what to do on Monday, **the sentence is an
observation wearing a recommendation's clothes**. The next section takes a recommendation apart to show
what a real one has to contain.

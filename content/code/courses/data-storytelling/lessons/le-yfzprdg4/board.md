title: The board: money, risk and one decision
version: 1

A board, or the executive committee that plays its part in a smaller company, meets rarely, covers a
great deal and decides at the level of the whole company. **It needs the finding in its own unit, the
size of what is at stake, and one decision it can make.**

## The unit is money, or risk to money

"Late first deliveries more than double cancellations" is a fact about operations. A board hears it as
interesting and asks the obvious question: *how much does it cost us?* So the board version starts
there:

> Late first deliveries are costing us about R$ 793 thousand a year in customer margin, and they can be
> fixed without new spending.

The number comes from lesson 11's arithmetic: each extra early cancellation loses the margin a customer
would have brought over their lifetime, and 510 customers a year cancel early who would not have if their
first box had been on time. **The board does not need that arithmetic on the slide.** It needs to know
the number exists, that finance agrees with it, and where to look if somebody asks.

## One level deep

A board presentation goes one level below the headline and stops. Faro's has three slides:

1. **The finding in money**, the sentence above.
2. **Why it was invisible**: deliveries are 94.5% on time overall, but first deliveries, 4.6% of the
   total, are 82.7%. One chart, the bar from lesson 2.
3. **The decision**: add the first-delivery on-time rate to the measures the board reviews each quarter,
   and endorse the pilot management has approved.

No cohort tables, no regional breakdown, no method. Those exist, in the appendix and in the technical
version, and the board trusts them to the extent that it trusts the people who checked them. **That
trust is the reason to have the technical team and finance review the numbers before the board sees
them**, a sequence lesson 13 returns to.

## What a board decides

Boards rarely approve a pilot; management does that. What a board can decide is **what the company
measures and what it treats as a priority**. Asking it to make the first-delivery rate a measure it
watches is the right request for this room: it is cheap, it is at board level, and it makes the problem
somebody's job permanently, after the pilot is forgotten.

A common error is to bring a board an operational decision because the analyst wants the highest
authority behind it. The board approves, management feels bypassed, and the change runs into exactly
the resistance lesson 14 describes.

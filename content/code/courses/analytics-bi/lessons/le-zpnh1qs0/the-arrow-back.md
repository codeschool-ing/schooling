title: The arrow that points back
version: 1

Every diagram of a data platform draws arrows in one direction. The shop's database, the payment
system and the website's events flow **into** a warehouse; the warehouse feeds the semantic layer;
the layer feeds dashboards. That direction is called ETL — extract, transform, load — or ELT, and it
is what `pipelines-etl` builds.

The arrows end at a screen, and that is where they stop being useful to most of the company. The
salesperson spends the day in the CRM, the support agent in the help desk, the marketing team in the
e-mail tool. None of them opens the dashboard, and none of them should have to: the number they need
is about one customer, at the moment they are dealing with that customer.

**Reverse ETL is the arrow drawn back**: take a number the warehouse computed — a customer's health,
lifetime value, segment — and write it into the operational tool, onto that customer's record, where
the person doing the work sees it without asking.

The name sounds like a new technology and describes an old job. Companies have always exported a
list from the database and imported it into the CRM, by hand, on the first Monday of the month. What
changed is that the job became continuous and automatic, with the same discipline as the arrows going
the other way: a definition of what is sent, a record of what was sent, and a way to tell when it
failed.

This lesson builds one by hand, small enough to read whole: a model of what the CRM should know, a
pretend CRM to receive it, and a sync that sends only what changed and remembers what it did. Lesson
8 then places the products that sell this job — Hightouch, Census, Segment — against what you built.

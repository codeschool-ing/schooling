The commonest way to build a dashboard is to open the tool, add every chart that seems relevant, and
arrange them until the page looks full. The result is a page that answers no question in particular,
which in practice means a page nobody opens after the second week.

**A dashboard is the answer to a short list of questions that a named person asks on a known
schedule.** Write the list before opening the tool. For Lantern's owner, on Monday mornings:

1. Is the month on track — against last month, and against the target?
2. Is anything unusual this week?
3. Where is the money coming from, and is that changing?

Three questions, one reader, once a week. Each chart that does not help answer one of them is a
candidate for removal, however good it looks. A fourth question — "how is the Black Friday cohort
doing?" — belongs to a different reader with a different rhythm, and lesson 9 gives it a page of its
own.

## Three kinds of dashboard

The reader's rhythm decides the kind:

| kind | reader | refreshed | what it carries |
|---|---|---|---|
| operational | the person running today's work | many times a day | a few live numbers and alerts; detail on demand |
| tactical | a manager deciding this month | daily or weekly | trends, comparisons, breakdowns by team or channel |
| strategic | leadership deciding this year | monthly | a handful of numbers against targets, with long history |

Lantern's Monday page is tactical. Mixing kinds is the second commonest failure: a strategic page
refreshed every five minutes invites somebody to react to noise, and an operational page with
eighteen months of history buries today under a year.

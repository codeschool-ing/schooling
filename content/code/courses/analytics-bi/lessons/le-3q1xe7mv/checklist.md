Every failure in this lesson was caught by a question, and none of them needed new data — only a second
look at the same table. Together they are a checklist for the moment between a query that ran and a
number that goes into a deck, a dashboard or a message:

| ask | catches |
|---|---|
| What does the length on this chart measure, from where? | a truncated scale |
| Is this change in points or in percent, and does the sentence say so? | percent against points |
| How many things is this rate counting? | a small denominator |
| Would the conclusion survive a different starting month? | a chosen window |
| Did the mix of what is being counted change? | Simpson's paradox |
| Who was removed from this list before I saw it? | survivorship |
| What else is different between the groups I am comparing? | confounding |
| Has the period finished? | the partial month of lesson 6 |
| Has a definition changed under the number? | the renamed event of lesson 8 |

The last two come from earlier lessons and belong on the list: an incomplete period and a changed
definition both produce numbers that are right about the data and wrong about the world.

## What a checklist cannot do

It cannot make the conclusion for you. Each question, answered, usually leaves *two* defensible numbers —
the total and the rate within each device, the year-on-year and the month-on-month — and the honest
report gives both, with a sentence saying what each answers. That sentence, and how to build an argument
around it for a particular audience, is the subject of `data-storytelling`; how to draw the two numbers
so the eye reads them correctly is `visualization`.

What this course can leave you with is the habit underneath both: **a number you have not tried to
break is not ready to be believed**, least of all by you.

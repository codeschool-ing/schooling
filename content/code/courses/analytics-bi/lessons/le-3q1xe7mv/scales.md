Lantern's net revenue for the five complete months of 2026:

```
lantern=# SELECT date_trunc('month', order_date)::date AS month, sum(net_revenue) AS net_revenue
lantern-# FROM semantic.orders
lantern-# WHERE order_date >= '2026-01-01' AND order_date < '2026-06-01'
lantern-# GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
   month    | net_revenue 
------------+-------------
 2026-01-01 |    87883.56
 2026-02-01 |    88361.90
 2026-03-01 |   117964.14
 2026-04-01 |   119821.78
 2026-05-01 |   140097.06
(5 rows)
```

From R$ 87,883.56 in January to R$ 140,097.06 in May: up 59% in four months, a strong rise. Now draw it
twice. On the left, bars that start at zero. On the right, the same bars on an axis that starts at
R$ 80,000, which is what many tools choose by default when every value is far from zero:

On the right, May's bar is more than seven times January's. Nothing on that chart is false: the axis is
labelled, the values are right. But a bar's length is read as its size, and **the length is now
measuring the distance from R$ 80,000, which is a number nobody asked about**.

The rule depends on the mark:

- **Bars and areas start at zero**, always, because their length or area is the value. If the
  differences are too small to see from zero, that is the finding: they are small.
- **Lines may start elsewhere**, because a line is read by its slope and position, not its length. A line
  of conversion between 6% and 8% drawn from zero is a flat line at the bottom of an empty chart. But
  then label the axis clearly and do not shade the area under the line, which turns it back into a bar.

The question that catches it: **what does the length on this chart measure, from where?**

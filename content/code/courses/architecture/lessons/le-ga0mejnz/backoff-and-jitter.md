title: Backoff and jitter
version: 1

The textbook fix for retries arriving together is **exponential backoff with jitter**. Backoff waits
before each retry, longer each time: 0.1 seconds, then 0.2, then 0.4. Jitter makes each wait random, so
callers that failed together do not retry together. Marc Brooker's analysis for AWS, in 2015, compared
the variants and found that **full jitter**, a random wait anywhere between zero and the backoff limit,
spreads the load best; it is what the client's `--backoff jitter` does.

**No better.** The failures, the calls per two seconds and the wasted answers are almost the same as
with plain retries. That is not because jitter is useless; it is because of what it does and does not
change. Jitter spreads retries in time, which helps when a spike of synchronised retries is the problem.
**It does not reduce how many retries there are.** Here the waits are fractions of a second, the overload
lasts much longer, and every request still makes four calls; spreading them over a second changes
nothing when the service is short of capacity for twenty.

Two further rules do reduce the number of retries, and both are standard:

- **A retry budget.** Allow retries only up to a share of the traffic, say 10% of calls in the last
  minute, and fail without retrying beyond that. Healthy times have few failures and the budget is never
  reached; in a storm, the budget caps the extra load at 10% instead of 300%. gRPC calls this retry
  throttling, and the AWS SDKs do the same with a token bucket.
- **Retry at one layer only.** If the browser, the gateway and the checkout each retry three times, a
  failing stock service receives 64 calls for one click. Pick the layer that knows whether the operation
  is safe to repeat, usually the one closest to the failing call, and make the others fail fast.

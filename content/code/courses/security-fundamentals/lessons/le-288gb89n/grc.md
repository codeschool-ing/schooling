**GRC** is the family that lessons 3 and 13 to 17 belong to. It is less visible than the SOC or the red
team, and in many organisations it is where security's decisions are actually made: what risks are
accepted, which controls are required, and how the organisation proves it to auditors, customers and
regulators.

### The work

| role | what it does | lessons |
|---|---|---|
| **risk analyst** | runs risk assessments, keeps the register, helps owners decide treatments | 2, 3 |
| **security policy and compliance** | writes policies, maps controls to requirements, prepares for audits | 13, 14, 16 |
| **auditor** | examines whether controls exist and work, internally or for a certification body | 13, 14 |
| **privacy and data protection** | handles the LGPD's duties, rights requests, incident communication; may be the encarregado | 17 |
| **third-party risk** | assesses suppliers' security before and during a contract | 13, 17 |

### A day

Much of GRC is conversation and documents: interviewing an area's manager about their risks, reading a
supplier's statement of applicability, writing the corrective action plan after an audit, explaining to an
owner what accepting a risk means. The skill being built is **translation**: turning a technical finding
into a business decision, and a requirement in a standard into something an engineer can implement.

### What it asks of you

Clear writing, patience with detail, and enough technical understanding to know when a control on paper
does not work in practice. That last part is what separates a good GRC professional from a checkbox
filler, and it is why a technical foundation like this course matters here too: an auditor who has run a
restore test knows which evidence to ask for.

GRC is also a common second career for people from IT, law, audit or finance. `threat-modeling` covers
risk quantification, control mapping across ISO 27001, the NIST CSF and SOC 2, and audit preparation,
which are the core of the work.

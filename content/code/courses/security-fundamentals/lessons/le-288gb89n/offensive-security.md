Offensive security is lesson 10's red team as a career: people paid to attack systems, with permission, so
that the organisation learns what a real attacker could do before one does.

### The work

| role | what it does |
|---|---|
| **penetration tester** | tests a defined scope, an application, a network, a cloud account, for a set period, and reports every weakness found with evidence and a recommendation |
| **red team operator** | runs longer, realistic campaigns against a whole organisation to test whether it detects and responds |
| **vulnerability researcher** | looks for new flaws in software, reports them to the vendor, and sometimes ends up with a CVE identifier from lesson 2 |

### A day

The glamorous part, finding the way in, is a small share of the time. A penetration test is mostly
methodical: agreeing the scope and rules (lesson 10), mapping what exists, checking each item carefully,
confirming that a finding is real and not a false positive, and then **writing the report**. The report is
the product the client pays for. A brilliant finding badly explained is a finding nobody fixes; a clear
report with evidence a developer can reproduce and a recommendation they can act on is what makes the work
worth doing.

### What it asks of you

A deep knowledge of how systems work, because you cannot find where something breaks without knowing how
it is meant to work. Patience, because most checks find nothing. Clear writing. And, without exception,
**respect for the rules of engagement**. The techniques are the same ones a criminal uses; the written
authorisation, the agreed scope and the honest report are what make it a profession. Testing outside scope,
even to be helpful, ends careers and can be a crime.

The first job is usually a junior tester working under a senior one, often after time in a SOC, in
development or in system administration. `pentest` teaches the profession, and it starts with the rules,
the authorisation and the legal limits in Brazil before any technique, in that order on purpose.

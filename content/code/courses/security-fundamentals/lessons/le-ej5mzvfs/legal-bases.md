title: The legal bases
version: 1
---

The LGPD's central rule is that personal data may be processed **only on one of the grounds the law
lists**. Article 7 lists **ten legal bases**:

| | legal basis | in short |
|---|---|---|
| I | **consent** | the person agreed, freely, informed and unambiguously, for a specific purpose |
| II | **legal or regulatory obligation** | a law requires the processing |
| III | **public policy** | the public administration carrying out public policies |
| IV | **research** | studies by a research body, anonymising where possible |
| V | **contract** | needed to perform a contract with the person, or the steps before one |
| VI | **exercise of rights** | in judicial, administrative or arbitration proceedings |
| VII | **protection of life** | of the person or of a third party |
| VIII | **health protection** | by health professionals, services or authorities |
| IX | **legitimate interest** | of the controller or a third party, unless the person's rights prevail |
| X | **credit protection** | as the specific legislation provides |

**Consent is one basis among ten, not the default.** A common mistake is to ask for consent for
everything. Consent can be withdrawn at any time, so a process built on it stops the moment the person
says so. Where another basis fits, it is usually more honest and more stable.

### The shop's processing, basis by basis

| processing | basis | why that one |
|---|---|---|
| name, address and email to deliver an order | **contract** (V) | without them the shop cannot deliver what was bought |
| keeping invoices for years | **legal obligation** (II) | tax law requires it, which is also why lesson 12's missing invoices mattered |
| checking that an order is not a fraud | **legitimate interest** (IX) | the shop's interest, balanced against a light intrusion, documented |
| sending the newsletter with new titles | **consent** (I) | nothing else justifies it, and the customer may say no |

The newsletter row is the clear case for consent: the shop wants to send it, the customer does not need it
to receive their books, and nothing in the law obliges it. So the customer is asked, separately from the
purchase, and can stop it with one click.

**Legitimate interest** needs care. It is not a blank cheque; the controller must weigh its interest
against the person's expectations and rights, and record that assessment. Using a customer's address to
prevent fraud on their own order passes easily. Selling their reading history to advertisers does not,
which is lesson 1's privacy example again.

### Sensitive data has its own list

For sensitive personal data, **article 11** gives a narrower set of bases. Legitimate interest and contract
are not among them as such, and consent, where used, must be specific and highlighted. That is why the
shop's policy is not to collect any.

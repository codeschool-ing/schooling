---
title: Compliance is not security
version: 1
---

**Compliance** is meeting a requirement somebody else has set, and being able to show that you meet
it. The requirement might be a law, a regulator's rule, a clause in a contract or a standard the
organisation chose to follow. **Security** is actually being protected against the risks of lessons 2
and 3. The two overlap a great deal, and they are not the same thing.

They come apart in both directions:

| | compliant | not compliant |
|---|---|---|
| **secure** | the goal | good protection nobody can prove: no records, no policy, nothing written down |
| **not secure** | the requirements are met on paper, and the risk that matters is not covered | the worst case |

The bottom-left box is the one that surprises people. A requirement is written in advance, for many
organisations at once, and it cannot know the shop's own risks. An organisation can tick every box
on a checklist and still be breached through something the checklist never asked about, and many
large breaches have happened at companies that had passed their audits that year. Treating the audit
as the goal produces **checkbox security**: the controls exist because a box asked for them, are built
to satisfy the auditor on the day, and protect nothing in between.

The top-right box is the other half, and it is common in small organisations. ana does a lot of good
work at the shop; until this course, almost none of it was written down. When an auditor, a customer
or the ANPD asks "how do you protect personal data?", **unproven protection is indistinguishable from
none.**

### Why compliance still matters

Compliance is not the enemy of security. It does three things security alone cannot:

- it sets a **floor**: the minimum that every organisation of a kind must do, so that the careless
  cannot simply skip it;
- it makes security **visible to outsiders**: a customer choosing a supplier cannot inspect its
  servers, and can read a certificate or an audit report;
- it forces the **paperwork** that this course keeps returning to: the written risk acceptance of
  lesson 3, the access reviews of lesson 6, the restore log of lesson 12. Those records are valuable
  to the shop itself, not only to the auditor.

The healthy attitude is to build security for the shop's real risks, and to arrange it so that the
evidence of it falls out as a side effect. An organisation that does that passes its audits without
preparing for them. One that builds for the audit has to prepare every time, and stays exposed between
audits.

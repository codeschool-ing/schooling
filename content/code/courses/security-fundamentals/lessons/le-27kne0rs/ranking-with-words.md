Lesson 2 ended with a formula, **risk = likelihood × impact**, and a warning that neither factor
comes from a table. The cheapest way to use the formula anyway is to stop pretending to know
numbers and use **ordered words**: a scale for each factor, agreed in advance, with a description
that two people would apply the same way.

A five-point scale is the common choice. What matters is that each step is defined, so "likely"
means the same thing to the person who runs finance and to the person who runs IT:

| score | likelihood | in words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | rare | not expected in the next ten years |
| 2 | unlikely | could happen once in the next five to ten years |
| 3 | possible | could happen once in the next two to five years |
| 4 | likely | expected about once a year |
| 5 | almost certain | expected several times a year |

| score | impact | at the shop |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | negligible | an afternoon of somebody's time |
| 2 | minor | a day of lost sales, no data involved |
| 3 | moderate | a week of disruption, or a small leak with no sensitive data |
| 4 | major | personal data of many customers leaked, a report to the ANPD |
| 5 | severe | the shop cannot trade for a month, or does not survive |

Multiply the two scores and every risk lands somewhere between 1 and 25. Drawn as a grid, the
result is the familiar **risk matrix** or heat map:

The shop's five risks from the workshop where ana and the owners of each area sat together:

| risk | likelihood | impact | score |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 a criminal logs in to the portal with the default password | 4 likely | 4 major | **16** |
| R2 ransomware encrypts the server and its only backup | 2 unlikely | 5 severe | **10** |
| R3 a laptop is lost or stolen | 3 possible | 3 moderate | **9** |
| R4 the website is down for a day after a failed update | 3 possible | 2 minor | **6** |
| R5 the shop is flooded | 1 rare | 4 major | **4** |

The order R1, R2, R3, R4, R5 is the answer to "what first?". It is not precise, and it does not
need to be. **A qualitative matrix is good at ranking and bad at deciding how much to spend.** It
cannot say whether a R$ 2,000 control is worth buying for R3, because "9" is not an amount of
money. That needs the next section.

Two weaknesses to keep in mind. The scores are judgements, so two workshops can rank the same
risk differently; writing the reason beside each score is what makes it reviewable. And the grid
hides the difference between a 5 × 2 and a 2 × 5: both score 10, and a frequent nuisance is not
the same problem as a rare catastrophe. R2 is a rare catastrophe, which is why the shop will not
treat it like R4.

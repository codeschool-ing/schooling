title: The record, whatever is decided
version: 1
---

The RCIS requires the controller to keep a **record of every security incident involving personal data**, communicated
or not, for **at least five years**. It is the document the ANPD can ask for, and the one that shows a decision not to
communicate was made on purpose. For Thursday, almost all of it already exists:

| the record holds | already written in |
|---|---|
| the date of the incident, and when the company knew | lesson 12's declaration, 09:12 on 17 September |
| a general description of what happened | lesson 20's executive summary |
| the personal data and the people affected | the previous sections of this lesson |
| the risk assessment, and its reasoning | the RCIS criteria table, with the decision |
| the measures taken | lessons 13 and 14, and lesson 15's action list |
| the communications made, with their dates, or the reasons for not making them | the ANPD form's receipt; the letters to the five contacts |

The left column is a summary of what the RCIS asks for; the lawyer checks the exact list. The right column is the
point of this whole course, seen from the end: **a response that was recorded as it happened produces its legal record
almost for free.** One reconstructed from memory a month later produces a document nobody can defend.

That is also where the course ends, with the same idea it started with in lesson 1: logs, timelines, hashes, custody
records, decision logs, postmortems and reports are all the same thing in different clothes. **Something happened,
somebody wrote it down at the time, and anybody can check it.** Everything a SOC does rests on that, and everything it
owes to the people whose data it protects does too.

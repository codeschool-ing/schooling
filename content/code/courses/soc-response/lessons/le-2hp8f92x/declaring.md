---
title: Declaring an incident
version: 1
---

**Declaring** is the moment somebody with the plan's authority says "this is an incident". It is a
decision, written down with its time, and it switches the organisation into a different mode:

| what starts | why it matters |
|---|---|
| **an incident record** with an identifier, such as `INC-2026-014` | every note, decision and piece of evidence from now on carries it |
| **the incident lead** named, and the team called (lesson 11) | somebody owns it, and everybody knows who |
| **the decision log** | what lesson 20's report and any later question are answered from |
| **preservation**: nothing about the affected systems is deleted, rebuilt or rotated away | evidence that is overwritten during the response is gone for good |
| **the notification clocks** | some deadlines run from when the organisation *knew*; lesson 21 shows the LGPD's |
| **a separate channel** for the response, outside systems that may be compromised | the response itself should not be readable by the intruder |

For Thursday, the record opens at **09:12 on 17 September**, when ana escalates and the managing partner
agrees. That time matters more than it looks: if the company later reports to the ANPD, the question "when
did you know?" is answered from this record, and an organisation that cannot answer it precisely has
already lost an argument.

Declare early. **Downgrading an incident to "not an incident" costs a few hours of a team's time; declaring
late costs evidence, deadlines and trust.** The plan should make declaring cheap, and say who may do it.

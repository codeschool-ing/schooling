The **executive summary** is written last and read first, and for most readers it is the whole report. It is half a
page at most, and it carries the four links in miniature, in plain language, with the uncertainty intact:

> On the night of 17 September 2026, an outside party guessed the password of a member of staff's account and used
> it to reach two of the company's servers. About 612 MB of data was sent from the file server to an outside address.
> We have not been able to establish which files were sent; the file server held client documents, including an
> export of contact details for five client contacts. The intrusion was detected within two minutes, but the alert
> was not seen until the morning; it was contained at 09:45 the same day, and all systems were back to normal service
> by 2 October. It was possible because the remote access server accepted passwords from the internet, alerts did not
> reach anyone at night, and the file server could send data anywhere. All three have been changed or are scheduled,
> with owners and dates in section 6. The DPO and our lawyer are assessing whether clients and the ANPD must be
> notified.

Read it against the chain. **Facts**: the night, the guessed password, two servers, 612 MB. **Impact**, with its
uncertainty: which files is not established, and what was on the server is said. **Cause**: three conditions, no
names. **Recommendations**: changed or scheduled, with a pointer to where. And the one open obligation, notification,
handed explicitly to the people who decide it.

The rest of the report is the evidence for that paragraph: a section per link, the timeline from lesson 12, the
intervals from lesson 15, and appendices with the queries, the hashes and the custody records from lessons 16 to 18.
**Every sentence in the summary should be traceable to a section, and every section to its sources.** A reader who
checks one claim and finds it supported will trust the rest; one who finds a claim with nothing behind it will trust
none of it.

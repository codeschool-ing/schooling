---
title: Five sources, and what each one cannot see
version: 1
---

The design of a monitoring programme starts with a list of sources, and the list has five entries more
often than any other. They differ in what they describe, and **each one is blind to something the next one
sees**.

| source | what it records | what it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| **host event logs** | what happened on one machine: logins, processes started, services installed. The Windows Event Log, `auth.log` and the journal on Linux | anything that did not touch that machine |
| **syslog** | not a source but the road most Linux and network logs travel on: a format and a protocol for sending a line to a collector | only what the sender chose to send |
| **firewall logs** | the connections a firewall allowed or dropped, with addresses and ports | who was typing, and what was said |
| **flow records** | one summary per conversation: who talked to whom, when, for how long, how many packets and bytes. NetFlow and IPFIX are the standard formats | the content of the conversation |
| **packet capture** | every byte that crossed a wire, as it crossed | anything encrypted, and anything older than the disk can hold |

Syslog sits in that list because it is how the others usually arrive. **RFC 5424** defines the message: a
priority, a time stamp, the host name, the program, and the text. A router, a firewall and a Linux
server can all send syslog to one collector, which is what makes the collector the first piece of a
SIEM. Lesson 3 ships logs that way, and lesson 4 reads them.

The table also hides an economic order. A firewall line for one connection costs a few hundred bytes; a
flow record about the same; a packet capture of the same connection costs everything that was sent. That
is why packet capture is the one source most organisations do **not** keep running everywhere. They
capture at a few points, for a few days, or only when an investigation asks for it, and lean on flow
records for the rest. The last section of this lesson puts real numbers on that.

The figure is the lab you are about to build, with each source drawn where it sits. That placement is
itself a decision: a sensor sees only the traffic that passes it, so a flow exporter on the internet side
of `fw` says nothing about two machines talking inside.

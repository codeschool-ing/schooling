Most traffic today is **encrypted**, with TLS, and an intruder sending data out has every reason to encrypt it.
A capture of encrypted traffic is still evidence; it just answers fewer questions:

What survives encryption is the **shape of the conversation**: which addresses, which ports, when, for how long,
and how many bytes each way. That is exactly what flow records hold, which is why lesson 12 could reason about
Thursday without a single packet. TLS also sends the **server name** in the clear at the start of the connection,
the SNI, unless both sides use the newer Encrypted Client Hello, which is still uncommon; tshark shows it with the
field `tls.handshake.extensions_server_name`.

Getting the content of encrypted traffic needs the keys. For traffic between the company's own systems, a **TLS
inspection** proxy at the edge, which decrypts and re-encrypts, is one way, with legal and privacy questions of its
own; a server that logs its session keys is another. For a connection the intruder opened to their own server,
neither exists, and the content is out of reach. The analysis then works with sizes and times: the files the
account could read, from lesson 17's image, set against the 612 MB and the minutes the transfer took. What that
comparison supports is what lesson 20's report can state, with its uncertainty said out loud.

**A capture is evidence like an image.** It gets a hash when the capture stops, a line in the custody record, and a
working copy for analysis. And it holds content, sometimes personal data, so who may read it is decided as
carefully as for the disk.

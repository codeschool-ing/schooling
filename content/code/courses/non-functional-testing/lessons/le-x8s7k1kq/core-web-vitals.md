Lessons 2 to 9 timed the server: a request leaves the load generator, the box office answers,
and the clock stops. That number is real, and it is not what a person waits for. **A person waits
for a page**, and between the first byte of the HTML and a page they can read and use, the browser
downloads pictures and scripts, runs the scripts, lays the page out and paints it. On a phone over
a mobile network that part is usually most of the wait. A server that answers in 20 ms can sit
behind a page that takes ten seconds to show anything useful, and no load test will notice.

Front-end performance measures that second part. It needs different numbers, because "how long
did the response take" has no single answer for a page that arrives in pieces.

## Three numbers for three complaints

Google's **Core Web Vitals** are three metrics, each standing for one thing people complain about,
with published thresholds:

| metric | the complaint | good | poor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Largest Contentful Paint** (LCP) | "it takes ages to load" | at most 2.5 s | over 4 s |
| **Interaction to Next Paint** (INP) | "I tapped and nothing happened" | at most 200 ms | over 500 ms |
| **Cumulative Layout Shift** (CLS) | "it moved just as I tapped" | at most 0.1 | over 0.25 |

Between good and poor is a band called *needs improvement*.

**LCP** is the time from the start of the navigation until the largest picture or block of text
in the visible part of the page has been painted. It stands for "the main content is there",
which is a better proxy for what a reader waits for than the first pixel or the `load` event.

**INP** watches every click, tap and key press during the whole visit and measures how long each
one took until the next frame was painted: the browser has to finish whatever it was doing, run
the handlers, and draw the result. The visit's INP is close to its slowest interaction. It
replaced First Input Delay as a Core Web Vital in March 2024, because FID measured only the
first interaction and only the wait before the handler started.

**CLS** adds up how far visible content moves without the reader having asked for it. Each shift
scores the fraction of the screen that moved times the distance it moved, as a fraction of the
screen, and CLS is the worst burst of shifts in the visit. A banner that pushes the page down by a
fifth of the screen scores about 0.2 on its own. It has no unit, which is why its thresholds look
nothing like the other two.

## The 75th percentile of real visits

The thresholds are not judged on one visit. **A page meets the Core Web Vitals when the 75th
percentile of its real visits is in the good band**, for all three, with phones and desktops
counted apart. Three visits in four have to be good, and the fourth is allowed to be the old phone
on a train. It is the same move lesson 8 made for response times: a percentile, because the mean
of a long tail describes nobody.

That is why lesson 1 wrote the box office's page requirement as "Largest Contentful Paint at most
2.5 s at the 75th percentile of real visits". It is a statement about what happens to customers,
and only measurements of customers can check it.

## Field and lab

There are two places these numbers come from, and they answer different questions.

**Field data** is measured in the browsers of real visitors. The public source is the **Chrome
User Experience Report** (CrUX): Chrome collects the three vitals from users who have agreed to
share usage statistics. Google aggregates them over a rolling 28 days per page and per origin and
publishes the result through PageSpeed Insights, an API and BigQuery. It covers only pages with
enough traffic, so a staging server, or the box office on your VM, has none. A site can collect its
own field data instead, with a few lines of JavaScript that read the same metrics from the browser
and send them home, which is called real user monitoring.

**Lab data** is one load of the page, in a browser somebody controls, on a device and a network
somebody chose. That is what Lighthouse produces. It is repeatable, it works before release, and it
says which file caused what. It is also one visit, by nobody in particular.

**The lab cannot measure INP, because in a lab run nobody taps anything.** Lighthouse reports
**Total Blocking Time** (TBT) in its place. A task that keeps the main thread busy for more than
50 ms is a long task, and while it runs the page cannot answer a tap. TBT adds up the part of every
long task beyond its first 50 ms, from the first paint until the page goes quiet. A page with a
high TBT will have a poor INP as soon as somebody touches it during those tasks; a page with a TBT
near zero can still have a poor INP if its click handlers are slow, which only the field shows.

Lighthouse also reports two lab metrics that are not vitals. **First Contentful Paint** (FCP) is
the first text or picture painted, which says when the screen stops being blank. **Speed Index**
says how quickly the visible area fills in, averaged over the whole load.

The working arrangement is to use both. A lab check runs before every release and catches the
regression while the change is still small; the field says whether customers are getting what the
requirement promised, and catches what no lab reproduced. Lesson 11 turns the lab check into a gate.

title: Asking for attention
version: 1
---

**A notification interrupts somebody who was doing something else.** It is the one thing in this course that acts
on a person who is not looking at the page, and the easiest to make a person regret allowing. A study planner is
a good case for it and a good case for restraint at once.

## What earns a notification

- **Something the person asked for**, at the time they asked for it: *remind me at nine before a session*. A
  reminder is the planner's one good reason, and it is the person's own setting, not the planner's.
- **Something that cannot wait for the next visit**: a session moved, an exam tomorrow.
- **Never something the site wants**: *come back*, *you have not studied today*, *new courses*. A person who allowed
  reminders and gets marketing turns notifications off, and for the site, a *no* is permanent.

## How often, and what it says

**One message that matters is worth more than ten that do not.** Group what can wait into one; use the same `tag`
for messages that replace each other, as the planner's `reminder` does, so a second reminder replaces the first
rather than piling up beside it. And say the thing itself in the notification, *Git, 30 minutes, today*, so the
person does not have to open the app to learn whether it mattered.

## Where it works

**Chrome, Edge and Firefox** show web notifications on desktop and Android, from a site or an installed app.
**Safari** shows them on macOS, and on iOS and iPadOS only from an app installed to the home screen, since iOS 16.4.
So on an iPhone the order of the lesson is the order of the person: install first, then allow, then receive.

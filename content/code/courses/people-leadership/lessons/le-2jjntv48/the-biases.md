title: The biases that survive good intentions
version: 1
---

Almost every interviewer believes they judge candidates fairly. **The research on bias in hiring is
mostly research on people who believed exactly that**, and found that their decisions still varied
with things that had nothing to do with the job. Good intentions do not reach these effects, because
they operate before intention gets involved. Process does.

## Five that matter in interviews

**The halo effect.** One strong impression spreads to everything else. A candidate who is articulate
in the first five minutes gets credit for technical depth they have not shown. The psychologist
Edward Thorndike named it in 1920, studying how military officers rated their soldiers.

**Similarity.** People rate others more highly when they resemble them: the same university, the same
previous company, the same hobbies, the same way of talking. It often hides under the phrase "culture
fit", which lesson 14 removed from Agenda's job description for exactly this reason.

**Confirmation.** An interviewer who forms an impression from the CV, or from the first answer, then
listens for evidence that confirms it and discounts evidence against it. A candidate from a
well-known company is asked easier follow-ups.

**Contrast.** A candidate interviewed after a weak one looks stronger than the same candidate after a
strong one. The scale has moved, not the candidate.

**Names and the signals around them.** In a 2004 study, Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan sent
fictitious CVs to job adverts in Boston and Chicago, identical except for the name at the top. CVs with
names perceived as white received about 50% more callbacks than the same CVs with names perceived as
Black. The people screening did not decide to discriminate. The names did it through them.

## Why trying harder does not fix it

The natural response is to resolve to be more careful. It helps less than people expect, because the
effects are not choices the interviewer can catch themselves making. **An interviewer cannot see their
own halo effect from inside it**, any more than somebody can see their own blind spot by looking
harder.

What does work is changing the conditions in which the judgement is made, so there is less room for
these effects to act:

| bias | what reduces it |
|---|---|
| halo | scoring each rubric row separately, with its own evidence, before an overall view |
| similarity | a rubric of behaviours the job needs, instead of an unstated sense of fit |
| confirmation | the same questions and follow-ups for every candidate, whatever the CV |
| contrast | scoring against the anchored scale, not against the previous candidate |
| names | removing names and photos from CVs during the first screen, where the tool allows it |

Every row of that table is one of the parts of structure from lesson 15, which is the point. **Structure
is the bias countermeasure**, and the reason it predicts better is partly that it removes these sources
of noise.

## A panel that is not all the same

One more measure is the panel itself. Four interviewers with the same background share the same
similarity bias, and it adds up rather than cancelling out. Caju's final round for the E2 role had two
engineers, a product manager and an engineering manager, of different genders and from different
regions of Brazil. That does not remove bias. It makes it less likely that every interviewer leans the
same way about the same candidate.

## Your task

Think of a decision you made about a person recently, at work or not: a hire, a choice of collaborator,
a judgement about a colleague. In your notebook, write which of the five biases could have played a
part, and what about the conditions of the decision would have reduced it. Be specific about the
conditions, not about trying harder.

---
title: The time it really takes, and the cost of hurrying
version: 1
---

New managers underestimate hiring in two ways. **They underestimate the calendar time, and they
underestimate the hours it takes from the team.** Both mistakes lead to the same bad decision: under
pressure to fill a seat, the bar drops, and lesson 21 is about what that can cost.

## Calendar time

Agenda's E2 role was opened in the first week of August. The offer was accepted in the second week of
October: nine weeks. The person who accepted, Lucas, was in a permanent job under Brazil's labour law,
the CLT, and owed his employer thirty days' notice. His first day at Caju was in mid-November, about
thirteen weeks after the role was opened.

Renata had told Helena in July that the new engineer would "help with the confirmations work this
quarter". By the time Lucas could contribute fully, after the onboarding in lesson 18, the quarter was
over. **The plan should have assumed a quarter to hire and a quarter to ramp up**, which is a
pessimistic-sounding estimate that turned out to be accurate.

## The team's hours

The hours came from people with other work to do. Using the funnel from the previous section, Renata
added up what the process cost in engineering time:

| stage | arithmetic | hours |
|---|---|---|
| Renata reviewing CVs that passed the recruiter | 60 CVs × 10 minutes | 10 |
| technical interviews | 15 × 1.5 hours, including the write-up | 22.5 |
| final interviews | 6 candidates × 4 interviewers × 1.5 hours | 36 |
| debriefs | 6 candidates × 5 people × 30 minutes | 15 |
| **total** | | **83.5** |

That is more than two working weeks of engineering time, spread across five people over nine weeks,
for one hire. It does not count the recruiter's time, which was larger. **Hiring is real work, and a
team that is hiring is a team that is shipping less**, which Renata now says out loud in planning
rather than discovering.

## The temptation to hurry

At week six, Helena asked whether the bar could be lowered a little, because the confirmations work
was late. One candidate in the final round had been strong in some interviews and weak in the one
about operating services in production, which was a must.

Renata did not hire him. Her reasoning, which she wrote down, was about asymmetry. A seat left empty
for another month costs a month of the confirmations work. **A hire who cannot do the job costs months
of the team's time in support, a difficult conversation or several, and possibly the process in lesson
21**, which is longer and harder than hiring. The empty seat is visible and the bad hire's cost is
not, until it is.

That does not mean a manager waits for a perfect candidate. It means the must list from the first
section is the bar, and it was written before the pressure arrived for exactly this reason: so that
a tired manager in week six is comparing a candidate against a document, not against the pain of an
empty seat.

## Your task

Estimate the hours your team would spend hiring one engineer, using the funnel you drew in the
previous section and the table here as a model. Then check:

- Did you include the debriefs and the write-ups, not only the interviews?
- How many weeks of one engineer's time is the total?
- If your plan for next quarter assumes the new person is productive, when would they need to have
  been hired?

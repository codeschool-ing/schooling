Hiring starts long before anybody applies. **The job description decides who applies, who does
not, and what every interviewer will later be looking for**, so a vague or inflated one damages the
whole process before it begins. Most are written by copying the last one and adding requirements.

## Agenda gets a headcount

After Renata's first two quarters, Otávio approved an eighth engineer for Agenda: a mid-level
backend engineer, E2 on the ladder from lesson 11, to take some of the reminders and calendar work
off Paula and Diego. The previous job description for a similar role at Caju was a page long and
listed fourteen requirements, from "5+ years of Python" to "experience with Kubernetes" to "passion
for healthcare".

Renata started again from a different question: **what will this person actually do in their first
six months?** She wrote the answer before writing any requirement:

> In your first six months you will take over day-to-day ownership of the reminder service from the
> engineer who built it, fix the time-zone bugs clinics report most, and build the next version of
> appointment confirmations with two other engineers. You will be on the on-call rota from your
> third month.

Everything else in the description follows from that paragraph.

## Must-haves, and the rest

A requirement belongs in the "must" list only if somebody without it could not do the work in that
paragraph, even with help. Renata's list came out short:

| must | why |
|---|---|
| has built and run a backend service in production | the job is owning one |
| writes tests as a matter of course | the reminder service is where clinics notice bugs first |
| can explain a technical trade-off to somebody who is not an engineer | Helena and the clinics will ask |

Then a separate list, labelled plainly as not required: experience with Python, with time zones,
with on-call. **Three musts, not fourteen**, because every extra must removes candidates who could
have done the job, and it removes them unevenly.

That last point has evidence behind it. An internal report at Hewlett-Packard, repeated in many
articles since, suggested that men apply when they meet some of the requirements and women when they
meet nearly all of them; the exact figures are disputed, but the effect of a long must list on who
applies is consistent with other findings. In 2011, Danielle Gaucher, Justin Friesen and Aaron Kay
showed that job adverts worded with masculine-coded language, words such as "dominant" and
"competitive", made the jobs less appealing to women, without changing how qualified they felt. **The words chosen
change the pool before anybody reads a CV.**

## What not to require

Some requirements recur in engineering adverts and predict little:

- **Years of a specific technology.** Five years of Python is not five times better than one; lesson
  11 argued the same about years in general.
- **A degree, when the job does not need one.** It filters out self-taught engineers and people from
  bootcamps without telling you whether they can do the work.
- **"Culture fit".** Unstated, it means "like us", which lesson 17 shows is how bias enters a decision.
  If there are values the team holds, write them as behaviours: "gives and asks for code review".

## The description is the scorecard

The must list is not only for candidates. It becomes the set of things interviewers assess, which
lesson 15 turns into a structured interview. **A requirement that is in the description and that
nobody assesses is decoration; one that is assessed and not in the description is a surprise to the
candidate.** Renata wrote both from the same paragraph so they could not drift apart.

## Your task

Write, in your notebook, the first-six-months paragraph for a role on a team you know, or for
Agenda's E2. Then write the must list. Check:

- Every must could be traced to a sentence in the paragraph.
- There are five musts or fewer.
- No must is a number of years.
- Every adjective describing the ideal candidate has been replaced by something they would do.

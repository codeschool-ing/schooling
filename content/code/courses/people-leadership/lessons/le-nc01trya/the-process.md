title: The process, and the parts that are not yours to improvise

Ending somebody's employment is the most consequential thing a manager does to another person at
work. **Almost all of the process belongs to people who are not the manager**: the people team, the
company's lawyers, payroll, and the law of the country. The manager's job is to make sure the
decision is sound, to follow the process exactly, and to handle the human part well.

## The decision, before the process

Lesson 20 ended with Renata, Sônia and Otávio deciding to end Marcos's employment. Before anything
else happened, Sônia asked three questions, the same three she asks in every case, and Renata had to
answer each with evidence rather than conviction:

- **Was the expectation clear, and when was it set?** The plan, signed and dated, and the written
  expectations from the conversation on 2 May.
- **Was the support given?** Renata's list: three pairing sessions, six check-ins, six summaries, the
  two on-call weeks off the rota.
- **Is this person being treated as others in the same situation would be?** Sônia checked it against
  the other cases she had seen in engineering that year.

**If any answer is weak, the decision waits.** That is not bureaucracy. A dismissal that cannot answer
those three questions is unfair to the person and, in many countries, a legal risk to the company.

## What the law and the company decide

In Brazil, ending a permanent employment contract without cause under the CLT has rules a manager does
not improvise. The employer owes notice, *aviso prévio*, of thirty days plus three days for each year of
service, up to ninety days in total, under Law 12.506 of 2011, and may pay it rather than have the
person work it. The employer also pays a penalty of 40% on the balance of the employee's FGTS fund, along
with other amounts the law sets out. Other countries have entirely different rules, and companies have
policies on top of the law.

None of this is in a manager's hands, and a manager should not try to explain it to the person in
detail. **The manager says what has been decided; the people team explains what it means in money,
dates and rights**, in writing, and answers the person's questions about it. Marcos had worked at Caju
for three years, and Sônia calculated his notice and payments; Renata did not need to know the figures,
and did not ask.

## Logistics that matter more than they seem

Sônia's checklist for the day had items that sound small and are not:

| item | why |
|---|---|
| the conversation early in the week, not on a Friday afternoon | the person can reach the people team with questions the next day |
| a private room, not a call from home, when possible | it is easier to have the conversation with dignity, and to end it |
| access to systems changed after the conversation, not during it | the person should not learn the news from a locked laptop |
| a written letter ready, with the terms | the person will remember little of what was said |
| a plan for what the team is told, and when | the subject of the last section |

**The order of access and conversation is a matter of dignity.** At some companies the person discovers
they have been dismissed because their badge stops working. At Caju, Marcos's access was changed after
he had left the room, by which time he knew.

## What the manager does not do

The manager does not decide alone, does not negotiate the terms, does not promise anything the people
team has not confirmed, and does not explain the law. All of those are where well-meant improvisation
creates problems the person pays for later. What the manager does is the conversation, which is the
next section.

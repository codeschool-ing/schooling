The conversation with the person is one half of letting somebody go. **The other half is the team,
who will notice an empty desk, a removed name, and a manager behaving differently, and who will fill
any silence with the worst explanation available**: that they could be next, that it was unfair, that
something is being hidden.

## What the team is told, and what it is not

Renata told the team the same day, in person, shortly after Marcos had left, and before any system
showed his account as removed. What she said was short:

> I want to let you know that Marcos's last day at Caju was today. He asked to say goodbye to you
> himself, and he'll send a message this afternoon. I know this will raise questions, and some of them I
> can't answer, because the details are his and not mine to share. What I can tell you is what changes
> for the team, which I'll go through now.

**What she did not say was why.** Not the plan, not the estimates, not anything from his one-to-ones.
The reasons for a dismissal belong to the person, and sharing them, even in a sympathetic tone,
publishes something private about somebody who is no longer there to respond. If people asked, and
two did, her answer was the same: "I'm not going to talk about the reasons, out of respect for him."

## What changes for the team

The practical questions are the ones people can be told about, and answering them quickly reduces the
anxiety:

| question | Renata's answer |
|---|---|
| who takes his work? | the reschedule tickets go to Lucas and Fábio; Renata will move two other pieces of work out of the quarter |
| who is on call in his weeks? | the rota is redone this afternoon, and nobody gets an extra holiday shift |
| will the role be filled? | she will ask Otávio this week and tell them the answer either way |

The last line is the promise list from lesson 7 again: a keepable promise about reporting back, not a
promise about the result.

## The question nobody asks aloud

When somebody is dismissed, the question in most people's heads is "am I next?" Some will ask it in
their next one-to-one, and some will not. Renata raised it herself, gently, in each one-to-one that week,
without referring to Marcos: "This week was a hard one for the team. Is there anything you're worried
about for yourself that you'd like to talk about?"

Two people took the opening. Thiago asked whether he should be worried about his on-call record from
months earlier, and Renata could say clearly that he should not, and why. **A team that has watched a
fair process, with clear feedback over months, can be told honestly that nobody is dismissed without
warning**, because they have seen what warning looks like.

## The manager, afterwards

Letting somebody go is hard on the manager too, and it is worth saying so. Renata spent the evening
going back over the decision, wondering whether she should have done something differently in the
first months, whether the plan had been too strict, whether she could have found him a different role.
She took those questions to Otávio the following week, and they went through them properly, which is the
use of a manager's own manager that lesson 24 describes.

The answer to most of them was that the process had been fair and the decision was sound. **The
discomfort did not go away, and it should not**: a manager who finds dismissals easy has lost
something they need.

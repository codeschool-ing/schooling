---
title: What you see is a symptom
version: 1
---

When somebody is not performing, a manager sees the results: missed estimates, slow responses, work
that comes back from review. **Those are symptoms, and the same symptom can come from causes that need
completely different responses.** A manager who goes straight from symptom to action is treating a
cough without knowing whether it is a cold or pneumonia.

## Marcos, again

Lesson 10 left Renata at the point of naming a pattern to Marcos: two weeks stuck without saying so,
estimates of three days that took more than two weeks, forty minutes on a page. She had said it plainly and written it
down. What she did not know was why.

The tempting conclusions were all available. That Marcos did not care. That he was not good enough for
the level. That he was distracted by something at home, which he had hinted at. Each would have led to
a different action: a firm conversation about effort, a plan to move him down a level, or patience and
time off. **Choosing among them without finding out would have been a guess dressed as a decision**,
and two of the three guesses would have been unfair to him if wrong.

## Why the symptom misleads

The same visible result can come from very different places. A missed estimate can mean:

- the person did not understand what was being asked;
- the work was harder than anybody realised;
- the person lacks a skill the work needs;
- the person is not putting in the effort;
- something outside work is taking their attention;
- something in the environment is in the way: a dependency, a broken tool, a teammate.

From the outside, all six look the same: the work was late. **Only one of them is about effort, and
only one is about ability**, yet those are the two conclusions managers jump to first, because they are
the ones that make the problem the person's.

## The order of operations

Lesson 10 said the first conversation names the pattern. This lesson is about what comes between that
conversation and any decision: a diagnosis. It has three parts, each the subject of a section:

1. **Look at the causes systematically**, rather than starting from the most obvious one. The next
   section gives a sequence of questions that does this.
2. **Ask the person**, in a conversation designed to learn rather than to confirm. The last section is
   about that conversation.
3. **Only then decide** what to do, which lesson 20 covers when the answer is an improvement plan.

This takes time, usually a few weeks. That can feel too slow when a team is suffering from the
problem. It is faster than the alternative: acting on the wrong cause, watching nothing improve, and
starting again three months later with the person's trust spent.

---
title: Where feedback ends and a performance conversation begins
version: 1
---

Most feedback is about a moment: a review comment, a meeting, a summary. **A performance
conversation is about a pattern that has continued after clear feedback, and that puts the
person's standing in their role in question.** Treating the second as if it were the first is the
commonest way a manager lets a problem run for a year, and then surprises somebody with it.

## Three tests

Renata uses three questions to decide whether she is still giving feedback or has crossed into
something else. A pattern that answers yes to all three needs a different conversation.

**Has it happened more than once?** One missed estimate is a moment. Missed estimates on four pieces
of work in a row, each by more than half, is a pattern.

**Has the person had clear feedback about it?** Clear means specific, in the shape lesson 8
described, said in a conversation, and written in the one-to-one notes. Hints do not count. If the
manager has only ever mentioned it in passing, the next step is clear feedback, not a performance
conversation.

**Does it affect what the role requires?** Some behaviours are irritating and do not touch the job.
Others are the job. An engineer whose pull requests repeatedly break the build, whose estimates
nobody can plan with, or who does not answer pages on call, is not meeting a requirement of the
role, and the pattern has consequences for the team.

## Marcos

Marcos, whose empty one-to-one section appeared in lesson 5, is the case this lesson follows. Over
two months Renata noticed three things: the notification-library upgrade from lesson 3, where he
had been stuck for two weeks without saying so; two pieces of work estimated at three days that took
over two weeks each; and a page during his on-call week that he acknowledged and did not act on for
forty minutes.

She had given him feedback about the first, in the shape of lesson 8, and written it down. The
second and third had happened since. All three tests now said yes: repeated, after clear feedback
on part of it, and touching what the role requires, because the team plans with estimates and
clinics depend on on-call.

**That does not mean she knew why.** Lesson 19 is about diagnosing underperformance before deciding
anything, and it is where Marcos's story continues. What changed here was the kind of conversation:
from feedback on separate moments to a conversation that named the pattern and said that it
mattered to his role.

## Why managers wait too long

The usual failure is not crossing the line too early. It is never crossing it. Managers keep giving
mild feedback about separate moments because the performance conversation is uncomfortable, and
because naming a pattern feels like an accusation. The person, hearing only occasional mild
comments, reasonably concludes that things are basically fine.

The cost lands on the person most of all. **By the time a manager finally says "this has been a
problem for a year", the person has lost a year in which they could have fixed it**, and they have
every right to ask why nobody said so. The rule from lesson 8, that nothing in a review should be a
surprise, applies here with more force than anywhere else.

## Why some managers cross it too fast

The opposite failure is rarer and does real damage when it happens: treating a bad month as a
pattern, or skipping clear feedback and going straight to "this is a performance concern". A person
who has never been told plainly what is wrong, and hears for the first time that their job may be at
risk, will not hear anything else in the conversation. **The second test exists for that reason.**
If you cannot point to the conversation where you gave clear feedback, have that conversation first.

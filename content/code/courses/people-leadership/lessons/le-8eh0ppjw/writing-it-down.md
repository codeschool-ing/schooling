A performance conversation that exists only in two people's memories will be remembered
differently by each of them within a week. **The written summary is what fixes it**, and it is
also the first piece of a record that may matter a great deal later, to both of them.

## The summary

Renata wrote hers the same afternoon and sent it to Marcos, with a line inviting corrections. It
was short:

> **Summary of our conversation on 22 April**
>
> We talked about three things from the last two months: the notification-library upgrade (stuck
> from 3 to 17 March before I knew), the waiting-list and reschedule tickets (each estimated at
> three days, each over two weeks), and the page on 14 April (acknowledged at 02:10, first action at
> 02:50). We had discussed the upgrade on 20 March.
>
> I said I'm concerned about the pattern, and that it touches what the role needs: estimates the
> team can plan with, and on-call response. You said you'd been finding it hard to ask for help, and
> that there's something outside work making this period difficult that you'd rather not go into.
>
> We agreed to meet again on Thursday 25 April. If anything here doesn't match how you remember it,
> tell me and I'll correct it.

Three properties matter. **It records facts, not conclusions**: dates, durations, what each person
said. **It includes what the person said**, in a form they would recognise, without the private
detail they chose not to share. **It invites correction**, and a correction, if one comes, is added
rather than argued with.

## What it is not

The summary is not a warning letter, a disciplinary notice or the start of a formal process.
Companies have those, they carry legal weight, and they are written with the people team, not by a
manager alone. Calling a summary a warning, or writing it in that tone, turns a conversation about
understanding a problem into a step in a dismissal, and the person will read it that way.

## When to bring in the people team

Caju has a people partner, Sônia, who supports the managers in engineering. Renata told her about
the conversation the same day, before sending the summary. She did not ask Sônia to do anything.
She wanted Sônia to know, for three reasons that apply in most companies:

- **The company's process may have rules the manager does not know**, about what has to be written,
  in what form, and when the person must be told what. Getting these wrong can make a later
  decision unfair, and in many countries unlawful.
- **The person may need support the manager cannot give.** Marcos had mentioned something outside
  work. Caju, like many companies, offers confidential help, and the people team knew how Marcos
  could reach it without Renata needing to know more.
- **If it becomes a formal process, it has to have been handled consistently from the start.** The
  people team sees every manager's cases, and can tell when one is being treated differently.

What Renata did not share with Sônia was the content of Marcos's one-to-one notes beyond what was
needed, for the reason lesson 5 gave: he had been told that what was written there stayed between
them unless she told him first. When it became relevant, she told him first.

## Your task

Write, in your notebook, the summary of a performance conversation for a case you invent or one you
remember, without real names. Then check:

- Every statement in it is something both people could check: a date, a duration, a quote.
- It includes what the person said, in words they would recognise.
- It leaves out anything private that the person chose not to explain.
- It ends with the next step and an invitation to correct it.

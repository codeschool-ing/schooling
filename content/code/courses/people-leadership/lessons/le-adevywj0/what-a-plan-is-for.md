---
title: What an improvement plan is for, and the dishonest version
version: 1
---

A performance improvement plan is a written agreement, for a fixed period, about a specific gap
between what somebody's role needs and what they are delivering: what has to change, how anybody will
know, what support they will get, and what happens if it does not change. **It has two honest
outcomes, and the person has to be told both: the gap closes, or the employment may end.** Every way
of writing one that hides either outcome is a version of dishonesty.

## Where Marcos was a month later

A month after the diagnostic conversation in lesson 19, Renata looked at what had changed. Some of it
had. Marcos had raised being stuck within a day on both occasions it happened, and the notification
library was documented. He was back on the on-call rota, and his two weeks there had gone well.

The estimates had not changed. Of four tickets in the month, two had taken more than twice the
estimate, and in neither had he revised the estimate or said anything until the work was already late.
The expectation they had written down together, a month earlier, had been specific about exactly this.

That combination is the situation an improvement plan is for: **a gap that has been named, diagnosed,
supported, and is still there**, in an area the role needs, where the causes outside the person's
control have been dealt with.

## The dishonest version

Improvement plans have a bad reputation, and much of it is earned. At many companies, a plan is
written after a decision to dismiss somebody has already been made, as a record that the company
"gave them a chance". Everybody involved knows the plan is not meant to be passed. The person usually
knows too.

That version harms everybody. The person spends weeks on a test designed to be failed, often while
being told it is a genuine opportunity. The manager has to pretend. And the next person put on a plan,
at that company, assumes it is the same thing, so the plans that are genuine lose their power.

**If a manager has already decided that somebody must leave, the honest course is to say so and go
through the company's process for ending employment**, which lesson 21 is about, rather than to stage a
plan. Renata asked herself the question directly before writing Marcos's: did she believe he could
close the gap in six weeks? She did, because the gap was a habit rather than an ability, and because
he had already changed one habit in a month. If the answer had been no, she would not have written one.

## What a plan is not for

Two more limits keep it honest.

**It is not for causes outside the person's control.** If the gap comes from unclear expectations, a
missing tool or an impossible workload, a plan puts the burden of the manager's problem on the person.
Lesson 19's sequence exists so that those are fixed first.

**It is not a punishment.** The tone of a plan, and of the conversations around it, should be the tone
of a clear, serious agreement, not of a warning shot. The person is still a member of the team, still
in their one-to-ones, still owed respect. That is easy to say and needs saying, because a plan changes
how everybody in a manager's head sees the person, and it shows.

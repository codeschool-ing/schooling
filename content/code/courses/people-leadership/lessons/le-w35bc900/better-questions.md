The fix for a question that gets "fine" is a question with edges. **It is about a specific
period, a specific kind of thing, or a specific event, so that the person has something to search
for instead of something to summarise.** Edges make a real answer cheaper to give than a polite
one.

## Five kinds that work

**Bounded in time.** "What was the most frustrating part of last week?" instead of "how are
things?". A week is short enough to remember and long enough to contain something. The word "most"
helps too: it assumes there was something, so answering "nothing" takes a little more effort than
answering.

**Scaled, with a follow-up.** "From one to ten, how sustainable does your workload feel?" The number
on its own says little, because people use the scale differently. The follow-up is the question:
"What would make it one point higher?" That asks for something concrete and small, which is easier
to name than a general complaint.

**About the team, not the person.** "What's annoying people on the team at the moment?" Some people
find it easier to report a problem as everybody's than as their own. The answer often turns out to
be their own, and that is fine: the question gave them a way in.

**About you.** "What's one thing I could do differently that would help you?" It shows that
feedback runs in both directions, and it is the hardest question for the person to answer
honestly, for the reasons in the previous section. Ask it anyway, regularly, and expect the honest
answers to start after a few months, not the first time.

**About the future.** "If you were still here in two years, what would you want to be doing?" It
opens the growth conversation that lesson 13 is about, without asking the person to declare a
career plan they may not have.

## A bank to draw from

Renata keeps a short list in her notebook and uses one or two per meeting, never a script. Some of
the list, by what each question is for:

| for | question |
|---|---|
| obstacles | "What's slowing you down that I might not know about?" |
| obstacles | "Is there a decision you're waiting on from anybody?" |
| how they are | "How's your energy been this week, compared with usual?" |
| how they are | "Is anything outside work making this month harder?" (then leave it with them) |
| the team | "Who on the team has helped you most recently?" |
| the team | "Is there anything in how we work that you'd quietly change?" |
| growth | "What's something you've done recently that you'd like to do more of?" |
| feedback | "What should I stop doing?" |

The "then leave it with them" is important. Some questions are offers, not requests. A person who
says "a bit, yes" and nothing else has answered.

## Questions that make it worse

Some questions sound open and close the conversation.

- **"Why" questions about the person's choices.** "Why did you take that approach?" is heard as an
  accusation even when it is curiosity. "Talk me through how you got there" asks for the same
  information without the charge.
- **Questions with the answer in them.** "You're fine with the on-call rota, right?" There is only
  one answer to that, and it is the one in the question.
- **Questions that are really instructions.** "Have you thought about pairing with Diego on it?"
  is a suggestion wearing a question mark, and the person knows it.
- **Several at once.** "How's on-call, and the calendar work, and are you OK with the new process?"
  The person answers the easiest one.

## How Renata asked Thiago the second time

When Renata came back to on-call with Thiago, she did not ask how it was going. She asked: "What
was the worst moment of your last on-call week?" He described the three o'clock page for an alert
he did not understand. She then asked, "From one to ten, how ready did you feel for it?" He said
three, and when she asked what would make it a four, he said a page in the runbook for that alert,
and somebody to call. **Both of those were things she could arrange within a week**, which is the
subject of lesson 7.

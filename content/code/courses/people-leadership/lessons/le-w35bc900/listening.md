title: Listening past the first answer

A good question gets a first answer. **The useful part is usually the second or third**, and
whether it arrives depends on what the manager does in the few seconds after the first. Most
managers do one of three things in those seconds, and all three end the conversation.

## Three ways to end it

**Fixing.** The person mentions a problem and the manager starts solving it. "Oh, the alerts? I'll
get Diego to write a runbook." It feels helpful, and it closes the topic before the person has
finished describing it. Thiago's problem was the alert, but also the sleep, and also not feeling
able to say so. A fix for the first part would have ended the conversation before the other two
came out.

**Explaining.** The manager offers the reason things are the way they are. "On-call is hard for
everybody at the start, it gets easier." It may be true, and it tells the person that the problem
was already known and not worth raising.

**Moving on.** The manager says "OK, good to know" and goes to the next topic. The person learns
that the answer was received and filed.

## What to do instead

Three habits keep the conversation open, and each is easy to describe and hard to do.

**Wait.** After the first answer, say nothing for a few seconds longer than feels comfortable.
People fill silence, and what they fill it with is often the thing they were not sure whether to
say. Renata counts to five in her head, which felt absurd the first week and now feels normal.

**Ask for more, without direction.** "Tell me more about that." "What else?" "What was that like?"
These carry no assumption about where the answer should go. They are better than a specific
follow-up at this stage, because a specific one points the person towards the manager's guess.

**Say it back.** Before responding, summarise what you heard in your own words. "So the alert itself
was confusing, and the worse part was not knowing who to call at that hour." It shows you listened,
and it gives the person the chance to correct you, which they often do: "Not exactly, it was more
that I didn't want to wake anybody."

## Noticing what is not said

Some of what a manager learns in a one-to-one is in what the person avoids. A topic they move
away from quickly, a colleague they never mention, a project they describe in more detail than
anything else, or less. None of this is evidence of anything, and treating it as evidence is
how managers become amateur psychologists. It is information to hold, sometimes to write in the
private notes from lesson 5, and occasionally to ask about directly and gently, weeks later.

## When the answer is hard to hear

Sometimes the second answer is about the manager. "Honestly, the way you ran planning last week
made me feel my estimate didn't count." The natural reaction is to explain, and the explanation is
usually reasonable. It still teaches the person not to say such things again.

The response that keeps the door open is short: thank them, ask one question to understand it, and
say what you will do with it, even if that is only thinking about it. "Thank you for telling me.
What did I do that gave you that impression? I'll think about how I run the next one." **The
explanation, if it is needed, can come in a week, once they have seen the thanks was real.**

## Your task

In your next real conversation where somebody tells you about a problem, at work or not, try only
the first habit: wait five seconds after their first answer before saying anything. In your
notebook, write down what they said in those five seconds, if anything. Do it three times before
deciding whether it works.

---
title: Why “fine” is a reasonable answer to a bad question
version: 1
---

Ask somebody who reports to you how things are going and the answer is almost always "fine".
**That answer is not a lie and not a failure of trust on the person's part. It is the correct
reply to the question that was asked**, and a manager who wants a different answer has to ask a
different question.

## Three reasons for "fine"

**The question is a greeting.** "How's it going?" is a phrase people exchange in corridors, and
"fine" is its standard reply. Asked at the start of a meeting, it is heard as the same ritual, and
answered the same way. Nobody has been asked anything yet.

**The question has no edges.** "How are things?" covers the person's whole working life and,
depending on how it is heard, their whole life. Faced with something that large, people do not
search it for the one thing worth raising. They summarise, and the summary of most weeks is
"fine". Vague questions get vague answers because a vague answer is a correct answer.

**The person is talking to their manager.** This is the reason that does not go away with better
wording. The manager writes the review, takes part in the promotion decision, and decides who
works on what. Telling them about a problem is a risk: it might reflect badly, it might be seen as
complaining, it might start something the person cannot stop. **The power is real even when the
manager is kind**, and pretending otherwise makes it worse, because the person can see it even if
the manager cannot.

## What the first answer hides

Renata asked Thiago how on-call was going in their third one-to-one. He said it was fine. Two
months later, in the ten-minute meeting from lesson 5, he told her that he had been sleeping badly
before every on-call week, that he had been paged at three in the morning twice for an alert he did
not understand. He had not said anything because everybody else seemed to manage.

Nothing in the first answer was false. On-call was functioning: he answered the pages, he fixed what
he could, and the incidents were resolved. **"Fine" described the outcome, and the cost of the
outcome was the part he left out.** The question had asked about the first and nobody had asked
about the second.

## The manager's part in it

There is a tempting conclusion here, that the person should be more open. It puts the work in the
wrong place. A manager controls three things the person does not: the question, the reaction to
the answer, and what happens afterwards.

The reaction matters more than people expect. If the last time somebody raised a problem the
manager looked irritated, explained why it was not really a problem, or immediately started fixing
it in a way that made more work for the person, the person learns. Marcos's empty section in lesson
5 was exactly that lesson, taught by a previous manager.

And what happens afterwards is the subject of lesson 7. A person who raises something, and sees
nothing change and hears nothing back, stops raising things. **The quality of answers a manager
gets is a record of how they handled the previous ones.**

## What this section is not saying

It does not say that people who answer "fine" are hiding something. Often things really are fine,
and a manager who treats every "fine" as a cover story becomes exhausting to talk to. The goal is
not to extract problems. It is to ask in a way that makes it easy to raise one if there is one,
and equally easy to say there is none.

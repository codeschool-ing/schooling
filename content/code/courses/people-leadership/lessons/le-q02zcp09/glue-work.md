title: Glue work, and the people it holds back

Every team has work that keeps it functioning and appears in nobody's list of achievements:
onboarding new people, reviewing pull requests, keeping the documentation current, noticing that two
people are building the same thing, following up on the action from last week's incident, running
the planning meeting. **Tanya Reilly called this glue work in a 2019 talk, *Being Glue*, and pointed
out that it is essential, under-rewarded, and not evenly distributed.**

## Why it matters and why it is not counted

Glue work is what makes other people's work possible. A team where nobody does it ships less, more
slowly, with more duplicated effort, and loses its new hires faster. But it rarely produces an
artefact a promotion panel recognises: there is no pull request for "made sure the payments
integration did not get built twice".

Reilly's point, and the reason this section exists, is that people who do a lot of glue work early in
their careers can find themselves told, at promotion time, that they have not done enough "technical
work". They were doing what the team needed, and the ladder or the panel valued something else.
**And the work is often handed to the same kinds of people**, those seen as helpful, organised or good
with people, which in many teams means it falls disproportionately on women and on whoever is
newest.

## What a manager can do

Renata looked at who on Agenda did the glue work and found the answer uncomfortable: Paula ran the
planning meeting, onboarded both recent hires, and kept the runbooks current. None of it appeared in
the notes of her performance review the year before Renata arrived.

Three changes followed.

**Rotate it.** Running planning now rotates monthly through everybody, including Diego. Onboarding
buddies rotate too. Glue work spread across a team is a shared cost; concentrated on one person, it
is a tax on their career.

**Name it, and record it.** When Paula's onboarding of Thiago meant he shipped his first change in
his first week, Renata wrote it in Paula's notes in the shape of lesson 9: what she did, and what it
caused. Glue work that is written down with its effect becomes evidence.

**Map it to the ladder.** Much glue work is in fact the influence row of lesson 11: raising the team's
standard, helping others' work get better. Written that way, onboarding Thiago is not "helping out"
but evidence of influence at the senior level. **The work is not invisible; it is undescribed**, and
the manager is the person best placed to describe it.

## When glue work is the job

For some people, glue work is what they want to do more of, and the path leads towards roles where
it is the main work: engineering management, technical programme management, or a staff role of the
right-hand shape from lesson 2. That is a legitimate direction and a manager can support it. What is
not legitimate is letting somebody slide into it by default, unrewarded, because they are good at it
and nobody else volunteers.

## Your task

List, for a team you know, the glue work done in the last month, and who did it. Then check:

- Is most of it done by one or two people?
- Is any of it recorded anywhere that would reach a performance review?
- For one piece, write one sentence describing it in terms of a ladder row, the way the onboarding of
  Thiago was described as influence.

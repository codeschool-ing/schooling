title: Three directions to grow that are not a new title

Promotion is one way to grow, and it is the slowest and least frequent one: at most companies a
person changes level a handful of times in a career. **Between promotions, and for people who do not
want the next level, growth happens in three other directions: scope, depth and visibility.** A
manager who only talks about growth in terms of levels has nothing to offer most of their team for
most of the year.

## Scope: more of the system

Growing in scope means owning a larger or more varied piece of work at the same level: a second
service, an area that touches another team, the on-call rota for a new system, the integration
nobody else understands. The level does not change; the territory does.

Paula is the example. After the "no" to the data-team project in lesson 7, she and Renata agreed on
something different: she would take over ownership of the calendar-sync service from Diego, who had
built it and had no time for it, in addition to the reminder service. It was a lateral move with no
new title, and it doubled the number of clinics' complaints that came to her first, which was the
point. **Scope growth is the most direct preparation for a promotion**, and also valuable for its own
sake, because it is what makes somebody hard to replace in a good way.

## Depth: becoming the person who knows

Growing in depth means becoming much better at one thing, often a thing the team depends on and few
people understand: database performance, accessibility, the time-zone logic that breaks every
daylight-saving change, incident response. Depth is how many excellent senior engineers grow for
years without changing level, and it is easy for managers to undervalue, because it looks from the
outside like doing the same job.

Fábio, who dislikes attention (lesson 9), told Renata he wanted to become the person who really
understood Agenda's database. They agreed he would lead the next two performance investigations,
and that Caju would pay for a course on PostgreSQL internals. **Depth needs a supply of hard problems
in the chosen area**, and the manager's job is to route those problems to the person rather than to
whoever is free.

## Visibility: work that other people can see

Growing in visibility means making existing work legible to people outside the immediate team:
writing up what you did, presenting it, being the person other teams ask. It is not self-promotion
for its own sake. **Work nobody outside the team has seen cannot count towards anything beyond the
team**, including a future promotion case, which lesson 12 showed depends on evidence from outside.

Diego is the example. His staff case failed for lack of influence beyond Agenda. Renata did not
suggest he change what he worked on. She suggested he write up the design of the calendar-sync
retry logic, which two other teams had asked him about privately, as a document the whole
engineering group could read, and present it at the monthly architecture review. That is the same
work, made visible.

## Choosing a direction

Renata asks each person, once a year and more often if things change, which of the three they want
for the next six months, and writes the answer on their page in her notebook. The answers shape
what she routes to them: scope people get the new services, depth people get the hard problems in
their area, visibility people get the write-ups and the presentations.

**A person can want none of them**, for a period. Somebody with a new baby, a sick parent or a
recent burnout may want their job to stay the same size for a while. That is a legitimate answer, and
a manager who pushes growth on somebody who has asked for stability is not helping.

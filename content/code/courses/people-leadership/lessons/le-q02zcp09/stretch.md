title: Stretch assignments, sized so they stretch and do not break
version: 1

The fastest growth comes from work slightly beyond what a person can do comfortably. **A stretch
assignment is work chosen to be at the edge of somebody's ability, with enough support that failing
at it is survivable.** Too small and nothing is learnt. Too large, or without support, and the person
fails publicly at something they were set up to fail at.

## Sizing it

Renata uses three questions when she chooses a stretch assignment:

1. **Which direction does it stretch?** Scope, depth or visibility, matched to what the person said
   they wanted. A visibility stretch for somebody who asked for depth is a burden, not a gift.
2. **What is the one new thing?** A good stretch adds one significant unfamiliar element, not three.
   Leading a project for the first time is a stretch. Leading a project for the first time, in an
   unfamiliar codebase, with a team from another department, on a fixed date, is a gamble.
3. **What happens if it goes wrong?** The cost of failure should land mostly on the learning, not
   on clinics or on the person's standing. If a failure would hurt customers, the assignment needs
   more support or a smaller first step.

## Support that does not take it back

A stretch needs support, and support is where managers most often undo the stretch. Lesson 3's
rescue is the danger: the manager sees the person struggling, which is what a stretch is supposed to
feel like, and takes the work back.

The support that works is the checkpoint structure from lesson 3, set at the start, plus somebody to
ask. When Thiago led his first small project, the integration of a new SMS provider for reminders,
Renata set a checkpoint after two days and weekly ones after that, and Yara agreed to be the person
he could ask anything without it reaching Renata. **The person needs a safe place to say "I don't know
how to do this"**, and that place is usually not their manager.

## After it

A stretch assignment ends with a conversation, not just a delivery. What went well, what was hard,
what the person would do differently, and what it shows against the ladder. Thiago's SMS project ran
a week over, and he handled a provider outage on the day it went live by rolling back cleanly and
writing it up. In the conversation afterwards, Renata pointed out that the rollback and the write-up
were exactly the operation and judgement the E2 craft row describes, which Thiago had not seen as
achievements at all.

**The conversation is where the stretch turns into growth the person can name**, and a stretch with
no conversation afterwards is just a hard month.

## Growth that does not lead to promotion

This lesson has described growth in directions that can feed a promotion case, but none of it has to.
A senior engineer who grows deeper in databases for five years, stays at E3 and is happy is a success
by every measure that matters to the team. **Managers who only value growth that leads up the ladder
end up with a team of people who feel they are failing**, because most of them, at any given time,
are not about to be promoted.

## Your task

Pick one person on a team you know, or one of the Caju team. Write, in your notebook, a stretch
assignment for them using the three sizing questions. Then check:

- It stretches in the direction they asked for.
- It has one new thing, not several.
- If it fails, clinics or customers are not the ones who pay.
- There is a checkpoint, and a person to ask who is not the manager.

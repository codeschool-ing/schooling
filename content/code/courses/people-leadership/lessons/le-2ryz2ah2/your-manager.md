Most of this course has been about the people who report to a manager. **Every manager also has a
manager, and the relationship upwards decides much of what is possible downwards**: the headcount, the
priorities, the support during a dismissal, the decision about a promotion. Managing it well is not
politics. It is making sure the person above you has what they need to help your team.

## What Otávio needed from Renata

In their first one-to-one after her promotion, Renata asked Otávio the question she later asked her own
team in lesson 1: what did he need from her? His answer had three parts, and they are close to what
most managers of managers need.

**No surprises.** He did not need to know everything. He needed to hear bad news from Renata before he
heard it from anybody else: a slipping date, an incident with a clinic, a person thinking of leaving.
**Bad news that reaches a manager's manager from somebody else costs trust that is hard to recover**,
because it raises the question of what else they have not been told.

**Problems with options.** When Renata brought him a problem, he wanted it to arrive with what she
thought the choices were and which one she recommended. Not because he could not think, but because his
week was cut into more pieces than hers, and a problem with a recommendation can be decided in five
minutes while one without can take a meeting.

**Her honest read.** He had eleven teams and saw each through its manager. He needed Renata to tell him
what she actually thought about Agenda's state, including what was going badly, rather than the version
that made her look best.

## The one-to-one, upwards

Renata's one-to-one with Otávio runs on the same principles as the ones she runs for her team, from the
other side. She keeps a shared document. Her topics come first, because it is her meeting. She brings
three kinds of item:

| kind | example |
|---|---|
| things he needs to know | "The confirmations work slips two weeks; here's why and what I'm doing about it." |
| decisions she needs from him | "Two options for the Payments dependency; I recommend the second." |
| help she wants | "I'd like your view on how I handled the debrief with Helena's dissent." |

The third kind is the one new managers leave out, because asking for help upwards feels like admitting
weakness. **A manager who never asks their own manager for help is not handling everything alone; they
are making the mistakes alone**, and their manager finds out later.

## When your manager is the problem

Sometimes the difficulty is the manager above. They are absent, or they change priorities every week, or
they give the team's work away in meetings without asking. Renata had a version of this in her first
quarter: Otávio twice committed Agenda to dates in leadership meetings without consulting her.

She used the feedback structure from lesson 8, in her next one-to-one, privately: the two meetings,
what was committed, the effect on the team's plan and on her credibility with the team. She asked what
he would need in order to check with her first. He had not realised; he had been answering questions
in the moment and assuming he knew the team's capacity. They agreed he would say "I'll confirm with
Renata" in those meetings. **Feedback upwards works the same way as downwards**, and lesson 8 said a
manager who never gives it is teaching their own team that feedback only travels one way.

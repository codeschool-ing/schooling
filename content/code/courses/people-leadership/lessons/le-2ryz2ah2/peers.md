A manager naturally thinks of their team as the people who report to them. **Patrick Lencioni, in *The
Five Dysfunctions of a Team* (2002) and later writing, argues for a different idea: a leader's first team
is their peers, the other managers who report to the same person.** It is a deliberately uncomfortable
claim, and it captures something real about how organisations work.

## Why peers come first

Most of the problems a manager cannot solve inside their own team are problems between teams. The
Payments dependency in lesson 22, the shared rota with the platform team, the headcount that two teams
both want: none of them can be solved by being a better manager of one team. They need a working
relationship with the other managers, built before the problem arrives.

**A manager who treats their peers as competitors for resources will win some arguments and lose the
organisation's trust.** One who treats them as the team that shares responsibility for the whole
product finds that problems between teams get solved earlier and more fairly, as the one-page
escalation in lesson 22 was.

## What Renata did with Bia

Renata and Bia had met only in incidents before the dependency argument. Afterwards they set up a
monthly coffee, with no agenda. In the months that followed it paid off in ways neither could have
planned:

- Bia warned Renata a month early that Payments would change a field name Agenda used, and the change
  was planned rather than discovered.
- Renata told Bia that one of Payments' engineers had been doing excellent work in a cross-team incident,
  which Bia had not seen and which went into that engineer's promotion case.
- When Otávio's group was reorganised, Renata and Bia were the two managers whose proposal, written
  together, shaped where the boundary between their teams fell.

None of this was friendship for its own sake, though it became that. **It was the working relationship
that lets two teams solve their problems directly rather than through their directors.**

## What a peer group needs

Otávio's managers met fortnightly as a group. Renata noticed in her first months that the meeting was
mostly status, each manager reporting to Otávio in turn while the others waited. It was a one-to-one
held in public, the drifted version of lesson 5 at a larger scale.

She suggested, and the group agreed, that the meeting open with problems that crossed teams, and that
any manager could bring one. **Status went into a written update read before the meeting**, and the
time together went on the things that only made sense with the other managers in the room: shared
dependencies, people moving between teams, a hiring bar that should be the same across the group.

## Your task

List, in your notebook, the peers of a manager you know, or of Renata at Caju: the other people who
report to the same person. For each, write one problem your team and theirs share, and whether there is
a working relationship to solve it with today. Then check:

- Is there at least one peer whose team you depend on and whose manager you rarely speak to outside an
  incident?
- What would it cost to have a conversation with them before the next problem?

---
title: What a one-to-one is for, and the meeting it turns into
version: 1
---

A one-to-one is a regular, private meeting between a manager and one person who reports to them.
Almost every engineering company has them, and most of them have drifted into the same thing: **a
status update, where the person tells the manager what they did this week and the manager nods.**
That meeting is worth almost nothing, because everything in it is already in the tracker, the pull
requests and the stand-up.

## What the meeting is for

Andy Grove, whose sum opened lesson 1, wrote about one-to-ones in the same book, and his framing is
still the most useful: **the one-to-one is the report's meeting, not the manager's.** The agenda is
theirs, and the manager's job is to make room for what they bring. Grove argued that its value
lies in what it lets the person raise that they would raise nowhere else.

Concretely, a one-to-one is the place for four kinds of conversation that have no other home:

| kind | what it sounds like |
|---|---|
| what is in their way | "I've been waiting on the platform team for a week and I don't know who to push" |
| how they are | "I'm finding the on-call rota hard with the baby" |
| where they are going | "I'd like to do more design work; how would I get there?" |
| what you need to tell each other | feedback in both directions, which lesson 8 is about |

Status belongs elsewhere because it has a better home. A question about whether a ticket is done
can be answered by looking at the ticket. A question about whether the person is thinking of
leaving cannot be answered by looking at anything.

## Why it drifts into status

The drift happens for understandable reasons, and knowing them makes it easier to resist.

**Status is safe.** For the person, describing work is the easiest thing to say to a manager. For
the manager, hearing it feels productive. Neither has to raise anything awkward.

**Nobody prepared.** If the person arrives with nothing to discuss, the manager fills the silence,
and the easiest thing to fill it with is "so, how's the calendar-sync work going?"

**The manager owns the agenda by default.** When the manager opens with a question, the meeting
follows the manager's interests, and the manager's interests lean towards delivery.

Renata inherited the drifted version. Her predecessor's one-to-ones with Paula, according to Paula,
had been "him reading the board aloud and asking if I was blocked". Paula had stopped bringing
anything to them a year earlier.

## What changed when Renata took them over

In her first one-to-one as Paula's manager, Renata said three things before anything else: that
this was Paula's time, that she would not ask about ticket status because she could read the
board, and that she would like Paula to start a shared document where either of them could add
topics during the week. The next two sections are about the second and third of those.

The first two weeks were awkward. Paula came with nothing, and Renata had to resist the reflex to
fill the silence with work questions. She used questions from lesson 6 instead, which are about
the person rather than the work. In the third week Paula added her first topic to the document: she
wanted to stop being the only person who understood the reminder service, because it meant she
could never take a full week off. **That topic had existed for a year and had never been raised,
because there had never been a meeting where it fitted.**

## The test of a good one-to-one

A short test separates the meeting from its imitation. At the end of it, ask yourself whether
anything was said that could not have been said in a group, or found in a tool. If the answer is
no, three or four times in a row, the meeting has drifted, and the fix is the agenda, not the
cadence. Lessons 6 and 7 are about the two things that keep it from drifting back: questions that
get past the first answer, and doing something with what you heard.

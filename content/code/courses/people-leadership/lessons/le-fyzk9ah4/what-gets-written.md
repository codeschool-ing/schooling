title: What gets written down, and who can read it

A one-to-one produces two kinds of record. **The shared document, which both people read, and the
manager's own notes, which only the manager reads.** Knowing which is which, and what belongs in
each, is the difference between notes that help and notes that one day hurt somebody.

## The shared notes

At the end of each meeting, Renata writes a line or two per topic in the shared document, and the
actions with owners and dates. She writes them in the meeting, with the person watching, or in the
five minutes afterwards and asks the person to correct anything.

Three properties make shared notes useful:

- **They are short.** A line per topic. The aim is to remember what was decided, not to transcribe
  the conversation.
- **The person agrees with them.** If Paula reads a note and says "that's not quite what I meant",
  the note changes. A shared note the person disagrees with is a record of the manager's version.
- **Actions have an owner and a date.** "Look into the on-call rota" is not an action. "Renata to
  ask the platform team about a second on-call rota by Friday" is. Lesson 7 is built on this column.

## The manager's private notes

Some things the manager needs to remember do not belong in a document the person reads together
with the manager every week. Renata's notebook, from lesson 1, holds a page per person, and it is
where these go: an observation she has not yet turned into feedback, a pattern across several
meetings, her own reaction to something said.

The rule from lesson 1 still applies, and it applies most here. **Write as if the person might one
day read it**, because in many places they have the right to ask for what is held about them, and
because notes leak. A private note saying "seemed distracted again, third week running; ask if
something is going on" is fair. One saying "probably going through a divorce, explains a lot" is
speculation about somebody's private life, and has no business on any page.

## What is never written

Some things a person says in a one-to-one should not be written down anywhere, or only in the
barest form:

| said in the meeting | what to write, if anything |
|---|---|
| details of a health condition | the adjustment agreed, if any: "Thursdays flexible until March" |
| a family situation | nothing, unless it changes work: "away 12 to 16 May" |
| something about a colleague, said in confidence | nothing until you have decided what to do; lesson 22 covers conflict |
| that they are interviewing elsewhere | nothing in shared notes; your private notes may record that you discussed their growth |

The common thread is the same principle as lesson 1's: **write what you need to act on**, and
nothing beyond it. Brazil's data protection law, the LGPD, treats health information as sensitive
personal data, and company policy may be stricter still. When in doubt, ask whoever handles people
matters at your company, Caju's people partner in Renata's case, before writing rather than after.

## Who else may see them

The shared document is between the two people. Nobody else reads it by default, not the manager's
manager and not HR, and the person should know that. If something in it ever has to go further,
for example because the person has described harassment, or because it becomes part of a
performance process, the manager says so to the person before it goes anywhere.

Renata told each person this in the first meeting, in one sentence: "What we write here stays
between us, and if I ever think something needs to go to somebody else, I'll tell you first."
**People decide what to say in a one-to-one by what they believe happens to it afterwards**, and
the sentence is what makes the rest of the meeting possible.

## When the notes become evidence

Notes written week by week also become, without anybody planning it, the best record of how a
person has been doing. Lesson 12 uses them to build a promotion case, and lesson 20 to write an
improvement plan. Both work only if the notes were honest all along. A promotion case that cites
six months of one-to-one notes saying "doing great" is weak; so is an improvement plan whose notes
never once mentioned the problem. **The notes are the evidence that feedback was given**, and if it
was not written down, for practical purposes it was not given.

How often to meet is the question new managers ask first, and the answer matters less than they
think. **What matters is that the meeting is regular enough that nothing waits long for it, and
reliable enough that the person trusts it will happen.** A weekly meeting that is cancelled every
other week is worse than a fortnightly one that always runs.

## A starting point

Most engineering managers settle on thirty minutes a week, or forty-five minutes every two weeks.
Grove suggested adjusting the frequency to how experienced the person is at the work they are
doing at the moment, which he called task-relevant maturity: more often for somebody new to the
job or to a task, less often for somebody who has done it many times.

| situation | a reasonable cadence |
|---|---|
| a new hire, in their first three months | weekly, thirty minutes |
| somebody in a new role or on unfamiliar work | weekly |
| an experienced person on familiar work | every two weeks, forty-five minutes |
| somebody going through something hard, at work or at home | weekly, for as long as it lasts |
| somebody being considered for promotion, or on an improvement plan | weekly |

The last row is worth noticing. Both a promotion and an improvement plan (lesson 20) are periods
when the person needs frequent, specific signal about how they are doing, and a fortnight is a long
time to wait for it.

Renata manages seven people. Weekly half-hours for all of them is three and a half hours a week,
which she judged worth it in her first quarter, when she was still learning the team. In her
second quarter she moved Diego and Yara, the two most experienced, to every two weeks, and kept the
others weekly. She asked both before changing it.

In the first weeks the meetings are mostly about getting to know each other and about immediate
obstacles, because those are what the person can raise safely with somebody new. As trust builds,
the share spent on obstacles drops, and conversations about growth and about how the person really
is take more of the time. **Feedback in both directions becomes possible only once the
relationship can carry it**, which is why a manager who gives hard feedback in the second week is
usually heard as attacking rather than helping.

The figure is a sketch of a typical pattern, not a schedule. Some people bring growth on day one;
some never talk about how they are, and that is their right.

## The meeting you never cancel

Moving a one-to-one is fine. Cancelling it, especially at short notice and especially more than
once, sends a message nobody intends: **that everything else in the manager's week is more
important than this person.** People keep count, even when they say they do not mind.

Renata's rule is that she moves a one-to-one, she never drops one, and if she moves it she moves it
within the same week. When she cannot, because she is travelling or ill, she says so with a reason
and offers a shorter slot. Twice in her first quarter the person said they had nothing to discuss
and offered to skip. She accepted the first time, and the second time asked for ten minutes anyway,
which turned out to be when Thiago mentioned he was struggling with on-call.

## The other direction

People sometimes want to cancel their own one-to-ones, and it is worth asking why. Occasionally
it means things are fine and there is nothing to raise. More often, especially when it repeats, it
means the meeting has drifted into status and the person sees no value in it, or that they have
something to say and are avoiding it. Neither is solved by agreeing to skip.

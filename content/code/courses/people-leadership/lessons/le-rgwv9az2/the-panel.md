At most companies past a certain size, a promotion is not decided by the manager alone. **A panel, a
group of managers and senior engineers from across the organisation, reads the cases together and
decides.** It exists for a good reason, and it changes how a manager has to prepare.

## Why panels exist

A manager deciding promotions alone has every bias lesson 17 describes, applied to their own team:
the people they like, the people who resemble them, the people whose work is visible. Different
managers also apply different bars, which is the inconsistency lesson 11 said a written ladder
exists to prevent. **A panel is how a company applies one bar across many managers.** It reads cases
side by side, which exposes the manager who calls everything excellent, and it includes people
with no stake in the outcome.

At Caju, the panel for E4 has five members: two engineering managers, two staff engineers and a
director, none from the candidate's own team. They read the cases in advance and meet for an hour
per batch.

## What a panel asks

Panels tend to ask the same questions, and a case that answers them before they are asked does
well:

- **Is this the person's work or the team's?** Every "we" in a case invites this question.
- **Is it sustained?** One great quarter is a good quarter.
- **Is the scope real?** Did the work really span three teams, or did three teams receive an email?
- **What would the person's peers at that level say?** Comparing with existing staff engineers is the
  panel's way of checking the bar.
- **What is missing?** The gaps section either answers this or the panel finds it.

Renata was not in the room for Yara's case. That is the norm at Caju and many companies: the case has
to stand on its own, which is the whole argument for writing it the way the previous section
described.

## When the answer is yes

Yara's promotion was approved. Renata told her in person, before the announcement, and said what had
convinced the panel: the two teams' statements about the reminder pattern. **Telling the person why
the case worked is feedback too**, and it calibrates them for the next level the way lesson 9
described.

## When the answer is no

The previous cycle, Diego's case for staff had been turned down, under the manager before Renata. He
had been told "not this time" and nothing else, and he still did not know why. Renata asked the panel
chair for the reasoning and found it was specific: the case had shown deep technical work within
Agenda and no influence beyond it.

The conversation after a no is one of the most important a manager has, and it has four parts:

1. **The decision, plainly.** Not softened into "it was close", unless it was.
2. **The panel's reasons, specifically.** What row, what was missing, in the panel's words where
   possible.
3. **What would change the answer.** The kind of work that would fill the gap, not a promise that
   doing it guarantees anything.
4. **Room for the person's reaction.** Disappointment, anger, or a decision to look elsewhere are
   all legitimate. The manager listens and does not argue.

What a manager must never do after a no is blame the panel to stay on the person's side. "I fought
for you, they just didn't get it" feels supportive, teaches the person that the process is arbitrary,
and gives them nothing to work on. Lesson 24 comes back to this: the manager carries the company's
decisions to the team, including the ones they argued against.

## Your task

Imagine the case you wrote a row of in the previous section is turned down for a gap in a different
row. Write, in your notebook, the four parts of the conversation you would have. Check that the
second part names a row of the ladder, and that the third describes work rather than a promise.

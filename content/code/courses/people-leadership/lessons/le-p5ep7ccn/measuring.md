---
title: Counting departures without fooling yourself
version: 1
---

Companies track how many people leave, usually as an attrition rate. **The number is useful only if
you know what it counts, over what period, and whether the people who left were the ones you would have
fought to keep.** Without those, a rate can make a healthy team look troubled or a troubled one look
fine.

## The rate

The usual calculation for a year is the number of people who left during the year, divided by the
average number of people employed over it, as a percentage.

Agenda's numbers for Renata's first full year, with the arithmetic:

| | |
|---|---|
| headcount at the start of the year | 7 |
| joined during the year | 2 (Lucas, and a replacement for Marcos) |
| left during the year | 2 (Marcos, Camila) |
| headcount at the end of the year | 7 |
| average headcount | (7 + 7) / 2 = 7 |
| attrition rate | 2 / 7 = 28.6% |

Almost twenty-nine percent sounds alarming, and for a team of seven it is two people. **Small teams produce
large percentages from single events**, which is why a rate for a team of seven says little on its own
and a rate for an engineering organisation of ninety says more.

## Regretted and unregretted

The more useful split is between departures the company regrets and those it does not. Marcos's
departure was a dismissal after a fair process: unregretted, in the language most companies use,
though not something anybody was glad about. Camila's was regretted: a good engineer the team wanted to
keep.

| | count | rate |
|---|---|---|
| all departures | 2 | 28.6% |
| regretted | 1 | 14.3% |
| unregretted | 1 | 14.3% |

A team with high unregretted attrition and low regretted attrition may be dealing honestly with
performance. A team with high regretted attrition is losing the people it most needs, whatever the
total says. **The question for a manager is always the regretted line**, and the one after it: why.

## Voluntary and involuntary

Another split, sometimes used instead, separates people who chose to leave from people the company
let go. It overlaps with regretted and unregretted without matching it: somebody who resigns because
they were struggling may be unregretted, and a layoff can remove people the company would have kept.
Use whichever split answers the question being asked, and say which one it is.

## What the numbers will not tell you

Even a clean regretted-attrition rate says nothing about why. Exit surveys help a little, and suffer
from the problem in the first section: people leaving give the safest reason. **The best source of
reasons is the one-to-one notes of the people who stayed**, read in the way lesson 7 described, because
the themes in them are the reasons the next person will give, months before they give them.

## Your task

For a team you know, or Agenda, compute the attrition rate for a year, and split it into regretted and
unregretted. Then check:

- You used the average headcount, not the headcount at one moment.
- You can say, for each regretted departure, one reason that was within the manager's control.
- You noted how small the team is before drawing any conclusion from the percentage.

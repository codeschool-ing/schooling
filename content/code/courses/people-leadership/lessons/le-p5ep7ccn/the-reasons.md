When somebody resigns, the reason given in the exit conversation is usually the safest one available:
a better offer, a shorter commute, an opportunity too good to refuse. **The real reasons tend to have
started months earlier, and a good share of them were within the manager's reach.** A manager who
accepts the official reason learns nothing; one who looks for the earlier causes can sometimes keep the
next person.

## Camila

Camila was a mid-level engineer on Agenda, steady and well liked, who had been on the team for two
years. In Renata's second spring she resigned to join a smaller company. In her exit conversation she
said the new job paid more and was closer to home.

Both were true. When Renata reread Camila's one-to-one notes for the previous year, she found three
other things. Camila had asked twice about moving towards front-end work and had been told "maybe next
quarter" both times. Her growth section, in the tally from lesson 7, had been empty since the autumn.
And she had been on call over two holidays in a row before the rota was fixed in lesson 1, a detail she
mentioned once and never again.

**None of those would have appeared in an exit survey**, and all of them had been visible in the notes
for months.

## The common reasons, sorted by who controls them

Research and surveys on why people leave jobs vary in method and in numbers, and it is worth being
careful about any single figure. What they agree on is the shape: a mixture of things the company
controls, things the manager controls, and things nobody at work controls. The useful exercise is to
sort them:

| reason | who has most control |
|---|---|
| no growth: the same work, no path to new work | the manager |
| the relationship with the manager | the manager |
| workload or on-call that is unsustainable | the manager, then the company |
| feeling the work is not valued or noticed | the manager |
| pay below the market | the company, with the manager’s advocacy |
| a better offer, unprompted | partly nobody |
| moving city, family, a change of career | nobody at work |

Frederick Herzberg, studying motivation at work in the 1950s, drew a distinction that fits this table.
Some factors, which he called hygiene factors, such as pay, conditions and policies, cause
dissatisfaction when they are bad but do not motivate much when they are good. Others, such as
achievement, recognition, responsibility and growth, are what actually motivate. **A manager controls
little of the pay and the policies, and much of the second kind**, which is where their effort on retention belongs.

## What Renata could have done

Looking back honestly, Renata could not have matched the new salary; Caju's bands were set elsewhere.
She could have done three things. Answered "maybe next quarter" with a real plan, or a real no, as lesson
7 described. Noticed the empty growth section, which lesson 13 says is information. And asked, in a
one-to-one months earlier, the question the next section is about.

That does not mean Camila would have stayed. It means some of the reasons were Renata's, and the
honest response is to own those and change how she manages, rather than file the departure under
"better offer".

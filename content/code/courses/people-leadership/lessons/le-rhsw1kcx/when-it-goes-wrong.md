---
title: Checkpoints, rescue and the difference between delegating and disappearing
version: 1
---

Delegated work goes wrong in two opposite ways, and new managers usually commit both within a
month. **One is grabbing the work back at the first sign of trouble. The other is never looking at
it until it is too late.** Both feel like respect for the person, and neither is.

## The rescue

Three days into the reminders work, Renata saw Paula's draft pull request. It was bigger than she
expected, it touched the reschedule code, and it did not look like anything she would have written.
Her first instinct was to comment with the fix she had in mind. Her second was to message Paula and
offer to pair, which is the same thing with better manners.

She did neither. She asked one question in the pull request: "What did you find about where the
duplicates come from?" Paula's answer explained the reschedule cause, which Renata had not known.
**The pull request looked wrong because Renata's model of the problem was wrong**, and rescuing it
would have replaced a correct fix with an incorrect one.

This is the typical shape. A manager who rescues sees an approach that differs from theirs and
reads it as trouble. Sometimes it is. More often the person knows something the manager does not,
because they have spent three days inside the problem and the manager has spent ten minutes. The
cost of a rescue that was not needed is high and slow to appear: the person learns that the
outcome was never really theirs, and the next time they wait to be told.

**A question before a correction is the rule.** Ask what they found, what they considered, why this
approach. If the answers are good, the manager has learnt something. If they reveal a real
problem, the person usually sees it while answering, which is better for everybody than being told.

## The disappearance

The opposite failure happened on Agenda a month later. Renata delegated the work of upgrading the
team's notification library to Marcos at level 7, because it looked routine, and then did not ask
about it for three weeks. When she did, he had been stuck for two of them on a breaking change he
did not know how to approach, and had said nothing because the work was supposed to be his.

Delegating at level 7 says the decisions are his. It does not say he is alone. **A delegated
outcome still needs checkpoints**, agreed in advance, so that being stuck surfaces in days rather
than weeks, and so that checking in does not feel like distrust.

## Checkpoints that do not take the work back

A checkpoint is a moment agreed at the start when the person shows where they are. What makes it a
checkpoint and not supervision is that the time is set in advance, and the question is about
progress, not about method.

| the work | a reasonable checkpoint |
|---|---|
| a few days, familiar to the person | one at the end, or none |
| a week or two | one after the first day or two: "what did you find, what's the plan?" |
| several weeks, or unfamiliar | weekly, plus one early: "tell me the first thing that surprised you" |
| anything with a hard date | one at the halfway point, against the date |

The early checkpoint does most of the work. It catches the misunderstood outcome while it is still
cheap to fix, and it catches the person who is stuck before they have been stuck for long. Renata
now writes the checkpoint into the delegation itself, as the last line under the five headings.

## When the rescue is right

Some situations do justify taking work back, and it is worth knowing them so the instinct can be
trusted when it fires for a reason. If the date is about to be missed and the date is real; if a
security or data-protection problem is being introduced; if the person asks for help and the help
they need is for somebody else to take over. In each case the right move is to say so openly and
change the rung, as the previous section described, rather than quietly doing the work and leaving
the person to find out.

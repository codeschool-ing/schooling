Delegating is usually discussed as if it had two settings: you do it, or they do it. **In practice
there is a whole ladder between "I decide and tell you" and "you decide and I never hear about
it"**, and choosing the rung on purpose is most of the skill. The wrong rung fails in both
directions: too low and the manager is a bottleneck, too high and the person is left alone with a
decision they had no way to get right.

## Appelo's ladder

Jurgen Appelo's *Management 3.0* (2011) names seven levels, and the names are worth learning
because they let two people agree on the rung in a sentence.

1. **Tell.** The manager decides and announces it.
2. **Sell.** The manager decides, and works to convince the person it was right.
3. **Consult.** The manager asks for the person's view first, then decides.
4. **Agree.** They decide together, and either can block.
5. **Advise.** The person decides, after hearing the manager's view.
6. **Inquire.** The person decides, and tells the manager afterwards.
7. **Delegate.** The person decides, and the manager does not need to hear about it.

The middle of the ladder is where the authority moves across. At level 3 the manager still
decides; at level 5 the person does. Level 4 is the only rung where nobody can decide alone, which
makes it slow, and it is best kept for decisions that really need two people's consent.

## Choosing the rung

Two questions settle most cases. **How costly is a wrong decision, and how often has this person
made a decision like it?** Expensive and unfamiliar sits low; cheap and familiar sits high.

Renata's reminders delegation was level 6 for the design: Paula would decide the fix and tell her
afterwards, because Paula had worked on the reminder service for a year and a wrong fix could be
reverted the same day. For the alert that came with it, Renata chose level 5, because it would
page whoever was on call and she wanted to hear the threshold before it went live. One piece of
work, two decisions, two rungs.

The same decision moves up the ladder as the person grows. When Thiago took the calendar-sync work
in lesson 2, the design was at level 3: he proposed, Diego decided. Two months later the next
integration was at level 5, and Diego only gave his view.

## Saying the rung out loud

The ladder is only useful if both people know which rung they are on. The commonest failure is a
manager who believes they delegated at level 5 and a person who believes it was level 7: the person
decides and does not mention it, and the manager hears about it from somebody else and feels
bypassed. Or the reverse, a person who thinks they are at level 3 and waits for a decision the
manager thought was theirs to make.

So Renata adds one line to the five headings of the previous section: the rung, in words. "This
one is yours to decide, tell me what you chose" is level 6. "Show me before it goes live" is level
5. It costs a sentence and prevents the conversation that starts with "I thought you were doing
that".

## Going back down the ladder

Sometimes a delegation goes badly and the manager has to take a decision back. **Doing it
silently is the worst version**: the person discovers that their decision was quietly reversed and
learns that the rung meant nothing. Doing it openly is ordinary: "this is bigger than we thought,
so I'd like to move it to consult; you propose, and I'll decide by Thursday." That is a change of
rung with a reason, and the person can disagree with it.

The opposite failure is just as common and harder to notice: the manager who delegates at level 7
and then never looks. Delegating is not disappearing. The next section is about the checkpoints
that let a manager stay informed without taking the work back.

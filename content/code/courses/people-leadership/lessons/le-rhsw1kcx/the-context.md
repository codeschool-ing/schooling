An outcome on its own is half a delegation. **The person also needs what the manager knows and
they do not**: why the work matters, what limits it has to respect, what they may decide without
asking, and whom to ask about the rest. Without that, an outcome is a riddle, and a capable person
will solve it in a way that is reasonable and wrong.

## Intent, from an older trade

Armies worked this out long before software. Orders that specify every movement fail as soon as
the situation changes, which in a battle is immediately. So the Prussian army in the nineteenth
century started writing orders around the commander's **intent**: what the operation was for, so
that an officer whose plan had fallen apart could still act in the direction intended. Modern
militaries call it mission command, and the idea transfers almost unchanged.

David Marquet, who commanded a US Navy submarine, described a version of it in *Turn the Ship
Around!* (2012). He stopped giving orders and asked his officers to come to him with "I intend
to…" followed by what they planned, and over time with why it was safe. **The officer did the
thinking, and the commander's job became checking it and, more and more often, just saying yes.**
That shift is what an outcome is meant to produce, and it only happens if the person has the
context to think with.

## Five things to send

Renata uses five headings when she delegates anything that will take more than a couple of days.
They are written in the message or the ticket, not said in passing:

| heading | what goes there | for the reminders work |
|---|---|---|
| the outcome | what has to be true, and how anyone will know | one reminder per appointment, and an alert if not |
| why it matters | who is affected and what it costs | patients ignore the messages; two clinics complained |
| the limits | what must not change, the budget, the date | no change to the message text, which legal approved; by Friday next week |
| what you decide | the decisions that are the person's own | the cause, the design, whether it needs a migration |
| who knows what | people with information the person will need | Helena has the complaints; Yara wrote the reschedule code |

The first two make the work make sense. The last three are where delegations go wrong, and each
for its own reason.

**Limits are the facts only the manager has.** Renata knew that the reminder text had been
approved by Caju's lawyers and could not change without them. Paula did not, and a perfectly sensible
fix (adding "this replaces your earlier reminder" to the message) would have gone through review
and been reverted a week later.

**What you decide stops the person asking permission for everything.** Without it, a careful
person checks every choice with the manager, which turns an outcome back into a stream of tasks.
"The design is yours" is a sentence that saves several conversations.

**Who knows what saves days.** The person most likely to know about the reschedule code was not on
Paula's mind and was on Renata's, because Renata had reviewed it the year before.

## What does not go in

Two things are better left out, though they feel helpful.

The first is the manager's guess at the solution. If Renata had added "I think it might be the
`sent_at` check", Paula would have started there, and a guess from your manager is hard to
discard even when the evidence points elsewhere. **If you have a hypothesis, say it is one, and say
it after the outcome.** Better still, leave it out until asked.

The second is a deadline with no reason. "By Friday" invites the question of whether Friday is
real. "By Friday, because the clinics' association meets on Monday and Helena wants to tell them
it is fixed" is a limit the person can weigh against what they find.

## Your task

Take a piece of work you have recently been given, or one you have recently given to somebody, and
rewrite it in your notebook under the five headings. Then check it:

- The outcome names no table, file, library or screen.
- The limits include at least one thing the other person could not have known.
- **What you decide** lists real decisions, not "small details".
- Somebody could start on it tomorrow without asking you a question.

If the original was a task, notice how much of the five headings you had to invent. That is the
thinking that was being kept back.

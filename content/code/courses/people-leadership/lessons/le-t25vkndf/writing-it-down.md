Two people sharing the leadership of a team agree on most things without discussing them. **The
trouble is the handful of things each assumes is theirs**, and those surface at the worst moment:
in a planning meeting, in front of the team, when both answer the same question differently.
The fix costs an hour, and it is a short document both people sign up to.

## What happened before Agenda wrote it

Three weeks after the split, Thiago asked in the team channel whether he could pick up the
calendar-sync work. Diego replied that it needed somebody more experienced and he would take it
himself. Ten minutes later Renata replied that Thiago should take it, because in his last one-to-one
he had asked for exactly that kind of work, and Diego could pair with him.

Both replies were reasonable. Each came from a person who believed the decision was theirs: Diego
because it was a question about technical risk, Renata because it was a question about a person's
growth. **Thiago learned that his two leads disagreed and that he had caused it**, which was the
one thing nobody intended to teach him.

## The agreement

Renata and Diego sat down that week and wrote one page. For each item in the middle of the figure
in the first section, they wrote who **decides**, who is **consulted** before the decision, and who
is **informed** after it. A decision with two deciders is a decision with none, so every row has
exactly one.

| decision | decides | consulted | informed |
|---|---|---|---|
| how a feature is designed | Diego | Renata, when it changes a date | the team |
| who works on what | Renata | Diego, on technical risk | the team |
| what the team commits to in a quarter | Renata | Diego and Helena | Otávio |
| whether a pull request meets the bar | Diego | — | — |
| the technical response during an incident | whoever is on call, then Diego | Renata | Otávio |
| a candidate's technical assessment | Diego | the other interviewers | Renata |
| whether to make an offer | Renata | Diego and the interviewers | Otávio |

Under the table they added two rules. **When either of them is unsure whose decision it is, they
ask each other before answering anybody else.** And when they disagree about a decision that is
already assigned, they argue in private, then the person who owns it decides, and the other
supports the decision in public. Lesson 22 comes back to that second rule, because it is harder to
keep than to write.

## Why the page matters more than the conversation

The conversation alone would have helped for a month. People remember an agreement in the version
that suits them, and the memory drifts in the direction each person already leaned. **The page is
what both of them can point at when they disagree about what they agreed.** It also does something
the conversation cannot: the team can read it. Agenda's agreement sits in the team's documents, and
when Thiago wants to know who decides who works on what, he reads it instead of asking in the
channel and watching two answers arrive.

They review it every quarter, and it has changed twice. Interviews moved from Diego's column to a
shared rota once Paula and Yara started interviewing, and incident follow-up moved to Renata after a
postmortem action sat unscheduled for six weeks.

## Your task

In your notebook's drafts, write the same page for a team you know: one you are on now, one you
were on, or Agenda itself if you have neither. List at least five decisions from the middle of the
figure, and for each one write a single decider. Then check it:

- Every row has exactly one name under **decides**.
- At least one row would surprise one of the two people, which means it is doing work.
- Nothing on it depends on who happens to be in the room.
- Somebody on the team could read it and know whom to ask.

If you could not fill a row because you genuinely do not know who decides that on your team, you
have found the thing the page is for.

title: What each shape costs, and the way back

Choosing between the two shapes is usually presented as a promotion and a sideways move, with the
manager's job as the promotion. **That framing is wrong, and it causes the worst mistake in this
area: people taking the management job because it is the only way up, and then being bad at a job
they never wanted.** Many companies now run two parallel career tracks for exactly this reason,
which lesson 11 describes. This section is about what each choice costs the person making it.

## What the manager gives up

The manager gives up the thing that made them good: their hands in the code. **A year or so into
managing full-time, the usual finding is that technical judgement still works and technical
currency does not.** They know how a system should be designed and no longer know the
specific framework version, the deploy tooling, or why the test suite takes nine minutes. The
second kind of knowledge decays faster than people expect, and it is the kind a tech lead needs
every day.

They also give up the fast loop. Lesson 1 showed how far apart the two kinds of feedback sit, and
for many engineers the short loop is the reason they liked the work. A manager who misses it is not
weak. They have noticed a real loss.

What they get in return is leverage over things code cannot reach: who joins the team, how people
grow, what the team agrees to, and whether a talented person stays.

## What the tech lead gives up

The tech lead gives up less code than people imagine and more of their own time than they expect.
Diego still writes code, but a third of his week now goes on reviews, design discussions and
questions from the rest of the team. His own tickets get done in the remaining time, which is
cut into pieces like a manager's, only less so.

He also gives up the formal authority that would make some of his work easier. A tech lead decides
technical questions by persuasion and by being right often enough, because he does not write
anybody's review or decide their pay. **That is a feature as much as a cost**: engineers argue more
openly with somebody who cannot affect their promotion.

## The manager who still writes code

There is a version of the manager's job with code in it, and it is legitimate. What matters is
which code. Three kinds fit a manager's week, and one kind does not.

| fits | why |
|---|---|
| reviewing pull requests | keeps them current with the codebase, and can be done in the gaps between meetings |
| small fixes and tooling off the critical path | nobody waits on them if a meeting runs over |
| prototypes that will be thrown away | they answer a question without becoming something to maintain |

**What does not fit is work the team's plan depends on.** A feature with a date on it, assigned to
somebody whose calendar lesson 1 described, will be late, and the team will wait for it while
nobody is allowed to say so. Renata keeps her own code to reviews and to a few small fixes a
month, and she does not put her name on any ticket in the sprint plan.

## The pendulum

Charity Majors wrote an essay in 2017 called *The Engineer/Manager Pendulum*. It argues that the
best people in either role have usually done both, and that moving back from management to
engineering, after a few years, is a normal career and not a failure. A manager who has recently
been an engineer makes better technical calls and is trusted more. An engineer who has managed
understands what their manager is dealing with and writes better for people outside the team.

Two things follow for somebody choosing now. **The choice is reversible**, and treating it as
permanent makes people stay in a job they dislike out of fear of looking like they failed. And the
way back gets harder the longer you wait, because technical currency is the thing that decays: two
or three years away is a return, ten is a restart.

Renata wrote one line in her notebook's decisions page on the day she accepted the job: "Manage for
at least two years, then decide again on purpose." The date she wrote beside it is the reason the
line is useful.

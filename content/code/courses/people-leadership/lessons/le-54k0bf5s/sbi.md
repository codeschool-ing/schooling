The Center for Creative Leadership, an American institute that researches leadership, teaches a
structure for feedback called **situation, behaviour, impact**. It is simple enough to use in the
middle of a busy week, and it forces the observation from the previous section into the shape the
person can use: **when, what you did, and what it caused.**

## The three parts

**Situation** anchors the feedback in a specific moment. "In your review of Thiago's calendar-sync
pull request on Tuesday." Not "lately", not "in general". A specific situation is what lets the
person picture it, and it keeps the conversation about that moment rather than about everything.

**Behaviour** is what the person did, as a camera would record it. "Your first comment was 'this is
wrong, redo it', with nothing about what was wrong." Words, actions, the absence of an action. No
adjectives about the person, no guesses about their intent.

**Impact** is what the behaviour caused, stated as something observable or as the speaker's own
experience. "Thiago didn't push anything for two days, and he told me he wasn't sure what to change.
He has since asked Yara to review his work instead of you." Impact is the part that gives the
feedback its reason. Without it, the person hears an instruction about style and nothing about why
it matters.

## Renata's feedback to Diego

Put together, what Renata said to Diego in his next one-to-one was this:

> I want to talk about the review on Thiago's calendar-sync pull request on Tuesday. Your first
> comment was "this is wrong, redo it", and it didn't say what was wrong. Thiago didn't push
> anything for two days, and when I asked he said he didn't know what to change. He's also started
> asking Yara for reviews instead of you, which means the person who knows the calendar code best
> isn't reviewing it.

Notice what is not there. Nothing about Diego being harsh, or arrogant, or bad with juniors. Nothing
about what Diego intended. Nothing about how other people review. **Each sentence is something Diego
could check**, and the last one connects the behaviour to something Diego himself cares about, the
quality of the calendar code.

## What SBI does not do

The structure gets the feedback said clearly. It does not decide what happens next. After the
impact, Renata stopped and asked Diego what he thought, which is the next section. Some versions of
the model add a fourth part, a request or a next step, and that is fine as long as it comes after
the person has had a chance to respond.

It also does not make feedback comfortable. Diego's face, when he heard the last sentence, showed
that it landed. **Clear feedback about something that matters is uncomfortable to receive, and the
structure is not there to remove that.** It is there to make sure the discomfort is about the right
thing: the behaviour and its effect, not an attack on who he is.

## Your task

Think of feedback you have wanted to give somebody, at work or elsewhere, and have not. Write it in
your notebook in three sentences, one per part. Then check:

- The situation is a specific moment, with a day or an event.
- The behaviour contains no adjective about the person.
- The impact is something observable, or your own experience, stated as yours.
- The person could check each sentence against their own memory.

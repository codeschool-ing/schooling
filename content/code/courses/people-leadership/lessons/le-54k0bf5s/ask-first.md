title: Asking before telling
version: 1

A well-built piece of feedback can still go badly if it arrives as a verdict delivered to somebody
who has not been asked anything. **Starting with a question, before the feedback or right after the
situation, changes what the conversation is.** It turns a correction into a joint look at
something that happened.

## Two ways in

Renata could have opened with the SBI from the previous section. What she actually did was say the
situation and then ask:

> I wanted to talk about the review on Thiago's calendar-sync pull request on Tuesday. How do you
> think that went?

Diego's answer surprised her. He said he knew the first comment was short, that he had written it
between two meetings, and that he had meant to come back and explain, and then the day got away
from him. He had not known about the two days Thiago lost, or about Thiago going to Yara.

Three things happened because she asked first:

- **She learned something.** The comment was not a style Diego defended; it was a lapse he already
  regretted. Her feedback could be shorter and land softer.
- **Diego owned it.** He named the problem before she did, which made the rest of the conversation
  his rather than hers.
- **The impact became news, not accusation.** Telling him about Thiago's two days, after he had
  explained, was giving him information he did not have, which is what feedback is for.

## Why it works

People are usually aware of at least part of what went wrong. A question lets them say it, and
**something you say yourself is far easier to act on than something you were told.** It also
protects the manager from the commonest mistake in feedback, which is being confidently wrong about
the cause. If Diego had explained that Thiago had asked for blunt comments, Renata's feedback would
have needed to change completely.

## When asking is a trap

Asking first is a technique, and it has a dishonest version. If the manager has already decided
what happened and asks "how do you think it went?" only to wait for the person to confess, the
question is a trap, and people recognise traps quickly. **Ask only questions whose answer could
change what you say next.** If nothing could, say what you have to say.

There are also situations where asking first is wrong. When the behaviour was serious, such as
something discriminatory said to a colleague, or a security rule deliberately broken, opening with
"how do you think that went?" makes it sound negotiable. Say what happened and what it means first,
then listen.

## Caring and challenging, at once

Kim Scott's *Radical Candor* (2017) gives a picture that explains why asking first helps. She draws
two axes: **caring personally** about the person, and **challenging directly** about the work.
Feedback that does both is what she calls radical candour. Caring without challenging is **ruinous
empathy**, the manager who never says the hard thing and lets the person fail. Challenging without
caring is **obnoxious aggression**. Doing neither is **manipulative insincerity**: the vague,
political non-feedback of somebody protecting themselves.

Asking first is one of the clearest signals of the caring axis, because it treats the person's
account as worth hearing. It does not weaken the challenge. Renata still told Diego exactly what the
comment had cost; she just told him after she had listened.

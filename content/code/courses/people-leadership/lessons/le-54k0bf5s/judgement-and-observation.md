---
title: A judgement is a conclusion; feedback starts before it
version: 1
---

Most feedback that fails was never feedback. **It was a judgement about the person, delivered as if
it were information**, and the person reacted to the judgement, which is the natural thing to do
with one. "You're careless" invites "No I'm not". "In the last two migrations there was no rollback
step" invites "Oh. Which ones?"

## The difference, concretely

A judgement is a conclusion the speaker has reached about somebody. An observation is what a
camera in the room would have recorded. **Feedback is built from observations, and the conclusion,
if it comes at all, comes last.**

| judgement | observation |
|---|---|
| "You're not a team player." | "You declined the last three requests to pair, each time saying you were busy." |
| "You're too negative in planning." | "In Tuesday's planning you said 'that won't work' to four of the six proposals, and offered an alternative for one." |
| "Your code is sloppy." | "The last two pull requests had no tests for the error paths, and both came back from review." |
| "You need to communicate more." | "The calendar-sync work slipped a week, and I heard about it from Helena on Friday rather than from you." |
| "Great job!" | "The runbook page you wrote meant Thiago fixed the disk alert alone at 3 a.m." |

The last row is not a mistake. Praise can be a judgement too, and lesson 9 is about why the
observation version is worth more.

## Why judgements fail

Three things go wrong when feedback is a judgement.

**It is arguable.** "Not a team player" is an opinion, and the person has a different opinion,
backed by everything they remember doing for the team. The conversation becomes a debate about
who is right, which nobody wins. An observation is not arguable in the same way: either the
pull-request reviews came back or they did not.

**It is about identity.** A judgement says what somebody is. People defend what they are much
harder than what they did, because changing what you are is not a thing anybody knows how to do,
and changing what you did next time is.

**It carries no instruction.** "Communicate more" leaves the person guessing what more would look
like. "I heard about the slip from Helena" tells them exactly what was missing: that they should
have been the one to say it.

## The test

Before giving any feedback, Renata runs one check: **could the person, hearing this, picture the
specific moment?** If not, she has a judgement and needs to find the observations it came from. If
she cannot find any, she does not have feedback yet. She has an impression, and an impression is
something to watch for, write down in the private notes from lesson 5, and come back to when there
is something concrete.

This is harder than it sounds, because judgements form quickly and the observations behind them are
forgotten. Renata's first impression of Diego's reviews was "he's harsh with Thiago". When she went
looking for what she had actually seen, she found two review comments and one remark in stand-up.
That was enough to give feedback, and it was much better feedback than the impression would have
been. The next section is how she gave it.

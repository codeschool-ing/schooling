---
title: Conflict with product, and disagreeing and committing
version: 1
---

The most frequent conflict an engineering manager has is with product: what to build next, how much
time goes to maintenance, how much risk is acceptable for a date. **It is a healthy conflict between two
legitimate concerns**, and it turns unhealthy when either side treats the other's concern as an
obstacle rather than an input.

## Helena and the waiting list

Helena, Agenda's product manager, wanted the waiting-list feature from lesson 4 extended to group
appointments, for a large clinic chain the sales team was close to signing. She asked for it in four
weeks. Diego said the booking code needed a month of refactoring before anything else was built on
it, because two recent incidents had come from it.

Both were right about their own concern. The clinic chain was real money. The incidents were real
risk. **Neither concern could be dismissed, and neither person could see the other's clearly**, which
is the usual state of this kind of disagreement.

## Making the trade-off visible

Renata's first move was to get both concerns onto one page, in numbers where possible: the revenue the
sales team expected from the chain, and the cost of the two incidents in hours and clinic complaints,
with Diego's estimate of the likelihood of another. She also brought out an agreement Agenda had made
with Helena the quarter before and written into lesson 2's page: roughly a fifth of the team's
capacity goes to maintenance and reliability, decided by the engineers, without needing to be argued
for each time.

With both on the page, the conversation changed. Helena could see that the refactoring was not an
engineer's preference but a risk to the same clinics she was selling to. Diego could see that the chain
was not a whim. They agreed on a version: the refactoring of the one module group appointments would
touch, two weeks, then the feature, another four. Helena told sales six weeks instead of four.

## Disagree and commit

Sometimes the trade-off is visible and people still disagree. One of Amazon's leadership principles
says it in three words, disagree and commit: argue your case fully while the decision is open, and once
it is made, commit to it fully, even if you still think it is wrong. The idea is older than Amazon; Andy
Grove described something similar at Intel.

The second half is the hard part. **Committing does not mean saying yes in the meeting and then working
slowly, raising objections at every step, or telling the team it was a bad decision.** That is passive
sabotage, and it is worse than open disagreement because nobody can address it.

In this case Diego still thought the whole booking code needed refactoring, not one module, and said so
clearly in the meeting. Once the decision was made, he planned the two weeks so that the module was left
in better shape than he had asked for, and did not mention the larger refactoring again until the next
quarter's planning, where he made his case properly. That is what committing looks like.

## When the manager disagrees with the decision

The same applies to the manager. If Helena and Otávio had decided on four weeks and no refactoring,
against Renata's advice, her job would have been to make sure the risk was written down, and then to
deliver the decision as the team's, the way lesson 7 described a "no" from above. **A manager who tells
the team "I argued against this" turns every decision from above into something the team can resist**,
and lesson 24 is about why that position, in the middle, is so hard to hold.

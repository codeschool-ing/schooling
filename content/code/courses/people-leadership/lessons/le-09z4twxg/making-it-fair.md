title: Making whichever format you use fairer
version: 1
---

No format is fair by nature. **Each can be made fairer by reducing the incidental thing it measures,
and by giving every candidate the same conditions**, which is the second part of structure from lesson
15 applied to the technical interview.

## For a take-home

- **Keep it short, and mean it.** Two hours, with a problem sized for two hours, tested by somebody on
  the team before it is sent out. If the team's own engineers need four, it is a four-hour exercise
  with a two-hour label.
- **Pay for it, or replace it.** Some companies pay candidates for take-home time, which changes who
  can afford to do it. Where that is not possible, offer an alternative, such as a pairing session or
  a walk-through of code the candidate has already written.
- **Talk about it afterwards.** A conversation where the candidate explains their decisions shows
  whether they understand the code, and gives credit for trade-offs the code alone does not show.

## For pairing and live coding

- **Give the problem in advance**, as Caju does, or at least the kind of problem and the language.
  Surprise measures surprise.
- **Let them use real tools**: their editor, the documentation, a search engine.
- **Brief the interviewer.** The same session can be supportive or hostile depending on the person
  across the screen. Yara and the other interviewers at Caju practise on each other before they run
  sessions with candidates.
- **Plan for nerves.** Say at the start that it is fine to think aloud, to be silent, to look things
  up, and to ask. Say it to every candidate, so it is part of the conditions rather than a kindness
  some candidates get.

## Accommodations

Some candidates need adjustments to show what they can do: more time because of dyslexia, a written
version of verbal instructions because of a hearing impairment, a break, a different format. **Caju's
invitation to the technical interview says what the session involves and asks whether the candidate
needs anything changed**, and the recruiter handles the request without the interviewers needing to
know why. Brazil's law on the inclusion of people with disabilities, Law 13.146 of 2015, requires
reasonable adaptations, and so do equivalent laws in many countries; beyond the law, an interview that
some people cannot take on equal terms measures something other than the job.

## The same conditions for everybody

The commonest unfairness in technical interviews is not the format. It is variation: one candidate
gets a friendly interviewer and a clear problem, the next a tired interviewer who had a bad morning and
explains the problem badly. **Structure reduces that variation**: the same problem, the same briefing,
the same rubric, scored independently before the debrief. Renata reads all the technical interview
write-ups for a round side by side, looking for the interviewer whose scores are consistently higher or
lower than the others', which is lesson 17's subject.

## Your task

Take the format you chose in the previous section. Write, in your notebook, the instructions a
candidate would receive before it, including what they may use, how long it takes and how to ask for
an adjustment. Then check:

- A candidate who has never done this format before would know what to expect.
- Nothing in it rewards practice at interviewing rather than practice at the job.
- An adjustment can be requested without explaining a medical reason to an interviewer.

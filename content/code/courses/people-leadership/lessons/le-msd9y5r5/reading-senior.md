---
title: What “senior” means once it is written down
version: 1
---

"Senior" is the most argued-about word in engineering careers, mostly because people use it to mean
different things: years of experience, a salary band, a title on a CV, a level of skill. **A written
ladder settles which of those it means at one company**, and at almost every company that has
written one down, it means scope and autonomy, not time.

## Two rows side by side

Caju's ladder describes the mid-level engineer, E2, and the senior engineer, E3, like this:

| dimension | E2, engineer | E3, senior engineer |
|---|---|---|
| scope | delivers features, from a defined problem to production | owns an area of the product over months, including its operation |
| autonomy | needs the problem framed; asks the right questions to finish framing it | turns an ambiguous problem into a plan other people can work from |
| influence | helps teammates when asked; reviews thoroughly | raises the team's standard: mentors, sets examples in review and design that others follow |
| craft | writes code and tests that rarely come back from review | makes design choices whose trade-offs hold up months later, and writes them down |

The difference is not that a senior engineer does the same things better. **It is that they work at
a larger scope, from a vaguer starting point, with an effect beyond their own work.** An E2 can be
an excellent engineer and still be an E2, because they need somebody to frame the problem first.

## Reading the autonomy row

The autonomy row is the one most promotion debates turn on, and it is worth reading carefully.
"Turns an ambiguous problem into a plan other people can work from" contains three tests:

1. **The problem was ambiguous.** If somebody else already broke it down, the person executed a plan,
   however well.
2. **The output was a plan.** Not just working code, but a decomposition, a sequence and a set of
   decisions that the team could act on.
3. **Other people worked from it.** A plan only the author understood is not what the row describes.

When Yara led the waiting-list work in lesson 4, all three were true. The problem arrived as "slots
freed late in the day stay empty", Yara produced the design document lesson 9 praised, and Thiago
and Fábio built from it. That is one piece of evidence against one row. Lesson 12 is about how many
pieces are needed, and over how long.

## Why years are not on the ladder

Some ladders used to include "five or more years of experience" in the senior row, and most have
removed it. Two reasons.

**Years measure exposure, not growth.** Five years of doing the same kind of work at the same scope
is one year repeated five times, and somebody with two years of steadily increasing scope may be
working at the senior level already.

**Years correlate with things a company should not reward for their own sake.** Time in the
industry is longer for people who started young, did not take career breaks, and were not held back
early. A ladder that counts years rewards those circumstances, and penalises people who had a child,
cared for a parent, changed careers or came to the field late.

## Titles across companies

One more caution. "Senior" at a small company and "senior" at a large one rarely mean the same
thing, because the scope available is different. An engineer who was senior at a ten-person startup
may have owned everything, and still come in as an E2 at Caju, where the areas are larger and the
expectations about operation and influence are written down. **The ladder describes the work, not
the person's past titles**, and a manager hiring or levelling somebody should read their evidence
against the row, not their previous business card.

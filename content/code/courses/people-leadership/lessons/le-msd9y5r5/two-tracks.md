Lesson 2 argued that management is a different job, not a promotion. **A written ladder is where
that argument becomes real or stays a slogan.** If the only way to reach the higher levels, the
higher pay and the larger decisions is to manage people, then management is a promotion, whatever
anybody says, and people who would be excellent senior engineers become mediocre managers to get
there.

## The individual-contributor track above senior

Caju's ladder continues past E3 for engineers who do not manage:

| level | scope |
|---|---|
| E4, staff engineer | an area that spans several teams, or a technical problem the whole organisation depends on |
| E5, principal engineer | the technical direction of a whole product line, over years |

Will Larson's four shapes from lesson 2 describe what E4 and E5 work looks like in practice: the
tech lead of a large effort, the architect of a domain, the solver sent to the hardest problem, the
right hand of an engineering leader. **Each is a job with as much scope as a manager's, exercised
through technical judgement and influence rather than through people reporting to you.**

## Where the management track sits

The management track at Caju begins at M1, an engineering manager of one team, like Renata. M2 is a
manager of managers, like Otávio. The figure in the first section draws M1 beside E3 and E4, not
above E3, and that placement is the point: **becoming a manager is a move sideways into a different
job, at roughly the same level of responsibility**, and the pay bands overlap.

That has consequences people should know before choosing:

- **An E4 can earn more than an M1.** Moving into management does not automatically raise pay, and
  moving back does not automatically cut it.
- **Promotion in management has its own row.** M1 to M2 is judged on the scope of the organisation
  managed, not on technical depth.
- **The tracks are connected.** People cross in both directions, as the pendulum in lesson 2
  described, and the ladder has to say how a person's level translates when they do. At Caju, an E3
  moving into management usually starts at M1, and an M1 moving back usually returns at E3 or E4,
  depending on their technical currency.

## The level everybody is expected to reach

Many companies define one level, usually senior, as the point every engineer is expected to reach
with time and support, and treat every level above it as optional. Caju does. **An engineer who
stays at E3 for a whole career, doing excellent work, is not failing.** The levels above it require
a kind of scope that not every person wants and not every company has room for.

This matters for the conversations in the next two lessons. A manager who treats every senior
engineer as somebody who should be heading for staff will push people towards work they do not
want, and will make the ones who are happy at E3 feel they are falling behind. Lesson 13 is about
growth that does not depend on the next level.

## Your task

In your notebook, draw the ladder of a company you know, or Caju's from this lesson, as two
columns. Then answer:

- Is there an individual-contributor level whose pay band overlaps the first management level?
- Could somebody reach the highest pay band in the company without managing anybody?
- If you moved from one track to the other tomorrow, what level would you be on?

If the honest answer to the second question is no, the company's ladder says management is a
promotion, whatever it says in words.

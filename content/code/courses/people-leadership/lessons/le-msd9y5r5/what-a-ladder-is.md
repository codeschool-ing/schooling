A career ladder, sometimes called a levelling framework or a career framework, is a document that
describes the levels in a job family and what is expected at each. **Its purpose is to turn "is
this person senior?" from a feeling into a question with a written answer** that the person, their
manager and a promotion panel can all read.

## Why write it down at all

Without a written ladder, levels exist anyway. They live in the heads of the managers who decide
promotions and pay, and each head holds a slightly different version. Three things follow, and
all of them are bad for the people being levelled.

**The same work earns different titles under different managers.** One manager's senior is
another's mid-level, and an engineer's career depends on who they happen to report to.

**People cannot tell what to work on.** "Keep doing what you're doing and we'll see" is the only
advice a manager can give when the target is not written down. Some people are good at guessing
what the unwritten version rewards, and they tend to be the ones who are already confident.

**Nobody can check a decision.** A promotion refused against an unwritten standard cannot be
appealed or even properly explained. Lesson 17 shows how unstated criteria let bias into hiring,
and the same happens to promotion.

Several companies have published their ladders, which is part of how the practice spread. Camille
Fournier made public the one she wrote at Rent the Runway, and the site progression.fyi collects
dozens of others. They differ in detail and agree on shape.

## The shape

Most ladders describe each level along a handful of dimensions. Caju's uses four, and they are
typical:

| dimension | the question it answers |
|---|---|
| scope | how big a piece of work can this person own: a task, a feature, an area, several teams? |
| autonomy | how clear does the problem need to be before they can run with it? |
| influence | whose work, beyond their own, gets better because of them? |
| craft | how good is the work itself: design, code, tests, operations? |

Each level is a row describing what is expected on each dimension. Reading across a row tells you
what a level is; reading down a column tells you how one dimension grows.

## What a ladder is not

**It is not a checklist.** A ladder describes what is typically true of somebody working at a level.
People who treat it as a list of boxes to tick produce promotion cases full of examples for each
box and no sense of whether the person actually works at that level. Lesson 12 is about building a
case that avoids this.

**It is not a schedule.** Nothing in a ladder says how long a level takes, and a manager who says
"you'll be ready in eighteen months" is making a promise the ladder does not support.

**It is not finished.** Caju revises its ladder every year, after the promotion cycle, using the
cases where the panel found the wording ambiguous. A ladder that has not changed in five years
describes the company as it was five years ago.

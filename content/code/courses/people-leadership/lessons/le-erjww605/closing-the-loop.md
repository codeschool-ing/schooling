title: Closing the loop, including when the answer is no

Closing the loop means telling the person what happened to the thing they raised. **It is the
step most often skipped, because the manager knows what happened and assumes the person does too.**
They do not. From the person's side, a topic raised and never mentioned again looks exactly like a
topic ignored, whatever work went on behind it.

## The three outcomes, and what to say for each

Anything a person raises ends in one of three ways, and each needs saying aloud.

**It was done.** Say so, and say what changed. "The runbook page for the disk alert is up, and Yara
is your backup for the next two on-call weeks." This sounds too obvious to need saying, and it is
the one most often skipped, because the person will notice eventually. They might not connect it
with what they said, and the connection is what teaches them that raising things works.

**It is still happening.** Say where it is and when you will next report. "I asked the platform
team about a second rota. They're discussing it on Tuesday; I'll tell you what they decide on
Wednesday." A loop can stay open for a long time if it is visibly open.

**The answer is no.** Say it, say why, and say what, if anything, is possible instead. This is the
outcome managers avoid most, because it feels like failing the person. It is far better than
silence. A clear no lets the person stop waiting and make their own decisions; silence keeps them
waiting for something that is not coming.

## Saying no well

Paula asked, in her fourth one-to-one, whether she could spend two days a week on a new project the
data team was starting, because she wanted to learn their tools. Renata asked Otávio, who said no:
the reminder service still depended on Paula, and the quarter's goal depended on the reminder
service.

Renata's reply, in the next one-to-one, had four parts:

1. **The answer, first.** "Otávio said no to the two days a week, for this quarter."
2. **The real reason.** "The no-show work depends on the reminder service, and right now that means
   it depends on you."
3. **What is possible instead.** "What I can do is make spreading the reminder knowledge the first
   thing we do, so that next quarter the answer can be different. And the data team runs a weekly
   session you could join from now."
4. **When it will be revisited.** "I'll raise it again with Otávio at quarter planning, in eight
   weeks."

What she did not do was soften the no until it sounded like a maybe, or blame Otávio in a way that
suggested she disagreed. **A no from above is delivered as the team's answer**, even when the
manager argued for yes. Lesson 24 comes back to this, because it is one of the hardest parts of
being in the middle.

## The loop that runs the other way

Closing the loop also applies to things the manager asks for. If Renata asks Paula for her view on
whether the planning meeting is still useful, and Paula gives it, Renata owes her an account of
what happened with it. "I took your point about the estimates, and we've dropped the second half of
the meeting" closes it. Asking for people's opinions and then never mentioning them again is the
same failure as leaving their problems unanswered, and people notice it just as quickly.

## Your task

Look at the promises page of your notebook, or, if it is new, think back over the last month of
your working life. Find one thing somebody raised with you, or you said you would do, that never
got a report back. Write the message that closes it, using whichever of the three outcomes is true,
and check:

- It starts with the outcome, not with an apology or an explanation.
- If the answer is no, it says why, and what is possible instead.
- If the loop is still open, it says when you will report next.
- It would make sense to somebody who had forgotten what they originally asked.

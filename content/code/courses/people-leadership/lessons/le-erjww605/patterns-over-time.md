One one-to-one tells you about a week. **Twelve of them tell you about a person, and a team's worth
of them tell you about the team**, but only if somebody reads them together. Most managers never
do, because each meeting is handled on its own and the notes are filed. The patterns are there,
and nobody looks.

## Reading across the notes

Once a month, on one of her kept mornings, Renata reads the last four weeks of every person's
one-to-one notes together, and keeps a short tally of themes. Not what each person said, but what
kept coming up. After her first full quarter, the tally looked like this:

Three things in it were invisible from any single meeting.

**Dependencies on Payments came up in five of seven people's notes**, sometimes as a blocker,
sometimes as a complaint about estimates that never held. No single person had raised it as a
problem in its own right, because to each of them it was one irritation among several. Together it
was the largest single drag on the team, and it went into Renata's next conversation with the
Payments manager, which lesson 22 describes.

**On-call came up often in the first month and rarely in the third.** The runbook page and the
backup arrangement had worked. That is the only evidence Renata has that the change helped, and
without the tally she would not have it.

**Growth came up for four people and never for Marcos.** That is not a problem in itself, but beside
his empty section in lesson 5 it was part of a picture Renata was starting to see, which lesson 19
takes up.

## Why themes need counting

A manager remembers the vivid. Diego's frustration with a design review was described at length
and with feeling, and Renata remembered it clearly. It was raised once. Paula's mention of the
reminder service being undocumented was a sentence, said calmly, and it was raised four times in
eight weeks. **Without a count, the loud thing wins over the repeated thing**, and the repeated thing
is usually the one that matters.

## The theme raised once and dropped

The hardest pattern to see is something raised once and never again. It might mean the problem
went away. It might mean the person decided raising it was pointless, which is what happened with
Paula's request in the first section of this lesson. The tally helps here too, because a theme that
appears once and is followed by silence is worth a question: "You mentioned the reminder service a
while ago and haven't since. Is that because it's better, or because you've given up on me doing
anything?"

## Skip-levels, briefly

Some companies add a second source of patterns: meetings between a person and their manager's
manager, called skip-levels. Otávio holds them with each of Renata's reports once a quarter. They
exist partly to catch what a manager is missing, including problems with the manager, and a new
manager can find them threatening.

They are better treated as a second instrument than as an audit. When Otávio told Renata, without
naming anybody, that two people had mentioned the planning meeting running long, it confirmed a
theme already in her tally. **When a skip-level tells you something your own notes never showed**,
that is the more useful finding, because it says something about what people find easy to tell you.

## Your task

Take the notes of the last month of one-to-ones you have had, as manager or as report. If you have
none, use the Caju team's from lessons 5 to 7. Make a tally of themes and count how many people, or
how many meetings, each appeared in. Then check:

- At least one theme appears more often than you would have guessed.
- At least one vivid conversation turns out to be a theme raised once.
- You can name one theme that was raised and then went silent, and what you will ask about it.

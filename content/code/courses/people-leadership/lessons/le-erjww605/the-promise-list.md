A one-to-one is a conversation, and conversations are forgotten. **What makes the meeting worth
having is what happens in the days after it**, and most of that is the manager doing what they said
they would. A manager who listens well and then does nothing teaches the person, more effectively
than any bad question, that raising things is pointless.

## Two promises that cost the most

Renata keeps the third part of her notebook from lesson 1, the promises page, as a plain list: one
line per thing she said she would do, for whom, and by when. In her first month it had eleven lines
on it. Two of them show why the list exists.

The first was from Thiago's one-to-one in lesson 6. She had said she would get a runbook page
written for the alert that woke him at three in the morning, and find a second person he could
call. She did both within the week: Diego wrote the page, and Yara agreed to be Thiago's backup for
his next two on-call weeks. Thiago mentioned it, unprompted, in a one-to-one a month later, as the
moment he decided the meetings were real.

The second was from Paula's. Paula had raised being the only person who understood the reminder
service, and Renata had said she would "think about how to spread that knowledge". That line sat on
the list for five weeks with nothing beside it, because it had no date and no first step. Paula did
not raise it again. **She did not need to: the silence had answered her.** When Renata noticed, she
apologised in the next meeting, and they agreed a concrete plan, which is the next section's
subject.

## What makes a promise keepable

The difference between the two was not effort or goodwill. It was the shape of the promise.

| keepable | not keepable |
|---|---|
| "I'll get a runbook page written for that alert by Friday" | "I'll look into the alerts" |
| "I'll ask the platform team about a second rota and tell you what they say on Thursday" | "I'll see what I can do about on-call" |
| "I'll talk to Otávio about the conference budget this week and come back to you either way" | "Leave it with me" |

A keepable promise has an action, a date and, when the outcome is not in the manager's control, a
promise about the report back rather than the result. **"I'll find out and tell you" can always be
kept. "I'll get you the budget" cannot**, because the budget is Otávio's decision.

## Saying fewer, keeping all

The easiest way to keep promises is to make fewer of them. New managers promise too much because it
feels like care: the person raises something and the manager wants to help. But every promise is a
debt, and a list of eleven open debts in the first month is a list the manager will default on.

Two habits help. The first is to ask, before promising: "Do you want me to do something about this,
or did you want to tell me?" Often the person wanted to be heard, and a promise is not needed. The
second is to say no plainly when the honest answer is no. "I can't change the on-call rota this
quarter, because the platform migration needs everybody on it, but I can make sure nobody covers two
holidays in a row" is a smaller promise, and a kept one.

## The list in practice

Renata reviews the list twice a week, on the mornings she kept free in lesson 1. Each line gets one
of three marks: done, in progress with a next step, or stuck. A stuck line gets a message to the
person the same day, saying so. **A promise you cannot keep is still a promise you can close**, by
telling the person what happened and why. What cannot be closed is a promise that was silently
dropped.

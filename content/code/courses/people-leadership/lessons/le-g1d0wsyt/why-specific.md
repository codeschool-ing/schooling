title: “Great job” carries no information

Positive feedback is usually treated as the easy half: say something nice, move on. **That is why
most of it is wasted.** "Great job" makes the moment pleasant and tells the person nothing they can
use. Specific positive feedback is the most efficient teaching a manager does, because it tells
somebody exactly what to do again, and people repeat what they understand far more reliably than
they avoid what they were warned about.

## What a vague compliment leaves out

When Paula wrote the summary of an incident for the clinics, Renata's first instinct was a message
in the team channel: "Great write-up, Paula!" It would have been sincere and harmless, and it would
have left three questions unanswered:

- **Great in what way?** Was it the speed, the tone, the accuracy, the length?
- **Great compared with what?** Better than usual for Paula, or what Renata expects of everybody?
- **Great for whom?** Did it matter to anybody, or was it just tidy?

Paula could not answer any of them from the compliment, so she could not deliberately do whatever it
was again. She would keep writing summaries the way she writes them, which is fine until the next
one is worse for a reason nobody noticed.

## The same structure, pointed the other way

Situation, behaviour, impact from lesson 8 works exactly as well for what went right. What Renata
said, in their next one-to-one:

> The incident summary you wrote for the clinics on Thursday. You opened with what had happened to
> them and when it was fixed, and put the technical cause in the last paragraph. Helena said two
> clinic owners replied thanking us, and neither asked a follow-up question, which almost never
> happens.

Now Paula knows what to repeat: lead with what the reader experienced, cause last. She knows it
worked, and how anybody could tell. **And she knows that her manager read it carefully**, which is
itself worth something, because it says the work was seen.

## Calibration: what “good” means here

The deeper reason specific praise matters is that it calibrates. **People learn what a team values
from what it notices.** If the only feedback anybody gets is corrective, they learn what to avoid,
and they never learn what the top of the range looks like. If praise is vague, they learn that
something was liked, and not what.

When Renata praised Yara's design document for listing the two options she had rejected and why,
she was not only encouraging Yara. She was telling the team, through Yara, that a design document at
Caju should show its rejected alternatives. Two weeks later Thiago's first design document had a
section called "what I considered and dropped". Nobody had asked him to add one.

## Praise the choice, not the trait

There is one more distinction, and it comes from research on children rather than engineers. In a
1998 study, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck praised some children for being clever and others for
working hard on a puzzle. The children praised for cleverness were more likely afterwards to choose
easier tasks, and did worse after a failure. **Praise for a trait teaches people to protect the
label. Praise for a choice teaches them to repeat the choice.**

In a team, that is the difference between "you're a natural at incident writing" and "putting the
cause last was the right call". The first is pleasant and makes the next hard incident a risk to
Paula's reputation. The second is something she can do again on purpose, whether the next incident
is easy or hard.

## Your task

Think of the last time you told somebody they had done well. Rewrite what you said as situation,
behaviour and impact, in three sentences, in your notebook. Then check:

- The behaviour names a choice the person made, not a quality they have.
- The impact is something you or somebody else saw.
- The person could repeat the behaviour on purpose next time.

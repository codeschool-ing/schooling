Corrective feedback goes in private, as lesson 8 said. Positive feedback has a choice, and **the
choice changes what it says.** Private praise is a message to the person. Public praise is also a
message to the team, about what is valued, and that is both its power and its risk.

## What public recognition does

When Renata thanks somebody in the team channel or in the fortnightly demo, she is doing two things
at once. She is recognising the person, and she is telling everybody else what kind of work gets
noticed on Agenda. Used deliberately, that second effect is one of the cheapest ways a manager has
to shape a team's habits.

The design document example from the first section worked because it was public. Renata wrote, in
the team channel: "Yara's document for the waiting list lists the two designs she rejected and why,
and it saved us an hour in review because nobody had to ask. That's the bar for design docs from
now on." The last sentence is the calibration, said out loud to the people it applies to.

## When public praise backfires

Three situations make public praise worse than private.

**The person dislikes attention.** Some people find public recognition genuinely uncomfortable, and
for them it is a small punishment presented as a reward. Ask, early in the relationship, how the
person prefers to hear that they did well. Renata asked everybody in their first one-to-one, and
Fábio said he would rather it went in the one-to-one notes than the channel.

**It credits the wrong person.** Public thanks to whoever presented the work, when somebody else
did most of it, is worse than no thanks at all, because the person who did the work sees it, and so
does everybody who knows. **Be precise about who did what.** "Thanks to Thiago and Paula for the
calendar sync: Paula found the time-zone bug, Thiago wrote the fix and the tests" costs one more
sentence than "thanks, team".

**It always goes to the same people.** Visible work, such as demos, incident leadership and design
documents, is easier to praise publicly than invisible work, such as reviews, mentoring and keeping
documentation current. A manager who only praises the visible teaches the team that the invisible
does not count. Lesson 13 comes back to this as a problem for promotion, because invisible work is
also harder to put in a promotion case.

## Positive feedback that names the level

The most useful positive feedback, especially for somebody who wants to grow, tells them where the
work sits against what is expected. "That's what I'd expect from a senior engineer" or "this is
beyond what your level asks for" carries more information than any amount of enthusiasm, because it
places the work on a scale the person cares about.

It only works if the scale is written down and both people can read it. Lesson 11 is about that
scale, and lesson 12 is about turning a year of this kind of feedback into a promotion case.

## Your task

For each person on a team you know, or each person at Caju, write in your notebook one piece of work
from the last month that deserved recognition and has not had it. For each, decide whether it
should be public or private, and why. Then check:

- At least one of them is invisible work: a review, help given, something maintained.
- Each names who did it precisely, not "the team".
- For anybody who dislikes attention, you chose private.

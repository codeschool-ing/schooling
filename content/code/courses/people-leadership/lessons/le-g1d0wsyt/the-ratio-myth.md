Two pieces of folk wisdom about positive feedback are repeated in management training everywhere.
One is a ratio: give several positive comments for every negative one. The other is a technique:
wrap criticism between two compliments. **The first was built on mathematics that was withdrawn,
and the second fails in a way anybody who has been on the receiving end will recognise.**

## The positivity ratio

In 2005, Barbara Fredrickson and Marcial Losada published a paper claiming that people and teams
flourish when their ratio of positive to negative emotions sits above about 2.9 to 1, and that a
mathematical model derived from fluid dynamics showed exactly where the line fell. The number spread
quickly, often rounded or altered, into advice about how many compliments a manager should give per
criticism.

In 2013, Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman published a critique showing that the
mathematical model did not support the claim: the equations had been applied in a way that made no
sense for the data. The journal that had published the original formally withdrew the modelling
part of it. Fredrickson accepted that the precise ratio could not stand, while defending the
broader idea that positive experiences matter.

The useful lesson is not that positive feedback is unimportant. It is that **no ratio tells a
manager how much praise to give**, and a manager counting compliments to hit a number is giving
praise for the wrong reason. The person can tell.

## The feedback sandwich

The sandwich puts corrective feedback between two pieces of praise: something good, the problem,
something good. The idea is that the praise softens the criticism. In practice, three things
happen.

**People learn the pattern.** After the second or third sandwich, any compliment from that manager
is heard as the warning before a "but". Positive feedback stops working on its own, because it has
become a signal that something bad is coming.

**The message gets lost.** A person hearing praise, criticism and praise remembers whichever part
they wanted to. Somebody anxious hears only the criticism; somebody confident hears only the praise,
and leaves thinking the meeting went well.

**The praise is not real.** Compliments chosen to cushion a blow are rarely the ones the manager
would have given on their own. They are vaguer, more general, and the person senses it.

## What to do instead

Give positive and corrective feedback separately, each when it is due. If Paula's incident summary
was excellent, say so on Thursday. If her estimate for the waiting-list work was off by two weeks,
say that too, in a different conversation or at a different point in the same one, without one
being used to carry the other.

This is easier when feedback is frequent, which lesson 8 already argued. **A manager who gives
small, specific feedback every week does not need a sandwich**, because no single conversation has
to carry the whole relationship.

## Honesty about the balance

There is a real point buried in the ratio, and it is worth keeping without the number. If a person
only ever hears from their manager when something is wrong, they will reasonably conclude that the
manager thinks little of them, whatever the manager actually thinks. Renata checks her own notes once
a month for exactly this: for each person, how many pieces of positive feedback and how many
corrective ones she gave. She is not aiming at a ratio. She is looking for anybody who has heard only
one kind, because that person is getting a picture of what she thinks that is not true.

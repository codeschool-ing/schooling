---
title: The first three months, written down
version: 1
---

"Settle in and get to know the codebase" is the most common instruction given to new engineers, and
the least useful. **A new person needs to know what is expected of them at each stage of their first
months, in terms concrete enough that they can tell whether they are on track.** Many teams write this
as a plan for the first thirty, sixty and ninety days.

## Lucas's plan

Renata wrote it before Lucas arrived and gave it to him on his second day. It mapped the paragraph
from the job description in lesson 14 onto three stages:

| by | expected | how anybody will know |
|---|---|---|
| 30 days | has shipped several fixes in the reminder service; knows the deploy and the alerts | merged pull requests; has deployed without help |
| 60 days | owns the reminder service day to day, with Paula as backup; has shadowed one on-call week | clinics' reminder questions go to him first; shadow week done |
| 90 days | has led a small piece of the confirmations work with Yara and Fábio; on the on-call rota | the piece shipped; first primary on-call week done |

Each row has a measurable outcome, so that a thirty-day check-in can say "on track" or "behind, and
here is why" without either person guessing. **And each stage builds on the last**: fixes before
ownership, ownership before leading, shadowing before on-call.

## The area flagged at hiring

The plan had one more line, which came from lesson 17's debrief: by sixty days, Lucas would have
written the summary of one incident for the clinics, with Paula's review, and by ninety days one
without it. It was the third must, explaining things to people outside engineering, turned into a
specific, supported piece of work.

Renata and Lucas agreed the line together in his first one-to-one, not imposed in a document. He said
it was the part of the interview he had felt worst about, which made it easier to talk about.

## Checking in

At thirty, sixty and ninety days, Renata spends one one-to-one on the plan itself: what has been
done, what is behind and why, and whether the next stage still makes sense. At thirty days Lucas was
ahead on fixes and behind on alerts, because there had been no incident to learn them from; they
moved alert training into the shadow on-call week rather than inventing an exercise.

**A plan is a starting point, and changing it is not a failure.** What would be a failure is having no
plan, and discovering at six months that the person and the manager had different ideas about what
the first three were for.

## When onboarding is not going well

Sometimes the thirty-day check-in shows somebody well behind for reasons that are not the onboarding.
That is a performance conversation, and lessons 10 and 19 apply, with one difference: the early weeks
are when a new person most needs to hear the problem plainly, because they have the least context to
notice it themselves. **Waiting to "give them time to settle in" when the plan says something is wrong
is the kindest-sounding version of the failure lesson 10 described.**

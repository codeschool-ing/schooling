title: The first week, and the first change in production
version: 1
---

A new engineer's first week has one goal that matters more than the rest: **a small, real change of
theirs reaches production.** Everything else, the meetings, the reading, the introductions, is
supporting that goal. A first week that ends without it ends with the person having learnt about the
team rather than having been part of it.

## Why the first change matters

The first change does several things at once. It proves the person's setup works end to end, from
their laptop to production, which surfaces every missing access and broken script while somebody is
paying attention. It teaches the team's real process, review, tests, deploy, by doing it once rather
than reading about it. And it changes how the person feels: **after their first change ships, they are
a member of the team who has contributed; before it, they are a visitor.**

## Lucas's first week

| day | what happened |
|---|---|
| Monday | laptop and accounts working at 9:00; a walk through the system with Paula; lunch with the team; the time-zone bug in his hands by 15:00 |
| Tuesday | reproduced the bug, found the cause with Paula's help; one-to-one with Renata |
| Wednesday | wrote the fix and a test; opened his first pull request; two rounds of review |
| Thursday | merged; deployed with Paula watching; the clinic that reported it confirmed the fix |
| Friday | wrote a short note on what he had found confusing in the setup, for the next person |

The first change reached production on day four. The Friday note became the first edit to Agenda's
onboarding document in a year: three steps that had changed and nobody had written down.

## What the first one-to-one is for

Renata's Tuesday one-to-one with Lucas set up what lessons 5 to 7 describe: the shared document,
whose topics come first, what gets written down. She also did three things specific to a new person.

**She shared the plan for his first three months**, the subject of the next section, so he knew what
was expected and when.

**She told him what the hiring panel had seen.** Including the area they had flagged, explaining
things outside engineering, and what she and Paula would do to help with it. Telling a new hire their
weak area from the interview might sound harsh. It is the opposite: it means the expectation is
explicit from the start and comes with support, rather than surfacing in a review six months later.

**She asked how he liked to receive feedback**, the question lesson 9 recommended for everybody.

## The things that go wrong

New hires rarely say when onboarding is going badly; they assume the problem is them. Three signs are
worth watching for in the first weeks:

- **They stop asking questions.** Usually because they asked some and the answers made them feel slow.
- **They work late.** Usually because they feel behind and do not want anybody to see.
- **They are quiet in team meetings.** Normal for a while, worth a question after a month.

None of these needs a confrontation. Each is a reason to ask, in the one-to-one, the kind of question
lesson 6 described: "What's been the most confusing part of the codebase so far?"

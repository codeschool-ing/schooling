title: What makes an interview structured
version: 1
---

An unstructured interview is a conversation: the interviewer asks what occurs to them, follows what
interests them, and comes away with an impression. **A structured interview asks every candidate the
same questions, drawn from what the job requires, and scores each answer against a scale written in
advance.** It feels stiffer, and it predicts later performance considerably better, which the next
section shows.

## The four parts

Structure is not one technique; it is four, and an interview missing any of them loses much of the
benefit.

1. **Questions come from the job.** Each question assesses something on the must list from lesson 14.
   A question that assesses nothing on that list is a question about the interviewer's curiosity.
2. **Every candidate gets the same questions**, in the same order, with the same follow-ups. Otherwise
   two candidates are being compared on different tests.
3. **Each answer is scored against an anchored scale**: a description of what a weak, adequate,
   strong and excellent answer contains, written before the first interview. The last section of
   this lesson writes one.
4. **Each interviewer scores alone, before any discussion.** Lesson 17 explains why the order of
   scoring and talking matters so much.

## How Caju's final round is built

Agenda's E2 role had three musts. Renata built the final round around them, plus one conversation
the whole company uses for every role, about how the person works with others:

| conversation | assesses | interviewer |
|---|---|---|
| running a service in production | the first must | Diego |
| writing and testing code | the second must, lesson 16 | Yara |
| explaining a trade-off | the third must | Helena |
| working with others | the company-wide conversation | Renata |

Each interviewer owns one area and goes deep on it, rather than four people each asking a bit of
everything. **Four shallow interviews covering the same ground produce four overlapping impressions;
four deep ones produce four pieces of evidence.** Helena, the product manager, interviews on the
trade-off conversation because she is the person who will be on the receiving end of those
explanations.

## Two kinds of question

Structured interviews use two kinds of question, and both have their place.

**Behavioural questions** ask about the past: "Tell me about a time you had to explain a technical
decision to somebody without a technical background." The premise is that what somebody did is better
evidence than what they say they would do. Follow-ups ask for specifics: what exactly did you say,
what did they decide, what happened next.

**Situational questions** describe a scenario and ask what the person would do: "A clinic owner asks
why the reminder was sent twice. What do you tell them?" They work better for candidates with less
experience, who may not have a past example, and they need an anchored scale more than behavioural
questions do, because a hypothetical answer is easy to make sound good.

## What structure costs

Structure takes preparation: the questions, the scale, a short briefing for interviewers. Renata
spent about four hours on it for the E2 role. It also feels less natural to interviewers, who are
used to conversations, and some candidates find it formal.

Both costs are real and both are small against the alternative. Lesson 14 counted 83.5 hours of
engineering time for one hire. **Spending four of them to make the other eighty produce evidence
rather than impressions is the best trade in the process.**

The anchored scale is the part of structure that does most of the work, and the one most often
skipped. **It describes, before any candidate is seen, what a weak, an adequate, a strong and an
excellent answer to each question contains.** Without it, "strong" means whatever the interviewer
liked, and four interviewers mean four different things.

## Helena's question

Helena's conversation assesses the third must: can the person explain a technical trade-off to
somebody who is not an engineer. Renata and Helena wrote the question together:

> Tell me about a time you had to explain a technical decision, with a real trade-off, to somebody
> without a technical background. What did you say, and what did they decide?

And three follow-ups, asked in this order whatever the answer:

1. What did they understand, and how did you know?
2. Was there a moment they did not follow you? What did you do?
3. Looking back, what would you say differently?

The follow-ups are fixed for the same reason the question is: so that every candidate gets the same
chance to show the same thing.

| score | an answer at this level |
|---|---|
| 1 | no real example, or an explanation in the same technical terms the engineer would use with another engineer |
| 2 | a real example, but the explanation stays technical, and no check that the other person understood |
| 3 | a clear explanation in the other person's terms, the trade-off stated as a choice between outcomes they care about |
| 4 | as for 3, and the candidate adapted when they were not understood, and can say what the other person decided and why |

Two things make it work. **Each level describes what is in the answer, not how good it felt.** And
the levels differ by observable steps: an example or not, in the listener's terms or not, checked or
not, adapted or not. Two interviewers listening to the same answer should land on the same number, or
one apart.

## Scoring with evidence

During the interview, Helena writes down what the candidate actually said, as close to their words
as she can. After it, and before talking to anybody, she gives a score and writes one or two lines of
evidence:

> 3. Explained the choice between a nightly batch and real-time sync to a clinic owner as "the
> calendar would be up to a day out of date, or the system would cost twice as much". Checked by
> asking which mattered more to her. Did not have an example of adapting when misunderstood.

**The evidence is the score's justification, and it is what the debrief in lesson 17 discusses.** A
score with no evidence, or evidence that is an impression ("seemed like a great communicator"), is a
score nobody can check.

## When an answer does not fit

Some answers fall between levels or outside the scale. Helena's rule: score the level whose description
the answer fully meets, and write what was missing. If an answer suggests the question itself is
flawed, for example because many candidates misunderstand it, that goes to Renata after the round, and
the question is changed for the next role, not mid-process for the next candidate.

## Your task

Pick one must from the job description you wrote in lesson 14. Write, in your notebook, a behavioural
question for it, three fixed follow-ups, and a four-level scale. Then check:

- Each level describes the content of an answer, not a feeling about it.
- The levels differ by something an interviewer could observe.
- Two people reading your scale would score the same answer within one point.

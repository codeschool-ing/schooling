---
title: The questions that predict nothing, and the ones you must not ask
version: 1
---

Some interview questions survive because they are traditional, fun to ask, or make the interviewer
feel clever. **They tell you little about whether the person can do the job, and some of them tell
you things you have no right to know.** Removing them is the cheapest improvement any interview can
make.

## Brainteasers

"How many piano tuners are there in São Paulo?" "Why are manhole covers round?" Questions like these
were famous at large technology companies for years. In a 2013 interview with the *New York Times*,
Laszlo Bock, then in charge of hiring at Google, said the company had found brainteasers to be a
complete waste of time, and that their main function was to make the interviewer feel smart.

The problem is not that they are hard. It is that they measure something the job does not ask for,
the ability to perform a particular kind of puzzle under observation, and they reward candidates who
have practised that kind of puzzle. If the job involves estimating, ask about an estimate the job
involves.

## The ritual questions

Three questions appear in interviews everywhere and produce rehearsed answers:

| question | why it tells you little |
|---|---|
| "Where do you see yourself in five years?" | candidates say what they think you want, and plans change |
| "What is your greatest weakness?" | the answer is a strength in disguise, prepared in advance |
| "Tell me about yourself" as the whole interview | it measures self-presentation, which is not on the must list |

None of these is harmful to ask briefly. The harm comes when they take the time that structured
questions would have used, and when the interviewer scores the polish of the answer.

## Trivia

"What does the third argument of this library function do?" tests whether somebody happened to read
that page recently. Engineers look things up all day; the job is knowing what to look up and judging
the answer. A question about a real problem from the job, with the documentation available, tells you
more.

## What you must not ask

Some questions are worse than useless: they discriminate, and in many countries they are unlawful. In Brazil,
Law 9.029 of 1995 prohibits discriminatory practices in access to employment, including requiring
pregnancy tests, and the Constitution prohibits discrimination on grounds such as sex, age, colour and
marital status. Most countries have equivalents.

In practice, an interviewer does not ask about:

- marital status, children, or plans to have them;
- age, religion, health, or sexual orientation;
- where the person's family is from, or their accent.

**Small talk is where these questions slip in**, unintentionally: "Do you have kids?" at the coffee
machine is still a question a candidate may reasonably think was part of the interview. Renata's
briefing to her interviewers says it in one line: if a question would not be on the sheet, do not ask
it, including over coffee.

## What to ask instead

For every question removed, the structured version from the first section asks about the job. If you
want to know how a candidate handles ambiguity, ask about a time they faced it. If you want to know
whether they would stay, describe the job honestly and let them decide. **The interview is for
evidence about the must list**, and anything else is either noise or something you should not know.

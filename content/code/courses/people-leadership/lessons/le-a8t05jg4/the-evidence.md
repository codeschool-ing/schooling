The case for structure rests on decades of research on how well different hiring methods predict
how people later do in the job. **Structured interviews consistently come out among the best
predictors available, and unstructured interviews well below them.** Neither is close to perfect,
which is the part people tend to forget.

## Validity, in one paragraph

Research on hiring measures a method's **validity**: the correlation between the scores candidates
got and how they later performed in the job, rated by their managers. A validity of 0 means the
method predicts nothing; 1 would mean it predicts perfectly. In practice the best methods sit
somewhere between 0.3 and 0.5, which is useful and leaves most of the variation in later performance
unexplained.

## Two large reviews

In 1998, Frank Schmidt and John Hunter published a review of 85 years of research on selection
methods. They estimated the validity of structured interviews at 0.51 and of unstructured interviews
at 0.38: both useful, structured clearly better.

In 2022, Paul Sackett and colleagues revisited those estimates, arguing that an earlier statistical
correction had inflated many of them. Their revised figures were lower across the board, and the
ranking changed. **Structured interviews came out at the top of the commonly used methods, at about
0.42, while unstructured interviews fell to about 0.19.** On their numbers, adding structure roughly
doubles how much an interview tells you.

## What the numbers do not say

Three cautions keep the research honest.

**Even the best method is noisy.** A correlation of 0.42 means that many people who interview well
do not perform well, and the reverse. An interview is evidence, not a verdict, and a hiring decision
made with humility about that is better than one made with confidence.

**The figures are averages across many jobs and studies.** They say structure helps in general.
They do not say a particular structured interview at a particular company works, which depends on
whether its questions actually reflect the job.

**Interviewers believe in their intuition more than the evidence supports.** Experienced interviewers
often report that they can tell within minutes whether somebody is good. The research on unstructured
interviews is the measurement of that intuition, and it comes out well below the structured version.
Renata's own first reaction to the research was that it applied to other interviewers.

## Why structure helps

The research measures that structure works more than why, but the reasons are not mysterious. The
same questions make candidates comparable. Questions drawn from the job ask about the job. An anchored
scale makes "strong" mean the same thing to every interviewer. Scoring before discussion stops the
loudest opinion in the debrief from becoming everybody's. **Each part removes a source of noise that
has nothing to do with the candidate.**

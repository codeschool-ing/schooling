**The Zachman Framework is a grid that classifies the descriptions of an enterprise by two things:
the question each description answers, and the audience it is written for.** John Zachman first
published it in 1987, in an IBM journal article about information systems architecture, and has
refined it since; version 3.0 dates from 2011. It is the oldest of the classic frameworks and the
one the others are most often compared with.

## Six questions across the top

The columns are the six plain questions anybody asks about anything, each with what it describes:

| column | describes | at Carcará |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What** | inventory: the things of interest | passengers, tickets, trips, buses |
| **How** | process: the transformations | selling, boarding, refunding |
| **Where** | distribution: the locations and networks | 26 stations, 140 routes, one cloud region |
| **Who** | responsibility: the people and roles | agents, drivers, passengers, the teams |
| **When** | timing: events and cycles | departures, the São João peak, the nightly Frota export |
| **Why** | motivation: goals and means | *every passenger boards digitally by 2027* |

## Six audiences down the side

The rows are perspectives, from the most general to the most concrete. Version 3.0 names them by
audience:

| row | perspective | the kind of description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **executive** | scope: lists of what matters |
| 2 | **business management** | the business model, in business terms |
| 3 | **architect** | the logical model of the system, independent of technology |
| 4 | **engineer** | the physical model: the technology chosen |
| 5 | **technician** | the detailed specification: code, configuration |
| 6 | **enterprise** | the running thing itself |

## Thirty-six cells

Each cell is the intersection of one question and one perspective. *What*, for the executive, is a
list of the main things the business deals with: passengers, tickets, buses. *What*, for the
architect, is a logical data model: the booking aggregate of lesson 2 without its database. *How*,
for the engineer, is the physical design of a process: lesson 2's sequence diagram with its
protocols. **Every description of an enterprise has a cell**, and two descriptions in the same cell
are two versions of the same thing.

The rows will look familiar. Rows 1 to 4 are IAF's contextual, conceptual, logical and physical
levels from lesson 9 with different names, and the resemblance is not an accident: Zachman's grid is
where the idea of separating *why the business cares* from *which product does it* first became a
diagram.

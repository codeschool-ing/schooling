or two of its audiences.** Placing them in the grid is a good way to see the course as a whole, and
to see what it has not drawn.

## The empty cells

The grid also shows what the course did not draw, and each gap is worth a sentence:

- **When · executive and business management.** There is no picture of Carcará's calendar: the São
  João peak, Christmas, the monthly close. It shaped half the scenarios, and it lives in people's
  heads. A one-page business calendar would fill it.
- **Where · business management.** No map of the 26 stations and 140 routes as the business sees
  them. It exists, on the wall of the operations room, and nobody counted it as architecture.
- **Who · engineer.** Who runs each container on call is in the incident tool, not in the
  architecture. Lesson 11 argues it should be next to the diagrams.

None of these gaps is a failure of the course; each is a choice about scope. **The grid turns
unnoticed choices into noticed ones**, which is all an ontology can do and most of what a checklist
is for.

**Draw it yourself.** Take the architecture documents of a system you know and place each in a cell.
Then look at the emptiest column and ask who would have needed a description there, and whether they
went without one or kept it in their head.

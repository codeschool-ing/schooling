**ATAM, the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method, is the Software Engineering Institute's method for
evaluating an architecture against its quality attributes**, published by Rick Kazman, Mark Klein and
Paul Clements around 2000. A full ATAM takes several days and a trained team, and few companies of
Carcará's size run one. It is taught here because **its ideas are inside every lighter review**, and
knowing where they come from makes the lighter ones better.

**Presentation**
1. **Present the ATAM** to everybody taking part, so that the vocabulary is shared.
2. **Present the business drivers**: the goals, the constraints and the qualities that matter, by
   somebody from the business. At Carcará, Celeste and Otávio.
3. **Present the architecture**, by the architect: the views of lessons 2 and 3, at the level of
   the scenarios.

**Investigation and analysis**
4. **Identify the architectural approaches**: the patterns and tactics the design uses, such as
   lesson 6's local fallback for boarding and the queue in front of purchases.
5. **Generate the quality attribute utility tree**: lesson 6's tree, with scenarios rated by
   importance and difficulty.
6. **Analyse the approaches** against the highest-rated scenarios: for each one, which decisions
   serve it, and what could go wrong.

**Testing**
7. **Brainstorm and prioritise scenarios** with a wider group of stakeholders, who usually add the
   ones the architects did not think of.
8. **Analyse the approaches again**, against the new high-priority scenarios.

**Reporting**
9. **Present the results**: the risks, the non-risks, the sensitivity points, the trade-off points
   and the risk themes, which the next section explains.

## Why two rounds of analysis

Steps 6 and 8 look redundant and are the core of the method. Step 6 analyses the scenarios the
**architects** thought of; step 7 brings in operations, support, finance and others, whose scenarios
the architects did not think of; step 8 analyses those. The difference between the two lists is
usually where the surprising risks are. At Carcará, the scenario that came out of step 7 was a
station agent's: *the station's internet link drops at 06:00 on the Friday before São João, with
forty people in the queue.*

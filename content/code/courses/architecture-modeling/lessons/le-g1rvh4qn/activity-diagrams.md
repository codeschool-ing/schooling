**An activity diagram shows a process as a flow of steps: what happens, in what order, where it
branches, what runs in parallel and, with swimlanes, who does each step.** It is the diagram for
questions like *what happens when a passenger cancels?*, where the answer is a procedure with
conditions in it.

## The notation

UML's activity diagram is a descendant of the flowchart with a few precise additions:

- an **initial node**, a filled circle, where the flow starts;
- an **action**, a box with rounded corners, named with a verb: *check departure time*;
- a **decision**, a diamond with one flow in and several out, each outgoing flow carrying a
  **guard** in square brackets: `[3 h or more before]`, `[less than 3 h]`. The guards must cover
  every case and must not overlap;
- a **merge**, also a diamond, where alternative flows come back together;
- a **fork** and a **join**, thick bars: after a fork, the flows run in parallel; a join waits for all
  of them;
- an **activity final**, a circle with a dot inside like a target, where the whole activity ends;
- **partitions**, also called swimlanes, the columns that say who or what performs each action.

The three lanes are the passenger, the ticketing system and the payment gateway. The diagram
encodes Carcará's cancellation policy, which Otávio wrote in a paragraph and nobody could quite
remember:

- A cancellation **three hours or more** before departure is refunded in full, and the refund and
  the seat release happen in parallel: the fork says that neither waits for the other.
- **Less than three hours** before departure, the ticket can be **changed** to another trip for a
  fee, but not refunded.
- The join waits for both the refund and the seat release before the confirmation e-mail goes out,
  so a passenger is never told *refunded* while the money has not been asked for.

Drawn, the policy had a hole the paragraph had hidden: **what if the trip has already left?** The
decision diamond needed a third guard, `[after departure]`, which leads to *no refund, no change*.
The paragraph had said *before departure* twice and never said what happens after it.

## Rules that keep it readable

- **Guards on every branch, and covering every case.** A decision with `[yes]` and nothing else is
  a question with half an answer.
- **One start and clear ends.** Several initial nodes make the reader hunt for where to begin.
- **Swimlanes for *who*, not for *where*.** Lanes for teams, roles or systems answer the question
  people actually ask about a process: *whose step is this?*
- **Stop at the level of the question.** *Refund* is one action here. How the refund is computed is
  another diagram, or a paragraph.

**Draw it yourself.** Draw the activity diagram for a station agent selling a ticket to a passenger
who pays in cash, including what happens when the receipt printer is out of paper. Use two lanes,
the agent and the ticketing system, and put a guard on every branch.

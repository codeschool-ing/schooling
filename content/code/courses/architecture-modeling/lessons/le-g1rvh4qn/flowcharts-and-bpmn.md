**The flowchart and BPMN describe processes too, and you will meet both: the flowchart because
everybody can read it, and BPMN because business analysts and process tools use it.** Knowing how
they map onto the activity diagram lets you read any of the three.

## The classic flowchart

The flowchart predates software engineering as a discipline. Its symbols are a terminator (a
rounded box for start and end), a process (a rectangle), a decision (a diamond with labelled exits),
input and output (a parallelogram) and arrows. It has no forks, no swimlanes in its basic form and
no precise meaning for anything subtle, which is both its weakness and why it survives: **it is the
one process notation a non-technical reader can follow without a legend**.

Use it for a reader like Celeste: a short procedure, a handful of decisions, no parallelism.

## BPMN

**BPMN, Business Process Model and Notation, is the OMG's standard for business processes**; version
2.0 dates from 2011 and is also an ISO standard. It is richer than an activity diagram in the
places business processes need, and process engines can run some BPMN models directly. Lesson 7 of
`enterprise-software` goes into executing them; here, the notation is enough:

| BPMN element | drawn as | the activity-diagram equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **start, intermediate and end events** | circles: thin, double and thick outlines | initial node and final node, with more kinds |
| **task** | rounded rectangle | action |
| **exclusive gateway** | diamond with an X | decision and merge |
| **parallel gateway** | diamond with a + | fork and join |
| **sequence flow** | solid arrow, within one pool | control flow |
| **message flow** | dashed arrow with an open circle at its start, between pools | a signal sent to another participant |
| **pool and lane** | a pool per participant organisation, lanes inside it | partitions |

The important difference is **pools**. A pool is a participant that runs its own process, such as
Carcará or the payment company. Inside a pool, steps are joined by sequence flows; between pools,
only messages can pass, because one organisation cannot tell another what to do next. That
distinction, which UML leaves loose, is exactly the boundary between *our process* and *their
system* that lesson 3 drew with a grey box.

## Which to use

- **A flowchart** for a short procedure and a non-technical reader.
- **An activity diagram** in a software design document, alongside other UML.
- **BPMN** when the process crosses organisations, when analysts will maintain it, or when a process
  engine may run it.

The flow is the same in all three. Choosing the notation is choosing the reader, as lesson 1 put it.

**A diagram shown whole is read in whatever order each person's eye happens to choose, and in a
meeting that means everybody is looking at a different box while the presenter talks.** Walking a
diagram is the habit of bringing the room through it in one order, on purpose.

## Build it up

A diagram with twelve elements shown at once is a wall. The same diagram built up in three or four
steps is a story: each step adds the elements that the next sentence needs, and nothing else.

On Monday, Tainá's context diagram appeared in three steps. First the passenger and the ticketing
system: *this is what passengers use to buy a ticket.* Then the payment gateway and Frota: *it
depends on two other systems, one outside the company.* Then the 26 stations and the link between
them and the cloud, in amber: *and this is the line the review is about.* By the time the amber line
appeared, every director knew what it connected.

Presentation software calls this an animation or a build. A deck generated from a model gets the same
effect from three views of it, each including a few more elements than the one before.

## Start where the reader stands

**Begin a walk at the element the audience recognises**, usually a person, and follow what that
person does. For the directors, the passenger buying a ticket; for the engineers, a request arriving
at the ticketing API. A walk that begins at the database begins where nobody in a non-technical room
can stand.

## One path at a time

Follow **one path through the diagram at a time**, and finish it before starting another. *The
passenger pays, the gateway answers, the ticket is issued* is one path. Jumping to the boarding app
halfway through leaves people holding half a story.

## Say the legend

Lesson 1 asked every diagram for a legend, and a legend on a slide is too small for half the room
to read. **Say what the notation means the first time it matters**: *dashed boxes are systems
outside Carcará; the amber line is the one at risk.* It takes five seconds and removes the question
that stops half the room following.

## Point, and say what you point at

*As you can see here* is the most common sentence in architecture presentations, and nobody can see
*here* on a call or from the back. Name the element, *the boarding sync service, in the bottom
right*, and point to it as well. Somebody listening on a phone has only the words.

**A diagram that is legible on the architect's screen is often illegible on a projector, on a call
and on a phone, which are where it is actually seen.** The diagrams in a document can be dense,
because a reader can zoom and take their time. A diagram on a slide gets the time the presenter
gives it, at whatever size the room allows.

## Fewer boxes, bigger words

- **About seven to nine elements on a slide.** A container diagram of fifteen boxes is a document
  page; on a slide, split it into the part the claim is about and a second slide for the rest.
- **Text at least as large as the slide's body text.** If a label has to be smaller than that to
  fit, there are too many labels.
- **Lines labelled with verbs**, as lesson 1 asked, but short: *pays*, *syncs*, *sends tickets*. The
  protocol belongs in the document.

1. **A title that states the claim**, in a full sentence: *every station-side failure goes through
   one link*, not *Station architecture*.
2. **The diagram**, cut down to what the claim needs.
3. **A legend**, short, for the notation on this slide only.
4. **A footer with the model's version and date**, and its owner, so that a slide forwarded in six
   months says how old it is.

The slide in the picture is the one the directors saw. How titles carry claims and how to build a
deck around them is `architect-communication` lesson 5; this course's part is the footer, which only
makes sense when the diagram comes from a model.

## For the people who cannot see it

Somebody in the room may not see the screen, and somebody on the call may be listening from a car.
The habits of walking a diagram serve them too: **name each element as it appears, say what the
lines mean, and say the conclusion in words.** When the deck is sent afterwards, each diagram needs
a text description, as the diagrams in this course have one, and the colour of a change needs its
word beside it, as the previous section showed.

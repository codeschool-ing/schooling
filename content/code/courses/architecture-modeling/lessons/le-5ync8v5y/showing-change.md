title: Showing what changes

**Most architecture presentations are about a change, and the audience has to see what is
different without comparing two pictures box by box.** Lesson 8 drew the baseline, the target and
the transitions between them; this section is about putting them in front of people.

## Keep the positions

The single most useful rule: **draw the before and the after with every element in the same place**.
If the ticketing system is in the middle of the first picture, it is in the middle of the second.
Then the eye finds the difference by itself, the way it finds the difference between two photos.

A tool that lays diagrams out automatically, such as most of lesson 12's, will move elements when
one is added or removed. For a before-and-after pair, fix the layout by hand, or draw the after
picture once and remove the new elements to make the before.

## Mark the change twice

The changed elements need to stand out, and **colour alone is not enough**: one man in twelve does
not tell red from green, a projector washes amber out, and a printed copy may be grey. Mark each
change twice, with colour and with a word or a shape:

- a new element in the highlight colour, labelled **new**;
- a retired one dashed and grey, labelled **retired**, rather than simply missing, so nobody wonders
  whether it was forgotten;
- an element that changes, with a short note on what changes, *now also sells at the counter*.

On Monday the directors saw the station back office dashed and grey with *retired* beside it, and
the second mobile link in amber with *new, 12 stations, before São João*. Nobody had to ask what
had changed.

## Show the steps, not only the ends

A change that takes a year has states in between, and those are what people will live in. Lesson 8
called them **transition architectures**. For the digital boarding programme, the question the product
team asked was not about the target but *what does a station look like in May, halfway through?* A
presentation of a long change shows **at least the next transition**, with its date, and marks
which capabilities each step adds or removes.

## Say what does not change

People hear *architecture change* and imagine their part of it moving. **Saying what stays the same
is part of showing the change**: *the passenger app, the payments and the boarding app are not
touched.* At Carcará it was the sentence that ended the questions from the Payments team before
they began.

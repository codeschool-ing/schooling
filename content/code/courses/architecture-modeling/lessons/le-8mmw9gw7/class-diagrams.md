title: Class diagrams: the things and how they relate

**A class diagram shows the kinds of thing in a model, what each one holds and does, and how they
relate to each other.** For an architect it is most useful at the level of the domain model: the
handful of concepts the business talks about and the rules that hold between them. The
`design-patterns` course explained aggregates in its lesson 12; here we draw one.

## A class

A class is a box in up to three compartments: the **name**, the **attributes** and the
**operations**. Either lower compartment may be left out when it is not the point.

Each attribute and operation may carry a **visibility** mark, `+` public, `-` private, `#`
protected, `~` package, a name, and a type after a colon: `- status: BookingStatus`,
`+ cancel(reason: String)`. In an architecture document, private details rarely matter; leave them
out and show what other parts of the system rely on.

- **Association**, a plain line: instances of one class are linked to instances of the other. It may
  carry a name, role names at each end and an arrowhead for navigability, which says which side
  can find the other.
- **Multiplicity**, the numbers at each end: `1`, `0..1`, `*` (any number, including none), `1..*`.
  Read it from the far end: in *Booking 1 —— 1..\* Ticket*, one booking has one or more tickets, and
  each ticket belongs to exactly one booking.
- **Aggregation**, a hollow diamond on the whole's end: a part-of relation where the part can exist
  without the whole. UML leaves its meaning loose, and many authors advise against using it at all.
- **Composition**, a filled diamond on the whole's end: the part belongs to exactly one whole and its
  life ends with the whole's. Deleting a booking deletes its tickets.
- **Generalisation**, a hollow triangle pointing at the more general class: *GroupBooking* is a
  *Booking*.
- **Dependency**, a dashed line with an open arrowhead: one class uses the other, for example as a
  parameter, without holding a link to it. **Realisation** is the same dashed line with a hollow
  triangle, pointing at an interface the class implements.

The diagram says several things in very few marks, and each could be checked against the code:

- A **Booking** is the aggregate root. It owns its **Tickets** by composition, so nothing else holds
  a ticket that is not inside a booking, and the multiplicity says a booking has at least one.
- Each ticket refers to one **Trip**, a departure on a date, and many tickets refer to the same
  trip. The arrowhead says a ticket can find its trip; a trip does not hold a list of its tickets,
  which matters, because the busiest trip at São João has forty-four seats and four hundred
  cancelled or expired tickets behind them.
- **Money** is marked `«value object»`, the design-patterns course's term, written as a stereotype.
  It has no identity: two amounts of R$ 89,90 are the same value.
- The booking depends on **PricingPolicy**, dashed: it asks for a fare when the booking is made and
  keeps no link to the policy afterwards.

**That is the whole diagram.** It has six classes because the question was *what is a booking made
of?*, not *what classes are there in the ticketing service?*. The service has eighty.

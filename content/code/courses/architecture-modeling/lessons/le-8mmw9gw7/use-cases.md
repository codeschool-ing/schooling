**A use case diagram answers two questions at once: who interacts with the system, and what goals
they reach with it.** It also draws where the system ends, which is the part people forget it is
for. It says nothing about how anything works inside.

## Four elements

- An **actor** is a role played by somebody or something outside the system: a passenger, a
  station agent, the payment gateway. It is drawn as a stick figure, even when the actor is another
  system, and it names a **role**, not a person: Tainá buying her own ticket is acting as a
  passenger.
- A **use case** is a goal an actor reaches through the system, named with a verb phrase from the
  actor's point of view: *buy a ticket*, *board a passenger*. It is drawn as an ellipse.
- The **system boundary** is a rectangle around the use cases, with the system's name. Everything
  inside is what is being built; actors are always outside.
- An **association** is a plain line between an actor and a use case it takes part in.

The actor who starts a use case to reach their own goal is its **primary actor**, and is usually
drawn on the left. An actor the system needs in order to finish it, such as the payment gateway, is
a **supporting actor**, usually on the right.

## Include, extend and generalisation

Two dashed arrows let one use case refer to another, and they point in opposite directions:

- **`«include»`** runs from the base use case to the one it always uses. *Buy a ticket* includes
  *take payment*, and so does *sell at the counter*. It is how a step shared by several use cases is
  written once.
- **`«extend»`** runs from the optional behaviour to the base use case it may extend. *Apply a
  discount coupon* extends *buy a ticket*: a passenger can buy without one. The base use case does
  not know about the extension, which is why the arrow points at it.

Actors can be generalised with a hollow-headed arrow: a *station agent* can do everything a
*passenger* can, at a counter, and more.

1. **Use cases that are screens or functions.** *Login screen*, *validate CPF*, *save to database*
   are steps, not goals. A use case is something an actor would recognise as worth doing.
2. **Arrows between use cases showing order.** A use case diagram has no time in it. *Search trips*
   followed by *choose seat* followed by *pay* is an activity diagram, lesson 4, drawn in the wrong
   notation.
3. **The database as an actor.** The booking database is inside the system. An actor is outside it,
   by definition; putting internal parts outside the boundary makes the boundary meaningless.
4. **Include used as decomposition.** Breaking *buy a ticket* into five `«include»`d pieces is
   writing a program in ellipses. Include is for behaviour that is genuinely shared.

## The text is the real use case

The diagram is a table of contents. **The use case itself is text**: who the primary actor is,
what must be true before it starts, the main success scenario as numbered steps, and the
extensions, the places where something else can happen. For *buy a ticket*, step 4 might read
*the system holds the seat for ten minutes*, and extension 4a *the seat was taken by another
passenger in the meantime: the system offers the nearest free seats*. Alistair Cockburn's *Writing
Effective Use Cases* is the standard reference for that text, and lesson 6 uses it again when it
writes functional requirements.

**Draw it yourself.** Draw the use case diagram for Carcará's boarding: the driver checking tickets
at the door, a passenger with no ticket buying one from the driver, and what happens when the phone
has no signal. Decide who the actors are before you draw any ellipse.

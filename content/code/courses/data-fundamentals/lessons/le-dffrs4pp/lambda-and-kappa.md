**Lambda and Kappa are two answers to one wish: a fast answer and a correct one from the same data.** The
lesson so far has shown why the two pull apart. A stream answers within minutes and has to bet on a
watermark; a batch job answers the next morning and sees everything. Both architectures are named here
so you can recognise them in a diagram or a job advert. Neither is built in this course.

## Lambda: two paths, merged

**The Lambda architecture runs the same computation twice, once in batch and once as a stream.** Nathan
Marz described it in 2011. Every event goes to two places:

- a **batch layer** keeps all the raw events and, every night, recomputes the answer from the whole
  history, late events included. Its answer is correct and hours old;
- a **speed layer** processes the same events as a stream and covers only the hours since the last batch
  run. Its answer is fresh and may be wrong at the edges;
- a **serving layer** merges the two: the batch answer for everything up to last night, the speed answer
  for today.

At Roda Livre, that is the live count of today's rides, corrected overnight by the job from
section 03. Passeio Público's late rides are wrong in the live count on Monday and right in Tuesday's
report, and nobody has to choose an allowed lateness of a day.

The cost is the obvious one. **The same logic is written twice, in two systems, by people who can make
two different mistakes.** When "a ride" changes its definition, both have to change on the same day, and
the morning the two answers disagree, somebody has to find out which code is wrong.

## Kappa: one path, replayed

**The Kappa architecture keeps only the stream path, and reprocesses by replaying the log.** Jay Kreps
proposed it in 2014, in an article whose title questions Lambda. It rests on the property of the log from
section 04: events are kept, in order, and a consumer can read them again from offset 0.

So there is one piece of code. When it changes, a second copy of the new version starts from the
beginning of the log and writes to a new output. Once it has caught up with the present, readers switch
to the new output and the old one is deleted. A backfill becomes a replay.

The cost moves to two places. The log now has to keep every event for as long as anybody may want to
reprocess it, which can mean years of events. And the stream code has to be right about late data on its
own, because no nightly job will correct it.

## Neither is a product

Both are patterns, and most real platforms are somewhere between them: a stream for the few answers that
need minutes, batch for everything else, and a raw copy of the events kept in cheap storage so either can
be run again. `streaming` builds the stream half and `pipelines-etl` the batch half. What this section
leaves you with is the question both are answering, which the next section turns into a decision.

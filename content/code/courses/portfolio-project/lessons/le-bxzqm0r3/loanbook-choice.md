Here is how the course's own project was chosen, including what was turned down, because the ideas
that lost explain the one that won.

The shape was a back-end service with a page, because it has the most moving parts to show every
track something. The problem came from the first of the four sources: somebody's previous job, an IT
room at a school, where the loan sheet on the door was always out of date.

| idea | first filter | second | third | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a help-desk ticketing system | too big: queues, e-mail, roles, SLAs | yes | yes | cut down, it became loanbook's idea of a record |
| a chat with the school's documents through a language model | yes | only for one track | needs a paid key to try | no |
| a clone of a well-known project tracker | too big, and lesson 2's problem | partly | yes | no |
| **an equipment loan register** | **yes** | **yes: an API, a rule, a deploy** | **yes: runs from the README** | **built** |

Three things are worth noticing. **The winner was the least impressive idea on the list**, and it
passed because it was small enough to finish and had one rule with real depth. **The first idea was not
thrown away**: it was cut down, and a ticketing system without queues, e-mail and roles is very close
to a register of who has what. And **the third filter killed an idea that was otherwise good**: a
project nobody can try without paying is evidence nobody can check.

Your table will have different rows. Write it anyway, because lesson 20's *why did you build this?*
is answered by the rows that lost.

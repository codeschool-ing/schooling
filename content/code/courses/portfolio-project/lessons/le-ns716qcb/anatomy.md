---
title: What a brief contains
version: 1
---

A brief is short. One page is plenty, and for a portfolio project half a page is usual. What makes it
useful is that it answers six questions, and each answer is something you will check against later.

| part | the question it answers | what goes wrong without it |
|---|---|---|
| **problem** | what is wrong today, in the words of the person who has it | you build a solution to a problem nobody has |
| **users** | who touches it, how many, how often, on what device | you design for yourself |
| **must** | the things it has to do for the first version to count | the first version never ends |
| **must not** | the things that would make it harmful or useless | a rule is discovered after it is broken |
| **out of scope** | what it will not do, said out loud | every conversation adds a feature |
| **done when** | the checks that say it is finished | nobody can say whether it is finished |

Two of these rows do more work than they look. **Must not** is where the interesting rules hide: *an
item must never be lent to two people at once* is one line in a brief and the hardest part of
loanbook. And **out of scope** is the only defence a project of one person has against growing
forever; lesson 6 is about using it.

A brief is not a specification. It does not say which database or which framework. Those are your
decisions, and lesson 16 writes them down separately, with their reasons.

---
title: The questions to ask back
version: 1
---

The gaps in a message are filled by asking, not by guessing. Five questions cover most of them, and
each maps to a part of the brief. These were loanbook's, with the answers Marta gave:

| question | the part it fills | Marta's answer |
|---|---|---|
| How will you know it is working, a month from now? | done when | "When nobody turns up for something that is already out, and the list matches the shelf." |
| Who would use it, and on what? | users | "About forty teachers, from the staffroom PC or their phones. Me, from the IT room." |
| What must never happen? | must not | "The same thing lent twice. And it can't lose track of what's out." |
| If it could do only one thing, which? | must | "Show what's out and who has it. The rest can wait." |
| What happens when something is late today? | must, problem | "I walk around asking. It would help just to see what's late." |

Three things about how to ask. **Ask about outcomes, not features**: *how will you know it worked*
gets a better answer than *do you want reminders*. **Ask what must never happen**, because people
describe what they want and forget what they fear, and the fear is usually the rule. And **ask for
the one thing**: *if it could do only one thing* is the question that cuts reminders, reports and the
library without anybody having to say no.

If your project has no real person behind it, answer the five yourself, in writing, before you start.
It is weaker than a real answer and much stronger than none.

**Choosing IaaS, PaaS or SaaS is choosing which security work stays on your list. The work changes
shape as you go up, it gets lighter, and the list never gets empty.** The `cloud` course's lesson 1
drew the line at three heights. This lesson takes one application of the clinic, the booking system,
and writes down what a security engineer would have to check if it ran on each.

The wrong picture is that a higher model is a safer one, so SaaS means "secured by the vendor". What a
higher model removes is **work**, and the work it removes is mostly the work people were doing well:
patching a kernel, hardening a runtime. What it leaves is the work people get wrong in every model:
**who can sign in, and what they can see**.

## Reading the drawing

Each list keeps the rows above its line, written as things to check rather than layers of a stack:

- **IaaS**, a virtual machine the clinic runs: the operating system and its patches, the network rules
  in front of it, how the machine itself reaches AWS, the runtime and the application, and then the
  data and the access, which every list ends with.
- **PaaS**, a function AWS runs for the clinic: which runtime version it uses, its code and
  dependencies, the permissions of the role it runs as, who may call it, the data and the access.
- **SaaS**, a booking product the clinic rents: who has accounts, what they may share and with whom,
  how long the records are kept, which other products are connected to it, the data and the access.

Two consequences matter for the rest of the lesson. First, **the items change shape, not just
number**. "Which ports are open" is an IaaS question; a function has no ports to open, and its
equivalent is "who may invoke it". The next two sections ask each list's most typical question in
your lab, and the fourth asks the SaaS one from documentation. Second, **every list ends with the same
two items**, and no model moves them: that is the `cloud` course's top two rows, seen from the to-do
list rather than from the stack.

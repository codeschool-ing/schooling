**A company that runs on two providers, or on one provider and its own data centre, has three
things to defend: the two sides and the join between them. The join belongs to neither side's
tooling, and that is why it is where control is thinnest.** The `cloud` course's lesson 2 measured
what a seam costs in time and money. This lesson is about what it leaves unguarded.

The wrong picture is that two clouds are safer than one, the way two locks are safer than one. For
availability that can be true. For security it runs the other way: **the exposure of two
environments adds up, and the defences do not**. A guardrail written in AWS's policy language does
nothing in Google Cloud. A detection rule tuned to CloudTrail never reads Azure's activity log. Each
side is as well defended as its own team made it, and the join is defended by whoever remembered it.

## Four things cross every seam

| what crosses | the usual way | what goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **an identity** | a job on one side needs to act on the other | a long-lived key, copied into the other side's configuration, that nobody on either side owns |
| **a route** | a VPN or a private link between networks | the other network becomes "inside", and a rule that trusted a private range now trusts all of it |
| **a record** | each side logs its own half | one action leaves two records, in two formats, in two places, and nobody joins them |
| **a policy** | each side has its own guardrails | a control decided once is enforced on one side only, and nobody notices the other |

**The identity is the one that hurts most often.** A network seam is drawn on a diagram and gets
reviewed. A key pasted into a scheduled job on another provider appears on no diagram at all: it is
a string in a configuration file, it never expires, and the team that created it in one console
cannot see where it is used from the other. The next two sections are that key, and the way to
remove it.

## Who owns the join

Ask of each seam: **if this breaks, or leaks, whose phone rings?** If the answer is "the team that
set it up", and that team has moved on, the seam is unowned. The clinic has one of those already.
Its reports run in Google Cloud, where bia's dashboards live, and every night a job there copies the
day's appointment export out of an S3 bucket. caio wrote the job two years ago with an access key
he created for it. He has not looked at either half since.

Nothing about that arrangement is a misconfiguration on its own. AWS's side is a user with a policy;
Google Cloud's side is a job with a secret. Each would pass a review of its own provider. **The flaw
only exists across the two**, which is the definition of a seam.

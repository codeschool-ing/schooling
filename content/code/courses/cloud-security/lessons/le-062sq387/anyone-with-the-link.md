**A link that opens a file for anyone who has it is a key written as an address.** Whoever holds the
address holds the file, and an address is copied, forwarded, pasted into a chat and kept in an
e-mail archive without anybody thinking of it as handing over a key. Every service in this lesson's
title offers such a link, and each lets an administrator narrow it, if the account has an
administrator.

The wrong idea is that a link nobody publishes is private because nobody can guess it. The address
is long and random, so guessing is not the risk. **The risk is everybody it is given to, and
everybody they give it to**, for as long as it works. A link sent to the accountant in March still
opens the file in October, from the accountant's intern's laptop.

## The two questions to ask of any shared link

1. **Who can open it?** Named people who must sign in, anybody in the organisation, or anybody at
   all.
2. **Until when?** A date after which it stops working, or never.

And a third, for whoever is defending: **can I see that it exists, and take it back?** On a company
account the answer is yes. On a personal account the company is not asked.

## How the services name the audiences

This table is taken from each provider's own documentation and **was not run here**: none of these
services has an imitation in the lab, and the labels change with each redesign of their screens. The
shape is what lasts, and it is the same everywhere: a narrow choice, an organisation-wide one, and an
open one.

| service | the open choice | the narrower choices | what a company administrator can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| OneDrive and SharePoint | *Anyone* links, with no sign-in | people in the organisation; people with existing access; specific people | limit the tenant to organisation-only or guest sharing, and require *Anyone* links to expire after a set number of days |
| Google Drive | *Anyone with the link* | restricted to people added by name; the Workspace organisation | turn off sharing outside the organisation, or allow it only to listed domains |
| Dropbox | a shared link anyone can open | on team plans, team members only | on team plans, restrict links to the team; passwords and expiry dates on links are paid features |
| Box | *People with the link* | people in the company; invited people only | disable open links for the whole enterprise |
| iCloud Drive | *Anyone with the link* | only people you invite | nothing: a personal Apple Account has no company administrator |

**Read the last column twice.** Every control in it belongs to the company's own tenant. bia's
personal Google Drive is a different account from the clinic's Google Workspace, and no setting in
the clinic's tenant reaches it. That is why the first row of defence against shadow storage is giving
people a company service that is as easy as their own, rather than a rule against their own.

## What the link does not tell you

An open link also removes the record. When a file is shared with named people who sign in, the
provider can tell the owner who opened it. When it is shared with *anyone*, the most a log can say
is that somebody with the link opened it from some address. **A defender who cannot tell the
accountant from a stranger has to treat every open as a stranger's.** Lesson 15 makes the same
point about the clinic's own logs: a record is only as useful as the identity in it.

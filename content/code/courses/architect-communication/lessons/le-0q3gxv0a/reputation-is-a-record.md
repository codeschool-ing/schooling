title: A reputation is a record, not a brand

**A technical reputation is what people expect of you when you are not in the room, and it is built
from work they could see, not from what you say about yourself.** The phrase "personal brand" suggests
the opposite: that reputation is marketing, managed with posts and profiles. Engineers who try that are
usually found out by other engineers within a conversation, because the record behind it is thin.

## Where Lívia's came from

By the end of the year covered by this course, people across Marola expected certain things of Lívia:
that a proposal of hers would have its alternatives honestly compared, that she would say no with a
reason and an alternative, that a postmortem she ran would not end with somebody blamed. None of that
came from her saying so. It came from documents people had read:

- the replica one-pager and proposal from lesson 2, which other teams copied as a template;
- the risk arithmetic from lesson 4, which Caio forwarded to the board;
- the diagnosis for logistics from lesson 7, which ended an argument nobody had been able to end;
- the 6 March postmortem from lesson 15, which support now uses to answer customers.

**Every document in this course was also, quietly, a piece of reputation.** That is the most useful way
to see the subject: not as an extra activity, but as the visible residue of doing the work well and
writing it down.

## Inside first

A reputation outside the company that is not matched inside it is fragile. The colleagues who work
with you every day are the people whose opinion becomes references, promotions and the next
interesting problem. The order that works is the one in the figure: **the team, then the company, then
the community**, each one built on evidence from the ring inside it.

## Keep your own record

Memory of one's own work is poor, and other people's is worse. Julia Evans, an engineer who writes
widely about her craft, popularised the **brag document**: a running list of what you did, why it
mattered and what came of it, updated every few weeks. The name is deliberately provocative; the
content is plain facts. Lívia's has one line per piece of work:

> **March.** Wrote the connections RFC after the 6 March incident; all seven teams adopted quotas by 1
> May; no connection-limit incident since.

It is the source for her performance review, for the talk in a later section of this lesson, and for
remembering, in a bad week, that the good ones happened.

title: Community: giving more than you take

**The technical community an engineer belongs to (open-source projects, meetups, forums, the people who
answer questions online) runs on people giving a little more than they take.** Contributing is how a
reputation outside the company grows, and it is also how an engineer stays connected to how other
people solve the problems they have.

## Small contributions count

The picture of an open-source contributor as somebody writing large features keeps many engineers from
starting. The contributions maintainers value most are often small:

- **a bug report with a minimal way to reproduce it**, which saves a maintainer an hour;
- **a fix to the documentation** that confused you, while you still remember why;
- **an answer to somebody else's question** in the project's forum or issue tracker;
- **a test** for a behaviour you depend on, so that it is not broken by accident.

After 6 March, Lucas found that the connection pooler Marola uses documented its per-client limit in a
way that had misled two teams. He sent a two-paragraph correction to its documentation. It was merged in
a week, and it is the kind of contribution that gets remembered by the people who maintain a project.

## Check before contributing

Most employment contracts say something about code written for work, and many companies have a policy
for open-source contributions. **Read it, or ask, before contributing anything that came from work**:
code, configuration, or even a detailed bug report that describes the company's systems. Marola's
policy allows documentation fixes and bug reports freely and asks engineers to check with their lead
before contributing code.

## Credit, given generously

Reputation in a community is damaged fastest by taking credit for other people's work. Lívia's article
and talk named the people who did the work: Bruna's team built the quotas, Lucas wrote the alert, Paulo
fixed the job. **"We" when the team did it, "I" only for what you did yourself**, and names wherever the
people involved are happy to be named. Credit given is noticed, by the people named and by everybody
who sees how the speaker treats colleagues.

## Mentoring outside

The mentoring from lesson 10 does not have to stop at the company's edge. Answering a junior developer's
question at a meetup, reviewing somebody's first open-source pull request kindly, or helping a student
with a portfolio project are the same skills, offered to people who have no other way to reach a senior
engineer. They are also how the next generation of the community decides what kind of engineers they
want to be.

**A good technical talk teaches one thing an audience of strangers can use, through a story they can
follow, and it is rehearsed until the speaker is bored of it.** Most of lesson 5 applies, with two
differences: the audience is deciding nothing, so the story is about a lesson rather than a decision;
and they owe the speaker no attention at all.

## Start small and close to home

Lívia's first talk was not at a conference. It was fifteen minutes at a monthly developers' meetup in
Recife, about the same incident as her article. A local meetup is the right first venue for almost
everybody: the audience is friendly, the slot is short, the organisers are usually looking for
speakers, and a talk given once is much easier to give a second time somewhere larger.

## The proposal

Conferences select talks from proposals, usually through a *call for papers*. The proposal is a short
document read by a committee with many others to read, so lesson 1 applies in full. A proposal that
gets read says, in this order:

1. **The problem**, in the audience's terms: "Your batch jobs and your checkout share a database."
2. **What the audience will leave with**: "three changes that stop one service starving another, and
   how to know you need them before an incident tells you."
3. **Who it is for**: "engineers running more than one service on one database; no specific
   technology assumed."
4. **Why the speaker**: one line. "I ran the postmortem and wrote the quotas."

A proposal that leads with the speaker's biography or the company's history is the technology-list trap
from lesson 5, in another form.

## Rehearsal

**Rehearse aloud, with a clock, at least three times**, and once in front of somebody who will be
honest. Lívia's first run of the fifteen-minute talk took twenty-three minutes; the cut was a section on
how PostgreSQL counts connections, which was interesting to her and to nobody in the audience.

Two habits from experienced speakers are worth copying:

- **Know the first two minutes by heart.** Nerves are worst at the start, and a memorised opening gets
  the speaker past them.
- **Plan what to cut if time runs short**, and mark it in the notes. Talks start late; the speaker who
  has already decided what goes ends on time without rushing.

## Questions from strangers

Lesson 5's rules about questions hold, with one more: some questions at a public talk are not
questions. **"That's more of a comment than a question, but…" deserves a thank-you and a short answer**,
and an offer to continue afterwards. The rest of the room came for the talk, not for the exchange.

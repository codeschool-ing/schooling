title: A development plan that gets used
version: 1
---

**A development plan is one goal, the evidence that would show it was reached, and a few activities
that produce that evidence, reviewed on a schedule.** Most plans fail by being a list of courses to
take and books to read, written once in a meeting and opened again a year later at the performance
review.

## Diego's plan

Written by Diego after the first conversation, edited once with Lívia, and shared with Bruna:

| | |
|---|---|
| **goal** | take a feature from idea to production myself, without step-by-step checking |
| **evidence** | by October: one feature where I wrote the design document, broke down the work, led the review, and shipped it; Bruna's review of it says she did not need to step in |
| **activity 1** | write the design document for the delivery-slot change by 30 June; Lívia reviews it |
| **activity 2** | review two pull requests a week from people outside my usual pairings |
| **activity 3** | ask for help after two hours stuck, not a day; note each time in my own log |
| **check-ins** | every two weeks with Lívia; a review with Lívia and Bruna on 30 September |

Every line is checkable. **"Improve communication" is not a goal; "lead the review of my own feature
and have it go through without Bruna stepping in" is.** The evidence row is the most important,
because it is what turns a plan into something two people can agree has been met.

## Most of it is work, not training

A widely quoted rule of thumb from leadership-development practice says people learn about 70% from
challenging work, 20% from other people, and 10% from formal training. It came from interviews with
executives at the Center for Creative Leadership in the 1980s, and the percentages were never a
measurement; take them as a shape, not a law. The shape is right for engineers: **the activities that
grow somebody are mostly assignments**, and the mentor's most useful contribution is often getting
the right assignment, which is the sponsoring role from the first section.

Diego's plan has no course in it. It has one assignment, one habit that exposes him to other
people's work, and one rule about asking for help.

## Reviewing it

Every two weeks, the first five minutes of the check-in go to the plan: what happened, what got in
the way, whether anything should change. Plans change; the goal changes less often. If an activity
has not happened for two check-ins running, either it is the wrong activity or something is in the
way, and both are worth knowing.

The check-in on 1 July found that the design document was done, a day late, and that Diego had asked
for help after two hours four times. Three of the four questions had been answered in under five
minutes. **That was the evidence that the third activity was working**, and it came from his own log,
not from anybody's impression.

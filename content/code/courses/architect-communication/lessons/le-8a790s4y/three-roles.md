**Three different things are called mentoring, and they ask different things of the person doing
them: advising from experience, helping somebody find their own answer, and using your standing to
open doors for them.** A senior engineer who knows only the first gives the same advice to everybody
and wonders why some people do not grow.

A common way to put it: **a mentor talks *to* you, a coach talks *with* you, and a sponsor talks
*about* you**, in rooms you are not in.

| | what it is | when it fits | what it costs the senior person |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mentoring** | sharing what you know and have seen: "when I had that problem, this worked" | the other person lacks knowledge or context you have | time, and the discipline not to turn every conversation into a story about yourself |
| **coaching** | asking questions until the other person finds their own answer | they have the knowledge and need to think it through, or to decide | patience: it is slower than telling them, and it feels unproductive |
| **sponsoring** | naming them for work, recommending them, defending them when they are not there | they are ready for more than they are being given | your reputation, which you stake on them |

## Diego

Diego joined Marola's checkout team eight months ago as a junior developer. He is quick, writes
careful code, and asks very few questions, which Bruna has noticed and is not sure how to read. In
June he asked Lívia whether she would mentor him. Bruna was glad: she has five engineers and Black
Friday to plan.

Lívia's first decision was which of the three Diego needed, and the honest answer was **all three
at different moments**. Mentoring when he lacks context ("why does checkout write to a separate
outbox table?"). Coaching when he has the knowledge and needs to decide ("which of these two designs
would you pick, and why?"). Sponsoring when he is ready for more than he is being given ("Diego
should lead the delivery-slot change; I'll review it with him").

## The default, and why it fails

Most senior engineers default to mentoring, because telling is fast and it feels like helping. It is
the right mode less often than it feels. **A junior who is always told the answer learns the answers,
not how to find them**, and the questions they bring stay the same size for a year. Coaching is
slower in the meeting and faster over the year.

Sponsoring is the one most often missing, and it is the one with the clearest effect on careers.
Research by Sylvia Ann Hewlett and colleagues on sponsorship in large companies found that people
with a senior sponsor were more likely to ask for and receive stretch assignments and pay rises than
equally qualified people without one. A sponsor does not make the junior better; a sponsor makes the
junior's work visible to the people who decide.

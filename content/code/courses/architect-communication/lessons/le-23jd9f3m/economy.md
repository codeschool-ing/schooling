title: Economy: cutting what carries nothing

**Every word costs the reader a little attention, so a word that carries nothing is a small tax on
the ones that do.** Economy is not brevity for its own sake: the precise sentence in the last
section was often longer than the vague one. It is removing what does not work.

## Five kinds of word that do no work

1. **Throat-clearing.** "I wanted to reach out to give a quick update regarding…", "As you may be
   aware…". The reader knows you are writing to them; start with the thing.
2. **Stacked hedges.** "It seems that it could possibly be the case that…". One honest hedge
   carries the uncertainty; three of them make the writer sound unsure of everything, including
   the parts they are sure of.
3. **Nouns made of verbs.** "Perform an analysis of", "carry out the implementation of", "make a
   decision about". The verb is hiding inside a noun: analyse, implement, decide.
4. **Passive voice that hides the actor.** "The deploy was approved" invites the question "by
   whom?", which is usually the point of the sentence. "Bruna approved the deploy" answers it.
   Passive is fine when the actor genuinely does not matter: "the table is backed up every night".
5. **Repetition of what the reader just read.** "As mentioned above", a summary of the previous
   paragraph, or a closing line that says the same thing again in a firmer voice.

## A paragraph, cut

This is a paragraph from the first draft of Lívia's proposal, 86 words long:

> I wanted to provide a quick update regarding the situation with the checkout system. As some of
> you may be aware, we have been experiencing some issues on Friday evenings. After performing a
> thorough analysis of the problem, it seems that it could possibly be related to the fact that the
> route planner is sharing the database with checkout. We believe that it might be a good idea to
> make a decision about separating them in the near future, as this would potentially resolve the
> issue.

And the version she kept, at 40 words:

> Checkout fails for about 180 customers every Friday evening. The cause is that the route planner
> and checkout share one database and compete for its connections at peak. I propose separating
> them, and I need a decision by 19 March.

Nothing true was lost, and two things were gained: the size of the problem and the date of the
decision, which the long version never stated. **Cutting made room for the facts the padding had
been standing in for.** That is the usual outcome. Padding is rarely an addition to a complete
paragraph; it is what a writer produces when the precise sentence is not yet clear to them.

## What not to cut

Economy goes wrong when it removes the words a reader needs:

- **The reason.** "Use the replica" is shorter than "use the replica, because the primary is the
  bottleneck at peak", and the shorter version will be undone by the first person who does not
  know why.
- **The context a newcomer lacks.** An acronym defined once costs five words and saves every reader
  who did not know it.
- **Courtesy that is doing a job.** "Thank you for the quick review" is not padding in a message to
  somebody who gave up their afternoon. It is information about how their effort was received.

The test for any word is the same: **if it were deleted, would a reader lose anything?** If not, it
goes. If so, it stays, however long the sentence becomes.

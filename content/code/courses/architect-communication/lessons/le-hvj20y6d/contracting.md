**Before any investigation, agree in writing what question you are answering, who will decide what
to do with the answer, what you will deliver and by when, and what you will not do.** It feels
bureaucratic between colleagues. It is the cheapest protection an internal consultant has,
because every later disagreement turns out to be a disagreement about one of those four things.

## Rewrite the question before accepting it

Henrique's request was "help us pick a message broker". Accepting it as written would commit Lívia
to comparing brokers, and at the end she would hand over a comparison whether or not a broker was
the answer. So the first conversation was about the question itself, using the open questions from
lesson 6:

- *"What happens today when a release breaks route planning?"* Routes for the next morning are
  generated wrong or late, and drivers wait at the depot.
- *"How often?"* Henrique was not sure. "Too often."
- *"How would we know the problem was solved?"* "Releases stop causing incidents."

So the contract's question became: **why do logistics releases break route planning, and what would
stop it?** A broker is one possible answer. Henrique agreed, slightly reluctantly, and added that he
still expected a broker to be the answer. That is fine. The contract does not need him to agree on
the answer, only on the question.

## The contract Lívia sent

> **Question.** Why do logistics releases break route planning, and what would stop it?
>
> **Decides.** Henrique, as tech lead of logistics. I recommend; the team chooses.
>
> **I will deliver** a written diagnosis with options, by Friday 29 May, after two weeks of about
> one day a week.
>
> **I need** access to the deploy log and incident notes for this year, and thirty minutes each with
> four people on the team, chosen by you.
>
> **Not in scope:** implementing anything; evaluating the team's performance; changes outside
> logistics' own systems without the other owners in the conversation.
>
> **We will check in** on Friday 22 May, halfway, so nothing in the final note is a surprise.

Every line is there because of a way this kind of engagement fails:

| line | the failure it prevents |
|---|---|
| question | answering the question that was asked instead of the one that matters |
| decides | the consultant's recommendation being treated as an order, or ignored as an opinion |
| deliver, by when | help that never finishes, and becomes an open-ended favour |
| I need | discovery stalled for lack of data nobody promised |
| not in scope | being drawn into blame ("was it Paulo's fault?") or into building it |
| check in | a final report that surprises the client, which Block names as the classic failure |

## "Evaluating the team's performance" is out on purpose

Henrique did not ask for it, and he did not need to. Whenever a team asks for outside help, some of
its members wonder whether the helper is also reporting on them. **Saying out loud that you are
not** is what makes the interviews honest. Lívia repeated it at the start of each one.

**An internal consultant is somebody asked to help with a problem they do not own, by people who
are free to ignore the advice.** That is Lívia's position most weeks. She does not manage the teams
that call her, she cannot order them to do anything, and the systems she is asked about belong to
somebody else. Everything she achieves, she achieves by being useful enough to be listened to.

The common mistake is to behave as if the position carried authority anyway: to arrive, study the
problem, and hand down the answer. It works for a while, and then teams stop calling, because being
told what to do in your own system by somebody who will not live with the consequences is
unpleasant, however good the answer.

## Peter Block's phases

Peter Block's *Flawless Consulting*, written for exactly this position, describes the work as a
sequence of phases, each with its own conversation and its own way of failing:

%%FIG phases%%

1. **Entry and contracting**: agreeing what the help is for, what each side will do, and what
   success looks like. The next section.
2. **Discovery**: finding out what is actually going on, which is often not what the request said.
   The section after.
3. **Feedback and the decision to act**: presenting what was found to the people who own the
   problem, so that *they* decide. The last section.
4. **Engagement**: doing the work, whoever does it.
5. **Extension or ending**: continuing, or finishing on purpose rather than drifting away.

Block's central claim is that **most consulting failures are decided in the first phase**, in a
contract that was never made explicit, and become visible only in the third, when the client
receives an answer to a question they did not think they had asked.

## It is always a people problem

Gerald Weinberg, who wrote about consulting from decades of doing it, put his second law of
consulting bluntly: *no matter how it looks at first, it's always a people problem*. He did not
mean that technology never fails. He meant that the reason a technical problem persists, and the
reason the obvious fix has not been applied, is almost always about who owns what, who talks to
whom, and who is afraid of what.

Lívia keeps the law in mind as a hypothesis rather than a certainty. In the case this lesson
follows, it turned out to be exactly right.

## The case

In May, Henrique, tech lead of the logistics team, writes to Lívia: "Our releases keep breaking
route planning. We think we need a message broker between the route planner and the zone service.
Can you help us pick one?" It is lesson 6's pattern in a new costume: a solution, chosen in
advance, with a problem underneath. This time Lívia is not asked to build anything. She is asked to
advise, which is harder.

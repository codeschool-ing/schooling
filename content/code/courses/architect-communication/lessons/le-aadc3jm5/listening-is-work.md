**Most people in a technical conversation are not listening; they are waiting for their turn to
propose a solution.** Engineers are trained to solve, and they are good at it, so the first noun
that sounds like a problem ("slow", "microservice", "cache") starts the solving. From that moment
the rest of what the other person says is heard as background to the solution already forming.

The psychologists Carl Rogers and Richard Farson gave the alternative a name in a 1957 paper,
*Active Listening*: listening for the whole of what the speaker means, including what they have not
managed to say, and showing them that you heard it. Their setting was managers and employees, and
the description fits an architect and a product director almost word for word.

## Listening to reply, and listening to understand

The difference is where your attention is while the other person talks.

| | listening to reply | listening to understand |
|---|---|---|
| attention is on | what you will say next | what they are saying and why |
| you hear | the first problem you can solve | the problem they are trying to describe |
| a pause is | your turn | room for them to go on |
| at the end you have | a solution | a question, or a summary to check |

Neither column is a personality. Everybody does both, and the same person switches between them
during one meeting. **The skill is noticing which one you are in**, usually at the moment you
realise you have stopped hearing words because you are composing a reply.

## The engineer's particular trap

Renata, the head of product, stops Lívia in the corridor in April: "We need a recommendations
service, like the big retailers have. Can you scope it?" Lívia's mind starts working at once: a
separate service, a model, where the purchase history lives, how long the data team would need.
Ten seconds later she has an estimate, a quarter of work for two teams, and she has heard nothing
Renata said after the word *recommendations*.

What Renata said next, and Lívia nearly missed, was "the basket has been falling for months and
everybody upstairs is asking me why". **The request was a solution; the sentence after it was the
problem.** The rest of this lesson is about catching that second sentence.

## What active listening is not

- **Not agreeing.** Showing that you understood Renata's problem commits you to nothing about the
  solution, and it makes disagreement later much easier, because she knows you heard her.
- **Not silence.** A listener who says nothing gives the speaker no evidence of being understood.
  The next two sections are about what to say.
- **Not a technique to manage people.** Nodding at intervals and repeating the last three words
  is noticed within a minute and is worse than plain inattention.

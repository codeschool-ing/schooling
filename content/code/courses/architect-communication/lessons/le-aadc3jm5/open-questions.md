**A closed question can be answered with a word and confirms what you already think; an open
question has to be answered with a story and tells you something you did not know.** Early in a
conversation about a need, almost every question should be open. Closed questions are for the end,
when you are checking specific facts.

| closed | open |
|---|---|
| Is the problem the home screen? | What changed for returning customers since January? |
| Do you want it before the review? | What would you need to have by the review? |
| Have you tried promotions? | What has been tried so far, and what happened? |
| Is the recommendations service the priority? | If this worked, what would be different in three months? |

The closed versions are not wrong; each one is a hypothesis. The trouble is asking them first,
because **a closed question imports the asker's guess into the answer**. Ask Renata "is it the home
screen?" and she will think about the home screen, whether or not it is the problem.

## Questions that do most of the work

A handful of open questions recover the real need in most conversations, and Lívia keeps them in
the back of her notebook:

1. **"What happens today when…?"** The current situation, described concretely. Not what should
   happen; what does.
2. **"What would be different if this existed?"** It moves the conversation from the solution to
   its effect, which is usually where the need is.
3. **"How would we know it worked?"** A measure, and often the first time anybody has said one
   aloud. For Renata: the basket for returning customers back to R$ 150.
4. **"What has been tried?"** It saves proposing what failed last year, and it says something about
   the constraints nobody mentioned.
5. **"Who else is affected?"** Stores, support, the data team. The people whose problem this also
   is, and who may describe it differently.

## Why "why" is risky

"Why do you want a recommendations service?" is open, and it often lands as a challenge: it asks
the speaker to justify themselves. **"What would it change?" asks for the same information and is
heard as curiosity.** The word *why* is not banned; it is reserved for the moments when the
relationship can carry it, and for asking about systems rather than people.

## The funnel

A conversation about a need usually runs from wide to narrow: open questions first, to hear the
situation in the speaker's own terms; then focused questions on the parts that matter; then closed
questions to confirm the facts you will write down.

**And after each question, wait.** Most people answer the surface of a question in their first
sentence and the substance in their third. A listener who fills the first pause with the next
question never hears the third sentence. Counting silently to five before speaking feels long and
looks thoughtful.

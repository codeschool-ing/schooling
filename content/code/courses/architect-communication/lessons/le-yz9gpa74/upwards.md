title: Saying no to your own boss
version: 1

**Saying no upwards is not refusing an order. It is making sure the person with the authority
decides with the cost in front of them, and then doing what they decide.** The CEO is entitled to
choose flash deals over the substitution flow, or over a calm Black Friday. He is not entitled to
choose it without knowing that is the choice, and the person who knows is the one who has to say.

## Make the trade-off explicit, then hand it over

The difference between a no to a peer and a no to your manager is the last step. With a peer, you
can decline. With the person who sets your priorities, **you set out the options and their costs, say
which you recommend, and let them choose**:

> "We can do flash deals with reserved stock by the 27th if the substitution flow moves to February
> and we accept a higher risk on Black Friday itself. Or the countdown banner by the 27th and full
> flash deals for Christmas. I recommend the second. It's your call."

"It's your call" is not giving up. It is the honest end of the sentence, and it changes Otávio's
position from somebody being refused to somebody being consulted. He chose the second option, and
added the early-access list from the table above, which was his own idea and better than either.

## Disagree, and commit

Sometimes the boss chooses the option you argued against. Amazon's leadership principles name the
expected response *have backbone; disagree and commit*: argue your case fully while the decision
is open, and once it is made, commit to it as if it had been your own. The phrase is older than
Amazon; Andy Grove used a version of it at Intel.

Committing has three parts:

1. **Make sure the disagreement is on the record**, briefly and without drama: "I'd still prefer the
   banner; noted that we're going with reservation. I'll flag the load-test results by the 20th."
2. **Do the work properly.** A half-hearted delivery that proves you were right is sabotage with a
   good excuse.
3. **Do not relitigate.** If new evidence arrives, bring it. If not, the decision stands.

## When no is not negotiable

Disagree-and-commit covers choices between legitimate options. It does not cover a request to do
something unsafe, illegal or dishonest: shipping a change that skips the security review, hiding an
incident from a client whose contract requires notice, misreporting a number to the board. **Those
are not trade-offs to hand upwards, and committing to them is not loyalty.** Say no plainly, in
writing, and take it to whoever the company names for it, if the person asking will not change
their mind.

**Most decisions in a meeting have been made, or lost, before the meeting starts.** A presentation
that is the first time the decider hears the proposal is a presentation asking them to change their
mind in public, in ten minutes, in front of their peers. Few people do that.

## No surprises

The practice has several names (*pre-wiring*, *walking the halls*) and one rule: **nobody important
to the decision hears the proposal for the first time in the meeting.** In the week before 19
March, Lívia spent twenty minutes with each of the three people who would decide:

- **Caio** saw the numbers and asked for the margin figure as well as the revenue, which went into
  the slide.
- **Renata** raised the substitution flow, which is how Lívia knew to prepare for it, and why the
  objection in the meeting took two minutes instead of twenty.
- **Otávio** asked one question, "does Bruna agree?", which told Lívia to have Bruna in the room.

None of these conversations was a sales pitch. **They were a chance for the decider to object
privately, where changing a view costs nothing**, and for Lívia to change the proposal before it
became a public position.

## Send the document before, not during

The one-pager went out two working days before the meeting, with one line on top: "For Thursday's
leadership meeting; five minutes to read." The presentation then assumes it has been read and does
not repeat it.

Lesson 2 described Amazon's habit of reading in silence at the start of the meeting. It solves the
same problem from the other side: if the document will not be read in advance, read it together,
but **never present a document the audience is reading at the same time**, because they will read
and not listen.

## Rehearse aloud, with a clock

A presentation read silently takes about two thirds of the time it takes to say. Lívia's ten-minute
slot held a version that took fourteen minutes the first time she said it aloud, alone, with a
timer. Two things went: slide 2's second chart and a paragraph of history. **Plan to use half the
slot and leave the rest for questions**, because the questions are where the decision is made, and
meetings start late.

## When the room is a call

Half of Marola's leadership meetings are video calls. What changes:

- **Share one window, not the whole screen.** Notifications, other tabs and a chat message from a
  colleague have ended more than one presentation.
- **Say the slide number when you change slide**, because some people are on a phone and the
  screen lags.
- **Ask for questions by name**, since silence on a call means nothing: "Caio, does the cost work
  for you?" is a question; "any questions?" is a pause.
- **Watch the chat**, or ask somebody to watch it for you. On a call, the objections often arrive
  there first.
- **Send the slides afterwards** with the decision written at the top, as lesson 2 did for the
  document.

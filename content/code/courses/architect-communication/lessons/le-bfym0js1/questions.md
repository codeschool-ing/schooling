**The questions after a presentation are where the decision is actually made, so prepare for them
more than for the slides.** A good presentation makes the decision possible; a well-handled
question makes it safe for the person deciding.

## Prepare the three hardest questions

Before the meeting, Lívia writes down the three questions she least wants to be asked and an answer
to each, no longer than two sentences. For 19 March they were:

1. *"Why didn't this get fixed in February, when you first raised it?"* Because the risk was
   accepted, on the record, pending the April work; the trigger for an earlier review fired on 6
   March, and here we are.
2. *"How do we know six weeks will be six weeks?"* The design has been reviewed by the two teams
   doing the work, and the estimate is a range, five to eight weeks; the first two weeks are the
   part that would reveal a problem early.
3. *"Couldn't we just buy a bigger server and stop talking about it?"* We could, for R$ 108,000 a
   year, and the problem returns as volume grows; it is option 2 on slide 4.

The answers matter less than the preparation. **A question you have already answered once, in
your head, comes out calm**; the same question met cold comes out defensive.

## How to take a question

1. **Let the person finish.** The second half of a question often changes the first.
2. **Restate it in one sentence**, when the room is large, remote, or the question was tangled:
   "So the question is whether six weeks is a reliable estimate." It confirms you understood, and it
   gives you three seconds.
3. **Answer the question that was asked, briefly**, then stop. A long answer to a short question
   reads as nervousness. If they want more, they ask.
4. **Check**: "Does that answer it?" Only when you are not sure it did.

## "I don't know" is an answer

**"I don't know; I will find out and tell you by Thursday" is a better answer than a guess**, and
experienced decision-makers know it. A guess that turns out wrong discredits everything else the
presenter said; a promise kept on Thursday adds to the credibility of the whole proposal. Write the
question down where the asker can see you writing it, and keep the promise.

## Objections are information

Some questions are objections: "This seems expensive for a problem that only happens on Fridays."
The reflex is to defend. The better move is to **find the concern underneath**, which is usually one
of three things: the cost, the risk of the change itself, or something the objector cares about
that the proposal touches.

Renata's objection on 19 March was "April is when we promised the substitution flow". Under it was
not the replica at all; it was a promise she had made to the stores and would now have to move.
Lívia did not argue the replica's merits again. She asked what the stores had been told, offered to
join the call where Renata would tell them, and the objection was resolved in the meeting.

## What not to do with a question

- **Do not argue with the person deciding.** If the decider disagrees after hearing the answer, say
  what you would need to change their mind, offer to come back with it, and stop.
- **Do not answer a different question** because you prepared for that one. It is noticed.
- **Do not let one person's detailed question take the meeting.** "That's a good one for the design
  review; can I send you the detail this afternoon?" keeps the meeting about the decision.

**A presentation that asks for a decision tells one story: how things were, what changed, and what
should be done about it.** The audience follows a story without effort, and they forget a list
before the next slide. Most technical presentations are lists, because the presenter organised them
the way the work was organised.

On 19 March Lívia has ten minutes in the leadership meeting to get the replica work approved.
Otávio, Caio and Renata will be in the room; they have read the one-pager, or said they have. The
ten minutes are not for repeating it. They are for making the decision feel obvious and safe.

## Situation, complication, resolution

Barbara Minto, whose pyramid appeared in lesson 1, also described how to open a document or a
presentation so the audience wants the answer: **situation, complication, resolution.**

- **Situation**: what the audience already knows and agrees with. "Friday evening is our busiest
  three hours: about 9,000 checkouts." Nobody argues, and everybody is now looking at the same
  thing.
- **Complication**: what changed, or what threatens the situation. "Since October, a growing share
  of those checkouts fail, and on 6 March checkout stopped completely for 32 minutes." This is the
  tension. Without it, there is no reason to listen to the rest.
- **Resolution**: what should be done, which is the decision being asked for. "Six engineer-weeks
  in April and R$ 4,000 a month remove both problems. I am asking for approval today."

Minto's version adds the **question** the complication raises in the audience's head ("so what do
we do?") between the second and the third, which is a useful check: if the complication does not
make the audience ask that question, it is not a complication, it is more situation.

## The decision first, still

Lesson 1 said to put the answer first, and situation-complication-resolution seems to contradict
it. It does not, because **the resolution is announced in the first thirty seconds and then
earned.** Lívia opens with one sentence, "I am going to ask you to approve six engineer-weeks and
R$ 4,000 a month to fix Friday checkout; here is why", and then tells the story. The audience knows
where it is going and listens to the reasons as reasons, rather than wondering what they are for.

## What the story leaves out

A ten-minute story has room for one complication and one resolution. Lívia's investigation found
eleven things worth knowing about the orders database, and nine of them are not in the
presentation. They are true and interesting, and none of them changes the decision. **Every
fact that does not move the audience towards the resolution makes it harder to reach**, and the
appendix of the one-pager is where they wait for anybody who asks.

## Three lengths of the same story

Prepare the presentation at three lengths, because the slot will change:

| length | when | Lívia's version |
|---|---|---|
| one sentence | the corridor, the lift, the meeting that overran | "Six engineer-weeks stop Friday checkout failing; I need a yes today." |
| two minutes | the meeting that overran badly | the situation, the complication with the 6 March outage, the cost, the ask |
| ten minutes | the slot as planned | the full story, the options, and time for questions |

The ten-minute version is not the two-minute one stretched. **It is the two-minute one with evidence
added where the audience is most likely to doubt**, which is a reason to know in advance where that
is.

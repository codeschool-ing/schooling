**Article 7, IX** allows processing for the *legitimate interests of the controller or of a third
party, except where the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject that require protection
of personal data prevail.* It is the most flexible basis, and the law fences it in **article 10**:

- the purpose must be **legitimate and considered from concrete situations** — supporting and
  promoting the controller's activities, or protecting the person's own rights, among others;
- only the data **strictly necessary** for that purpose may be used (§1);
- the controller must ensure **transparency** about it (§2);
- and the ANPD may ask for an impact assessment when this is the basis (§3).

## The balancing test

Using legitimate interest well means writing down, before the processing starts, a short assessment
that is often called a **LIA** (*legitimate interest assessment*). Its four questions follow the
article:

1. **Is the purpose legitimate?** Concrete, lawful, and something the controller actually needs.
2. **Is the processing necessary?** Could the purpose be met with less data, or none?
3. **Do the person's rights prevail?** Would they reasonably expect this? Could it harm them? Are they
   a child or adolescent? Is the data sensitive — in which case the basis is not available at all
   (section 5)?
4. **What safeguards reduce the impact?** Transparency, an easy way to object, pseudonymisation,
   short retention.

Two of Ipê's purposes, through the test:

| | detecting fraudulent orders | e-mailing existing customers about similar products |
|---|---|---|
| legitimate? | yes: losses, and harm to the cardholder | yes: promoting its activity |
| necessary? | order, payment and device data, yes; prescriptions, no | the e-mail and the purchase category |
| expectations | a customer expects a shop to check for fraud | a recent customer may expect it; a customer from 2019 may not |
| prevails? | rarely | **yes, if the category is health** — section 5 rules it out |
| safeguards | access limited to the fraud team; short retention | an opt-out in every message; consent instead for health products |

The second column is where legitimate interest usually goes wrong at a pharmacy. "Customers who bought
X might like Y" is ordinary marketing in a bookshop and processing of health data in a pharmacy, and
no balancing test turns it into legitimate interest. **The test is worth writing for exactly that
reason: it is where the answer "no" gets found before a regulator finds it.**

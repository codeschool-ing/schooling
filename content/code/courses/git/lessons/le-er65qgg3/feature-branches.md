**A workflow is a team's agreement about how branches are used.** Git has no opinion. It will let a
team commit everything to `main`, or keep forty branches alive for a year, and it will merge whatever
it is asked to. The three shapes in this lesson are the agreements most teams end up with, and the
first is the one lessons 5 to 8 have been describing all along.

## The shape

- `main` always holds work that is finished and reviewed.
- **Every change starts a branch from `main`**: a fix, a feature, a page. It is named for what it is,
  often with the ticket's number, and lesson 12 shows that convention.
- The branch is pushed, a pull request is opened, reviewed and merged, with one of lesson 8's three
  buttons. Then the branch is deleted.

Nothing about it needs a tool beyond what you already know. It is popular because it makes review
the default: nothing reaches `main` without somebody else reading it.

## What goes wrong: branches that live too long

The weak point is time. A branch that lives for two days is merged almost without incident. **A branch
that lives for three weeks becomes a second version of the project.** Meanwhile `main` has moved on
under twenty other merges. The longer the two drift, the more lines both of them touch, and the merge at
the end becomes lesson 6's conflict many times over, all at once, when everybody is tired of the work. The review suffers the same way: a pull request of two thousand lines gets approved, not read.

Two habits keep feature branches healthy, and both are about size rather than about Git:

- **Merge `main` into the branch, or rebase it onto `main`, every day or so**, so the drift stays small
  and conflicts arrive one at a time.
- **Split the work so each branch is small.** *Sunday hours* is one branch; *redesign the whole site*
  is ten. Lesson 16 is about splitting a ticket, which is the same skill.

A team that keeps its branches to a day or two has already moved halfway towards the next shape.
